Thank God for hockey.

This was Shane’s refrain over the next few weeks as he bottled up every thought, every other emotion *apart* from hockey; he bottled it up tight and sealed it and locked it away. Hockey was majestically all consuming. If he wasn’t playing hockey, he was training for hockey, reading about hockey, watching hockey, analyzing hockey. With very little effort at all, Shane could fill every single second of the day with hockey.

And he did.

“You’ve lost a little weight,” Ali told him, frowning at the scales. “A little more than I would like this close to playoffs. You doing anything different?”

“I’m training a little harder,” Shane said, attempting to sound nonchalant.

“Oh? Well, we should adjust your meal plan.” Ali smiled. “Maybe a second Snickers, too.”

Shane accepted the new meal plan, with the additional protein and carbs. Eating was also about hockey, so that was okay. But the Snickers bars were currently taking up an increasing amount of space in his freezer, waiting for a treat day. He didn’t feel much like treating himself at the moment.

He flew to New York in March to have a fitting for his new game day suit, an entirely bespoke suit that was also entirely out of Shane’s comfort zone. Instead of the ubiquitous black, it was a deep burgundy, tightly fitted. An open-collar white shirt and black leather shoes completed the outfit and as Shane looked in the mirror a brief flash of not-hockey crept in, imagining Rozanov’s reaction if he saw Shane in something like this.

Then he crushed it. It hurt to do so.

“It’s great,” he told Sargent, summoning shards of enthusiasm from the very depths of his soul.

He’d had a couple of sessions with the stylist Sargent had recommended, Hana, as a way of adding more Things in his life that could fill the Rozanov-shaped gaps. She was a Korean-American woman of indeterminate age who had kind of a reassuring, stern vibe. She hadn’t required that he visit her in Boston – which Shane had been relieved about, for obvious reasons – and they’d instead had a couple of virtual calls where Shane told her about himself in clothes terms, his likes and dislikes, which he had found very hard, and what he was looking for as a general vibe. And then she’d set him about a hundred things to try on.

From this they’d whittled down a menu of options and now he got a package a month from her with little print outs of what to wear with what. Occasionally she asked him to send photos and she’d gently correct him.

His game day suits – there was going to be more than one – were a little more elaborate and had involved collaboration between all three of them, with Sargent sharing designs and Hana making suggestions and Shane just agreeing when necessary.

Sargent fussed with Shane’s collar. “You’re looking a little pale. Are you getting enough sleep?”

Shane didn’t know for definite that Sargent was gay. He knew enough now to know that eyeliner and false eyelashes and – today’s addition – dark red nail polish, didn’t actually confirm a guy’s sexuality. It could just be part of his style. Sort of emo, at a guess. Shane also didn’t know much about style. But he suspected Sargent *was* gay and it was interesting because Shane wasn’t in the slightest bit attracted to him. After Petrov he’d kind of wondered if that was how his sexuality worked. “Not really,” he admitted, letting Sargent fuss next with his sleeve.

“Worrying about the big hockey thing?”

Shane’s mouth twitched. *The big hockey thing.* Sure. “Not really. I don’t worry like that.”

“You don’t?”

“No. I just get… really intense. I mean, I guess that could be how my worry manifests.”

“Probably. But you’re not sleeping well.”

“No.” The issue was he couldn’t make sleep about hockey. Oh, he had tried. He had tried to convince himself that a solid eight hours was important to rejuvenate his body so he had optimum rest. Rest was important for recuperation. For energy. For all the things that made him good on the ice. But. “Can’t seem to switch off.”

“Sounds like nerves to me.”

“It’s not.” Shane grunted and wondered if saying it out loud would help. Sargent didn’t know him. Didn’t know his friends, his team. He knew Petrov but Petrov was out of the picture now, as well, and he didn’t think that Sargent would be as unprofessional as to gossip about him. “I’m kind of— I kind of broke up with someone. Or they broke up with me.”

“Oh.” Sargent stood up straight, immediately sympathetic. “I’m sorry. That’s totally shit, Shane.”

“Yeah. It is.” His voice broke a little. He cleared it, not certain if this had made him feel any better at all. “So, Hana says no socks, hey?” Shane wiggled his foot, where his bare ankle flashed.

“That’s right,” Sargent said gently. “No socks.”

Shane carried several extremely heavy patio slabs from the driveway to the back yard which if he was honest was slightly more of a workout than he’d been expecting. He should probably have stretched beforehand. And changed.

“Why didn’t you get the delivery guy to do this?” he asked, wiping sweat from his brow.

Yuna Hollander scowled. It was a particular expression of dissatisfaction that his dad always said – usually laughing – that Shane had inherited. “He arrived when we were out, a full three hours before the estimated delivery time. I have written a strongly worded complaint,” his mom explained, handing him an icy glass of water.

Knowing his mom, she would get a hefty discount for this inconvenience.

“Stay for dinner?” his dad asked him. “Nothing fancy. Garlic chicken and salad.”

“Sounds good.”

These days, his dad cooked a lot. It hadn’t been the case when he was younger. Before she’d quit her job to become his full-time manager, it had been his mom putting meals on the table every night after she got home from work. At some point in his mid-teens, they’d had a blow-out fight about this arrangement – Shane listening in terror up in his bedroom, afraid his parents were on the verge of divorce – but the result was the redrawing of the domestic spheres.

Admittedly, his dad’s meals tended to be very simple – spaghetti and steak and grilled chicken – but Shane wasn’t very picky about food. Or he hadn’t been until food became fuel and then it had to be a very specific kind of fuel and then he found himself becoming pretty damn picky.

As his freezer full of Snickers bars would now attest to.

So Shane ate his chicken and salad and didn’t look at the crispy little potatoes on his parents’ plates and after they had fruit salad with Greek yogurt and a drizzle of honey and then they sat on the back porch with tea, bundled up in fleeces.

His dad paced the yard, explaining what they were having the landscaper do. “This bit here,” he said, marking out a few square feet with big stretches of his legs, “is a real sun trap first thing in the morning. We thought a little patio set right here. Show him, honey. And I’m wondering some kind of water feature?”

Yuna duly showed Shane the patio set and he made the right noises. Four chairs, he noted. It was always four chairs. For his mom and dad and Shane and the partner Shane might one day have.

“Hey,” he said, haunted by this image, “so I should probably tell you guys something.”

His mom smiled at him, totally no idea what was about to hit her.

“I’m gay.”

Because he was looking at his mom, he didn’t know what his dad did, but his mom kind of jolted in her chair, a tiny movement that sloshed the tea in her favorite bone china cup. “Oh, Shane.”

“Shane,” his dad said, moving quietly up the steps of their back deck. Shane glanced at him then. His face was soft – kind of sad, maybe? “Son.”

“I’m… I’m sorry?” Shane managed because he had been utterly calm, just before, when he’d decided on this spur-of-the-moment announcement, but now his heart was racing. He couldn’t tell what they were thinking. He couldn’t tell.

“Sweetheart, don’t be sorry.” His mom got up, the blanket falling from her lap. She came over to the little loveseat he occupied, squeezing herself in next to him. Her arm went around his shoulders. “Thank you, thank you for telling us.”

“Okay,” he squeaked, wide-eyed.

His dad rested his big hand on Shane’s head and smiled down at him. “I think you could say we’re not totally surprised, Shane.”

“What? Really?”

His mom hugged him against her. “We do know you pretty well, Shane.”

“Oh.”

Shane hadn’t imagined that.

“You were never very interested in girls,” David said in explanation, a little wry. Just… totally fine with this.

And then his mom, *his mom*, laughed, and said, “You were, however, pretty passionate about certain hockey players.”

“Jesus,” Shane said, wishing the earth would open him up and swallow him whole. So, they had noticed his preoccupation with Scott Hunter too then. Awesome. “You could have maybe told *me*.”

On Fridays, Wiebe had introduced an excruciating new feature to their team bonding called ‘The Retro’. Shane more than suspected the existence of this exercise was wholly his fault, as he had been the one who had gone to Wiebe to request ways to get the team to gel better, so he had only himself to blame.

The Retro involved the team getting into one of the conference rooms, where facilities had arranged chairs in a circle and put up inspirational posters that said inspirational things like *‘Alone we can do so little; together we can do so much’* over clearly stock bought images, and they all sat around and Wiebe brought up topics that had been friction points during the week to discuss.

And then they bitched about each other.

Some of it was the kind of shit that should never be discussed again and *genuinely* made Shane question his life choices, such as today’s play-by-play of an ‘argument’ between Mitty and O’Conner about Mitty’s choice of bodywash.

The team was playing good hockey. Excellent hockey. People kept telling him that and Shane kept nodding. He didn’t feel it, himself, or he did but in a once-removed kind of way. Like he was looking at himself through a pane of glass, glass smeared with Vaseline. A blurry Shane played hockey, ate six meals a day, slept maybe four hours a night and got up and did it again. Ali upped his calorie intake twice more. Sargent sniffed disapprovingly at Shane’s waistline. All blurry.

He knew in some remote sense that he wasn’t doing okay, and that the reason for his not doing okay was shaped like a hockey player and had a Russian accent and probably wasn’t thinking of him at all – but he pushed that aside and told himself that it would get better. That’s what they always said about heartbreak, which was what this surely was.

Rozanov had broken Shane’s heart.

Broken it. Because Shane had been in love with him and hadn’t known it.

No.

That was a Lie. Capital ‘L’. He could call it out now. He’d had plenty of time to think about this, to do his own mental tape review and come to terms with his own fallacy.

Shane had been *in love* and he had pretended he wasn’t. Because he’d known, he’d *known*, that Rozanov wasn’t with him for the same reasons. That he liked Shane, sure, and he liked what they did together. But Shane had been one of many. Maybe he got a thrill from the danger – Shane sure as hell had – but that was it.

Meanwhile Shane, inexperienced, *stupid* Shane, had let himself fall in love with someone utterly unobtainable, someone who would never – and could never – love him back. Had told him plainly that it wasn’t possible. The life Rozanov wanted did not include Shane.

And so here he was. Broken hearted.

It would get better. Time healed all wounds. That’s what they said in all the songs and stuff. It was really inconceivable to Shane that people willingly fell in love, if this was what it felt like when it ended. He didn’t plan to do it again.

Hayden, sitting on the bench, scrolling through his phone, whooped obnoxiously, “Looks like Rozanov’s been locked down.”
