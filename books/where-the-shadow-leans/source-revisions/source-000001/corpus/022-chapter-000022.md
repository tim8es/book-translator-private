## Chapter 21: Chapter Nineteen - The Coronation

*Then shall the priest lift up the sun above the head of him that kneels, and ask of the blood of the house, beginning at the youngest, *Will you stand under this sun?* And each shall answer for himself, aloud. And until the last of the blood has answered, the sun shall be held, and not set down.*

— From *The Order of the Crowning*, as it is kept in the temple of the Citadel

\* \* \*

The thread had gone short in the night.

For five nights it had run out of him from one end of the Marches to the other, over the strait and the black ridges of the Coast, and ached along all its length as a kite string aches in a boy’s fist when the kite has gone too high to see. Last night, somewhere past two hundred, lying behind the lead in the black coat with his palm over the crescent, he had felt the ache stop. The other heart went on keeping his count, slow and even and in no hurry for anybody, but it was no longer a country away. It was somewhere under the city. He had sat up in the dark and looked at the shutters, as if he might hear it through the lead, and the thread had lain slack in his chest as a well rope lies slack when the bucket has come up to the lip and somebody’s hand is on it.

He had not slept after that. He had lain with his two fingers on his wrist and felt it come nearer.

At the day’s first bell he got up and threw back the shutters, and the noon came in white, as it had every morning he could remember. It was the eve of the Long Eclipse.

Below the window the upper town lay shuttered against it, terrace under terrace, every house barred and sleeping with its back to the day. Lower down, where the spires had never reached, the smoke of the refineries went up out of the Underglow into the white in slow grey ropes, and in its doorways there would be people already with a paper of the Treasury’s cut held under their noses, breathing the eve away, with their eyes open on a light they could not see.

The coronation silk lay on its chair in its tissue, stiff with so much gold thread that it had kept its shape unworn, like a man asleep sitting up. He did not unfold it. He had a coat.

The salver came at the second bell, and this morning there was no speech on it, only a note folded once.

*You will answer for the blood. When the priest asks, speak. A.*

He put it into his right-hand pocket, with the speeches and the dented tin of violets and the flask of sunlight that was warm all through, so that the pocket stood out from the black silk like the cheek of a child who has been given a sweet and told to keep it for later. He turned to the glass and settled his collar. The coat had made up its mind about him some days ago. It took his warmth as it was cut to do, and let the gold through only at its seams, in fine lines, like the leading in a window, and over his heart the closed crescent of House Conti lay in black thread too thick for any light to pass.

Beneath the crescent, under a row of Vesna’s close even stitches, the ring rode against his ribs, colder than anything else he had on. He laid his hand flat over it until his own heart had steadied, and felt the other steady with it, nearer than it had been in five days.

Paul was standing in the corridor when he came out.

The ash had come out of the creases of his knuckles at last, or somebody had scrubbed it out for him. He looked at Caleb as he had looked at him on every morning of the last five, as a man looks at a door he has been told not to knock on, and said nothing.

Then he did.

“Highness.” His voice scraped. “Will you say it? What he’s written?”

Caleb looked at him. Paul had not asked anybody anything since the lilies.

“I shall say something,” he answered. “It will be true. I find I have got out of the way of saying anything else.”

He went on down the corridor, and after a moment Paul’s boots came after him, two paces back, all the way down through the palace to the Throne Hall.

\* \* \*

The Throne Hall had been built to make kneeling feel like a privilege, and on the eve of the Long Eclipse it had been dressed to make it feel like the only thing worth doing.

The noon came down from the high glass in columns, rank on rank down either side from the west doors to the dais, so bright and so square-cut that they might have been quarried and set up on the marble by masons, and the dust going up through them turned gold and slow, as gold leaf turns when a gilder breathes on it. Under them the court stood on its own reflection like a congregation on still water. There were white lilies at the foot of every column, armfuls of them, and their smell lay over the beeswax and the warm stone, thick and sweet and grave, as lilies smell in a house where somebody has been laid out in the front room.

Two hundred of the court and more stood between the columns in silks the colours of sugared almonds, and every face turned as he came in at the side door below the dais, and every face found the black.

He heard it go down the hall as wind goes over a field of flax, a long silk sigh from the front ranks to the back, and then a hush with no smile in it at all. He had once walked the Coast’s black hall with all Vesperia watching, and he did not hurry for this one. He went up the three steps of the dais to his place, and stood in it.

The blood of the Solar House stood on the steps of the dais at a crowning, the eldest highest and the youngest last, so that the heir kneeling below might see every one of them without turning his head. His place was the lowest step. Above him stood Cecily, in the Grand Alchemist’s grey with the alembic worked in silver at her breast, and she had put up her hair. She had put it up in a smooth high coil, pinned with more pins than Caleb would have thought one head could carry, and already, at the back, one long brown lock had begun quietly to make its escape. She looked down at him, and at the black, and her chin went up, and she did not say anything, which was how he knew she had been crying in the night. Above her stood Eleanor in the Chancellery’s dark blue, with the Treasury’s chain of office lying flat across her breast like a row of coins laid out on a counter, and her despatch case at her feet. Every lamp on the dais had brightened a little when she took her place, and she had not looked up at them.

Above Eleanor, on the highest step, there was nobody.

It was the High Paladin’s place. A space had been left on the marble the width of a soldier, and the light lay in it.

Behind the steps rose the dais, and on the dais, under the great golden sunburst on the wall, stood the throne of the Solar House. It was empty. The King’s mantle had been laid over it, white and gold, with its long train arranged down the steps of the throne in folds as careful as the folds of a flag on a coffin, because the King was still King until the sun was set on another head, and the King kept his study with the shutters closed. Beside it, in the Queen’s chair, his mother sat in white. Her hair was up. Her hands were folded in her lap, and the rings on them did not knock together, and she was looking at him.

He looked back, and held it, and then turned his eyes down the hall.

At the foot of the dais, on the white marble, in the full fall of the largest column of all, his brother was kneeling.

Andrew wore white silk stiff with gold, the twin of the coat that lay folded on a chair in Caleb’s rooms. He knelt on one knee with his palm to his breast and his head bent, as he had knelt at twelve on that same floor, home from the Vales with his words by heart, and the noon lay on his bowed head and his shoulders and laid his reflection out beneath him on the marble in white and gold. He had taken the Treasury’s own that morning. Caleb could see it. It lay on his skin in a fine gilt glaze from the throat to the brow, so that he looked young and grave and lit, as if the light of the column were coming up out of him and not down onto him. Only at his temple, where the hair began, something grey waited under the gold for its hour.

Over him stood the high priest of the Citadel temple, who had crowned their father, and who had once had a wooden sword drawn on him at Caleb’s naming day, and who had never since, so the palace said, held a child of the Solar House without a glance round for wherever the High Paladin might be standing. He looked round now, at the empty step, and seemed relieved, and lifted the sun.

It was a circlet of old gold with rays standing up from it all round, and every ray had been set along its length with slivers of the deep trench, so that when the priest raised it into the column the noon went into every sliver at once and came out of them doubled, and the thing blazed over Andrew’s head like a second sun hung on the first. The court let out its breath. The priest held it there with both hands, and Caleb saw his arms take the weight, and settle to it, because the Order said that it was to be held until the last of the blood had answered, and the Order did not say how long that might take.

At the foot of the dais, a little to one side, so that the court might see her write, there was a gilt chair no bigger than a child’s, with a cushion of white velvet on it, and in it sat Oriane de Chardon.

She wore her coat of feathers, which in that blaze had gone every green and violet of a starling’s back, and her copper coin lay at her throat on a green ribbon, and her green leather book was open on her knee with the silver pen laid in its gutter. She was the only person in the hall who was not looking at the sun. She was looking at him.

She lifted the pen a hair’s breadth, as a duellist lifts a blade in salute.

Behind the *Lantern*’s chair the envoys of the Dominion sat in a row in the order the Holds had measured for them, the ambassador of Lys in blue feathers, three gnomes of Lowmere in starched caps like a fleet of barges at anchor, a dwarf of the Holds with his beard in a single white rope and a ledger on his knee, and the Thalassan envoy, whose nephew Caleb had once known rather better than was wise. Along the walls the Radiant Guard kept the hall in white with their halberds grounded. And against the wall behind the dais steps, where a prince’s sworn man stands, Paul had taken his place.

The priest drew breath.

“Caleb of the Solar House,” he called, in a voice that climbed into the vaulting and came down again altered, as every voice did in that hall. “Youngest of the blood. Will you stand under this sun?”

The whole hall turned to the lowest step.

Andrew lifted his head. He did not rise. He turned on his knee, a little, and looked up at his brother past the blaze of the sun over his own head, and the white rose in his eyes and stood there, and it was joy, as it had been on the landing, and there was something under the joy now that the Dust had glazed over and could not quite hide, a stretched brightness, like the skin of a drum.

He nodded once. *Speak.*

And under Caleb’s ribs, under the ring, the thread pulled.

It pulled as it had at the anvil in Dornhöhle, tight and sudden from the breastbone outward, and it did not pull south. It pulled towards the west doors. He kept his eyes where they were. The other heart was going in time with his own, beat for beat, and it was close enough now that he could feel the cold of it at the end of the thread, as one feels the cold of a cellar through a floor, somewhere beyond the white doors at the far end of the hall, in the walls.

He reached past the violets in his pocket and drew out his brother’s speech.

\* \* \*

He unfolded it. Five mornings of folding had worn its creases soft, and it lay open on his palm like a missal that falls open at the same page.

“I, Caleb of the Solar House,” he read aloud, “was taken from my father’s house under the Accord, and carried into the dark.”

His voice went up into the vaulting, clear and unhurried, the voice of a hundred embassy dinners. At the foot of the dais the copper coin lay quiet on its green ribbon at Oriane’s throat.

“Where I was held,” Caleb read, “against my will. And fed upon, by force.”

The hall drew in its breath all together, as a hall does when a thing it has whispered about for a week is said aloud in it at last, and a lady in the front rank put her hand to her mouth. In the column of light, under the blazing sun, Andrew closed his eyes, as a man closes them who has carried something a long way and felt it at last taken out of his arms.

The coin was quiet. All of it was true, and the *Lantern* would print it.

Caleb read the fourth line.

“And kept by such craft as is not natural to our people.”

The coin began to sing.

It began thin and high, a single bright note like a gnat at a nursery window on a summer night, and it did not stop. It rose. It went up out of the green ribbon into the white light as clear as a struck glass, and the copper turned on its ribbon by itself and caught the noon and threw it in a bright spot that went wandering up the white marble and across the lilies and onto the hem of the King’s mantle on the empty throne, and every face in the front ranks turned from the lowest step to the gilt chair. Oriane did not touch the coin. She sat with her pen lifted and let it sing, and her feathers went slowly from the colours of a starling’s back to a sober and considering bronze.

“I renounce,” Caleb read, over it, “the Sovereign of the Obsidian Coast.”

The coin went up half a note and held there, like a choirboy who has been told to hold the last note and never told when to stop.

Andrew’s eyes had opened. The white had gone out of them. He was looking at the gilt chair, at the bright disc of copper turning and singing in the light, and his face under the glaze of the Dust had gone quite still.

Caleb folded the speech along its own folds.

“That is my brother’s speech. Madame de Chardon’s coin has been so kind as to correct it for me, and I do not think I shall go on with it.” He put it back in his pocket with the violets. “I was asked to tell the courts of the world what was done to me. I will tell all of it. And since the *Lantern* has a seat, I should be obliged if Madame de Chardon would write it down, every word, in the order in which it is said.”

The coin had stopped the moment he stopped reading, as a cricket stops when a lamp is carried past the hearth.

“Every word, Highness,” Oriane promised. “And not one of them in the wrong order. I have waited a great many years to be asked.”

Above the kneeling heir the priest held up the sun. It blazed on, and its light lay over Andrew’s bowed head and white shoulders like water poured from a ewer that never empties.

“In the spring,” Caleb began, “a vineyard of the Coast burned in the Eclipse Marches, and forty-one people burned in it, among the vines and in the cellars. One of them was Livia Conti, who kept it. She died on her own cellar stair, before a door of black iron, with my signet closed in her hand. The Coast returned the signet to my father, and the next night I was given a cup of wine and woke on a yacht with ash in my mouth.” He let it lie. “I was never at the vineyard. My brother had borrowed the signet of me some weeks before. It fitted no finger he had, so he wore it on a chain under his collar, and the wardens at the Chalmont bridge wrote him down as Prince Caleb, who goes everywhere and is suspected of nothing. He went down into the cellars of that vineyard with it, and came up again without it.”

In the nearest column of light the gold dust had stopped turning, and hung, as if the light itself had leaned in to listen.

Andrew had not moved from his knee.

“Caleb,” he said. Only that, gently, as a man says the name of a child talking in his sleep.

The coin kept its peace. His name was perfectly true.

\* \* \*

“Madame de Chardon.”

Oriane’s pen lifted.

“Some weeks ago, in the great hall of Castel Nerezza, I asked you a great many questions and kept one back. I said it was better kept. I should like to spend it now. You told the Nine that a Solar yacht flying no colours had set down at the Kessel Pass by night, a little before the new moon. Whose yacht was it?”

Her feathers stayed bronze.

“The Crown Prince’s, Highness.”

“How do you know?”

“I was in the mule stable. I am often in mule stables. Nobody ever looks in them.” A murmur went along the envoys and died. “I saw His Highness come down off the yacht in his grey with his hood up and nobody with him, and go in at the iron door. Don Salvatore Caruso had gone in an hour before him, with his granddaughter. They were two hours inside. His Highness came out last, alone, and stood on the landing shelf in the snow with his gloves in his hand, and did not put them on.” She looked at Andrew’s bowed head. “I was paid by the Ashward to bring the Coast a report of the Radiant Guard. Nobody paid me to mention the yacht. I put it in for nothing.”

The coin lay still. Along the envoys’ row the dwarf of the Holds had opened his ledger on his knee and begun to write in it, slowly, in a square hand.

“Eleanor,” said Caleb.

Eleanor bent and opened the despatch case at her feet, and the lamps of the dais brightened over her head as she straightened. She laid her papers along her white-gloved forearm in order, as a dealer lays out a hand, and spoke to the hall in the Chancellery voice, cold and dry and clear, the voice for the returns of a quarter that has gone badly wrong.

“There is an account at the Seventh Chamber of Dornhöhle that has no name,” she said, and read out its number, and the dwarf of the Holds stopped writing and looked at his own page a while with his pen held over it. “It is fed every week from the dust of the Underglow, by way of Lowmere and Lutèce. Out of it Van Houten and Daughters was paid to carry twelve crates of refinery machinery to the Kessel Pass, which weighed rather more than machinery does.” One of the gnomes of Lowmere sat up as straight as a mast under her starched cap and studied the ceiling. “Four nights earlier three hundred crowns went out of the Crown Prince’s household purse, for a tour of the Guard’s positions that the High Paladin says he never made. He was, it seems, in a mule stable.” The ink on her second finger came through the glove as she turned the sheet. “And last winter, when the Coast’s ships came in at half their weight three months together and the grid went short, the Treasury’s reserve issued eight deep-trench stones to the Grand Alchemist’s works.” She held the sheet up so that the hall could see the hand at its foot. “*By warrant of the Crown Prince.*”

She looked at the hall, not at Andrew.

“I keep the books. Everything in them is somebody’s handwriting.”

“And the night before,” Caleb prompted.

Her eyes came down to him.

He saw her know what he was asking, and what it would cost, and whom, all at once, as she had always known a sum before anybody else at the table had finished writing it down. He saw her look past him up the dais to the white figure in the Queen’s chair. Then she drew out the last sheet, a slip cut from a longer page, and he knew it, because he had read it once across a desk on the Coast while the Sovereign’s frost crept over his father’s sun.

“On the night the signet came home to this hall on a black cushion, the household purse bought the Chalmont bridge’s register of crossings for eleven crowns, and had the wardens strike through their own copy. At the next bell the Throne told the Coast that it required no inquiry. The night after, my brother was given a cup of wine.” She lowered the slip. “It is sealed with the sun, and with the lily.”

The whole of the Throne Hall looked up the dais.

The Queen sat upright, as a queen sits through a long ceremony in shoes that pinch, with her hands folded and the rings on them still. She looked at Caleb on the lowest step in the black of the Coast, and he looked back at her and did not help her with it, as he had not helped her in the dark of his rooms.

“It is my seal,” said the Queen. The hall had been built to carry any voice that spoke from that chair, and it carried hers to the west doors. “I read it twice.”

Beside her the King’s mantle lay over the empty throne in white and gold, in the full light, with nobody in it.

At the gilt chair the coin lay silent. Oriane’s pen went across the green page, and waited.

“Cecily,” said Caleb.

She was already moving. She came down the one step between them and stood beside him on the lowest, which was not her place, and the lock of hair that had been escaping all morning chose that moment to come down altogether across her cheek, and she let it. She folded her burned forearms across herself and held her own elbows, as she had held them at the bench in the spring, and spoke to the hall in a voice much too high that did not break.

“I made the blades.”

It went down the Throne Hall like a dropped glass.

“The Crown Prince came to my laboratory and told me my brother was held against his will, and asked me for twelve blades of refined Aether within the month. He said they would bring Caleb home. He told me not to tell my sisters, and I did not. I cut the runes on the hilts with my own hand, and anybody who has seen the High Paladin’s sword can read whose hand it was. They went to the Ashward. The Ashward drew them on my brother’s road, and a boy of sixteen broke his leg on a carriage wheel, and the fifth of them went into a man’s side and broke there.” She did not look at Andrew. “I set the fifth twice. It sang.”

On the Coast every candle in the hall would have bent over in its cup. Here the columns of light only stood where they stood, white, and burned.

“There is a warrant drawn,” Cecily went on, “to be sealed tonight when he is King, for forty more by the morning the sun goes black. There are twenty finished on my bench under a cloth.” She unfolded her arms and laid her hands flat at her sides, as a child lays them who has been told to stand up straight and say her piece. “I will not make the rest. And nobody will carry the twenty out of my door while I am standing in it.”

The coin was silent. Under the sun, the priest’s arms had begun faintly to shake.

\* \* \*

Andrew rose.

The Order had not provided for an heir who got up, and so the priest went on holding the sun where it had been, blazing, over a patch of white marble with nobody under it.

Andrew did not look up at it. He brushed the white dust of the marble from his knee with two fingers and turned to face the hall in his white and gold, and spoke in the voice of the dais, which he had been building since he was twelve.

“My brother speaks beautifully. He always has. The fae who brought him home say that the enthralled speak so. They all speak beautifully at the last, for whoever holds them, and weep to go back.”

The coin at Oriane’s throat lay still. He believed every word of it.

“Everything my sister the Arch-Chancellor has read to you is true,” Andrew admitted. “I drew eight stones from the reserve. I paid for twelve crates. I went to the Kessel Pass and sat down at a table with a Caruso, in the iron, where nobody could burn anybody, and bought my brother’s life from the only house on that Coast that would sell it to me. I would buy it again tomorrow.” His hands had come together behind his back. “And I went to the vineyard.”

Nobody in the hall moved, and under the weight of the sun the priest could be heard breathing.

Andrew lifted his hand, without looking, to the blaze over the empty marble behind him.

“Every ray of that is set with stones of the deep trench. The nursery calls them dragons’ hearts, and the nursery is right, and there will be no more dragons. Every stone comes up out of the Coast, and their Sovereign may darken this hall any winter he likes by sending us half a ship. Last winter he did, three months together, and I watched our light run thin.” The gilt on his face shone in the column. “There was a heart under that vineyard older than any ever cut for a lamp. The Vales keep it written that such a heart, taken into the blood of our line, would give us back the sun our first kings carried in themselves, and we should never again buy our daylight of anybody. I went to ask for it.” His voice stayed level. “She refused me. And there was a fire.”

“Forty-one people.”

Andrew turned his head and looked at him.

“Forty-one,” he agreed. “I have the names. I keep a column.” He held Caleb’s eyes. “And for them the Coast took you, and kept you in the dark, and fed on you by force on a floor of black glass, and you have said so here, and the fae’s coin has not said one word against it. That was done to you. Not by me.” He drew breath. “You have come home in his colours. You stand at the foot of your father’s throne with his crest over your heart, and call it choosing, and you cannot so much as take your hand off it.”

Caleb’s hand was on the crescent. He had not known it was there. He left it.

“I need not argue with my brother,” said Andrew, more gently. “There is a man in this hall who slept across his door every night of those eleven weeks, and told me everything.” He did not turn round. “Walker.”

Behind Caleb, at the wall below the dais, Paul’s boots shifted on the marble.

“Come here.”

Paul came out from the wall into the white light and stopped two paces from the Crown Prince, as he had stopped two paces behind Caleb on every stair of the palace since Caleb was fourteen. His face was the colour of the ash in a cold grate. His big hands hung open at his sides.

“Tell them what you saw,” said Andrew, “in the gallery.”

Paul looked at the floor. There was nothing on the floor of the Throne Hall to look at but himself, upside down in the white marble with the light all round him, and he looked at it as he had once looked at the black glass of a gallery.

“I saw the Sovereign of the Coast feed on His Highness,” said Paul, flat, as a soldier gives a report. “In the black gallery, with His Highness’s wrists held on the glass. I told Your Highness so that night.”

The court made its sound again, long and low.

“By force.”

“By force, Highness.”

“And since?”

Paul did not answer.

“Walker,” said Andrew, and his voice went gentle in the white light, as Caleb had heard it go once through a door on a yacht. “Your mother is well. I had word from the steward at Loch Aird only yesterday. Her cough is much improved. Your sisters send their love.”

The whole court heard it, and not one of them knew what it had heard. Caleb knew. He saw it go down through Paul from the shoulders to the boots, as a blow goes down through a fence post, and the big hands closed, and opened, and closed.

The coin at Oriane’s throat kept still. It was true. Her cough was much improved.

“Tell them,” Andrew pressed, “whether my brother is enthralled.”

Paul lifted his head.

He looked at Caleb on the lowest step, and his lycan eyes were brimming in that light, and Caleb watched him stand on the edge of it, with his mother on one side of a word and his prince on the other.

And at the far end of the hall, the west doors opened.

\* \* \*

They opened as they had opened on a homecoming twenty-seven years ago, on a ripple of silk and a turning of heads, and the gold dust in the colonnade of light went up in the draught like chaff off a threshing floor. A woman came up the hall in the white of the Radiant Guard.

Her cloak was mired to the knee with the Highland road and her plait had come half out of its binding, and she came up the white floor between the columns of light as straight as she had once walked into a cave in the Fault with her sword out, with the noon running off her mail in sheets. Behind her came twenty of the Guard in their white, two by two, and in the middle of them three women who were not of the Guard.

They were lycans of the Highland March, broad and brown, and they looked up at the high glass and the blazing columns and the court in its silks as hill people look at a city, all at once and not liking any of it. Two were young and tall, in plaid shawls pinned at the shoulder, with the same broad face and the same tawny eyes, and they held the arms of the third between them. Her hair was grey, as only a lycan’s ever goes, and she walked up the Throne Hall of the Solar House in a grey wool shawl and her best boots with her chin up, and halfway up it she stopped, and put her hand to her chest, and coughed.

It was the cough of a woman who has had a bad winter and is nearly over it.

At the foot of the dais, Paul made a sound.

The high priest, holding the sun over the empty marble, looked round to see where the High Paladin was standing, saw her, and held on tighter.

Matilda did not go up to her place on the highest step. She stopped on the marble below with the Highland women at her back and her Guard behind them, and looked at her brother Andrew once, from his boots to his crown, as she would have looked at a sentry found asleep. Then she turned her head to Paul.

“Walker,” said the High Paladin. “Your mother is well.”

She said it flat, as she gave the watchword at a gate, and it was the same five words. It went into the hall and lay there beside the others, and every one of them was true, and they did not mean the same thing at all.

“The steward at Loch Aird kept a bad table and a worse gate. He has neither now.” She glanced at the older woman. “She has coughed the whole way here and told me it is nothing. Your sisters have told me a great deal about you on the yacht that I did not ask to know.”

Paul looked at his mother.

She did not cross the floor to him. She stood where she was among the Guard with her daughters holding her arms, and she lifted her two hands before her mouth and cupped them, as the Highland March teaches its children, so that what is said goes only where it is sent, and said something into them that nobody in the Throne Hall could hear but her son. Then she opened her hands.

Paul reached inside his livery.

He took out the sliver of crystal, a finger long, glowing at its heart, and went down on one knee at the Crown Prince’s feet and laid it on the white marble in the column of light, where its glow went out in the noon as a candle end goes out on a sunny sill, and stood up again without it.

“I’ll not be needing that, Highness.”

Andrew looked down at it.

“You asked was His Highness enthralled,” said Paul. He turned and faced the court and spoke to all of it. “He’s not. I’ve slept across his door fourteen years. I know what he’s like frightened and lying and pretending to be bored so nobody sees he’s neither. On the Coast he was none of them, after the first. He was happy. I watched him be it.” His voice cracked and he let it. “And I saw the Sovereign of the Coast come to the doors of that garden with his boot smoking on the sill, to come in after him through twelve suns, and not come in, because His Highness asked him not to. I told the Crown Prince that too.”

“Walker,” Andrew warned.

“I told you all of it, Highness, every night, into that.” He tipped his head at the crystal lying dark on the marble. “The garden. The gallery. The day we’d leave Dornhöhle, and which road, and what hour.” Paul looked at him at last, all the way. “I told nobody else. Nobody else was asking. And the Ashward was waiting in the Fault with my lady Cecily’s blades at the hour I gave you.” His hands had closed into fists. “You had my mother. You never had to say what you’d do. You only ever said she was well.”

The coin was silent. Oriane’s pen went on across the green page, and the court in its silks had gone still from the dais to the doors, as a field goes still before weather.

Andrew had not moved.

He stood in the column of light with the gilt of the Dust on his face, and Caleb saw that it had begun to go, at the temple and under the cheekbone where the grey waited, as the gilt rubs off a christening spoon with use. His voice was still the dais voice, with a single thread pulled out of it.

“Walker told me the road. That is what Walker was for. What I did with it, Walker does not know.” He looked along the envoys’ row. “You have ledgers that say I bought machinery, and a fae who says I sat at a table, and my sister who says I asked her for knives, and a lycan who says I was told a road. Every one of them is true. Not one of them is a line in my hand that gives any man a road.” He turned back to Caleb. “Show me one.”

Caleb said nothing.

“You cannot,” said Andrew. He said it without triumph, as a physician says a thing he had hoped not to have to say.

A guard of the Radiant Guard at the west doors had turned his head and was looking out into the vestibule beyond them.

“Matilda,” said Andrew, without looking at her. “Take my brother to his rooms. He is unwell.”

Matilda did not move.

“The Guard takes its orders from the King’s seal,” she answered, “and from the High Paladin while the King is silent. The King has been silent nine days. The Guard stands where I stand, Your Highness. Today I stand here.”

And from the far end of the hall, from the west doors, in a low, even voice that had first come to Caleb out of the dark of a hall on the Coast, someone said, “I have brought three, Your Highness. One will do.”

Caleb had known since the thread pulled. He turned anyway, because everybody else in the Throne Hall was turning, and because he had wanted to for five days.

Arian stood in the west doors.

He stood where the great leaf of the right-hand door threw the one shadow it could throw at noon, a wedge on the marble no wider than a man, with his left hand on the frame. He wore the Coast’s black, buttoned to the throat, and under his left arm he carried a box of black lacquer as long as his forearm, with a thorn of jet on its lid, and the frost was going off him. It rose from his shoulders and his hair in thin white threads, as the breath goes up off a horse in a cold yard at morning, and Caleb could see from the length of the hall that the left side of his face was red from the brow to the jaw, and that the hand on the frame had blistered across the knuckles, and he understood that there had been windows on the way here, and that Arian had walked past every one of them.

The court of the Solar House saw a vampire in the Throne Hall.

It made no sound at first. It only drew back, rank behind rank, towards the dais, as the shallows draw back down a shingle beach before a wave, in a long rustle of silk, and a lady in the second rank sat down on the marble quite suddenly in her skirts without seeming to know that she had done it. The three gnomes of Lowmere had all three taken hold of one another’s sleeves. The dwarf of the Holds had not moved at all, except to turn a page.

Andrew had turned too.

He had never once seen him. Caleb watched him look the length of the hall at the man in the door, as a general might look at last on a country he has drawn on maps all his life and sent soldiers into. The white came up into Andrew’s eyes. It came up hard, and the gilt on his face flared with it for a moment as a coal flares in a draught, and the column of light he stood in brightened round him.

At the far end of the hall Arian’s eyes found him. They rested on the man in white and gold for the space of a breath, as Enzo’s pen rests on a name in a column before it goes on down the page, meaning to come back to it. Then they went past him to the lowest step, and stayed there.

“Guard,” Andrew called. “The Coast is in the Throne Hall.”

Along the walls, in their white, every man of the Radiant Guard looked at the High Paladin.

“Stand,” said Matilda.

They stood.

Arian let go of the frame and stepped into the light.

The first column took him a pace inside the doors. Caleb felt it before he saw it. It came down the thread into his own chest as a blow had once come down it on the Fault road, sharp under the left ribs and then all over, a heat like a palm pressed to an oven door, and then like the inside of the oven, and he heard his own breath go in through his teeth. He saw the frost go off Arian all at once in a white rush, and the black silk on his shoulders begin to smoke, and the white of his face go red under the noon from the brow down, as a white rose goes brown at the rim in a window that gets the noon, and then go past red. Along the left side, where the vestibule windows had been at him already, the skin rose along the cheekbone in a long blister, and broke, and ran, and what ran of it dried white in the next column before it had reached his collar. Smoke went up off the ends of his hair. His shadow, which at that hour had been no more than a coin of black at his heels, boiled away under him to nothing, and he walked on without one. In the second rank a man put his sleeve over his mouth, because there was a smell in the Throne Hall now, under the lilies and the beeswax, of scorched silk, and of something under the silk that was not silk.

Caleb saw him not stop. Arian came on into the second column with the box under his arm and his head up and his hands at his sides, at a walk, the pace at which he had once crossed a black hall to a stranger with a seal in his palm, without haste, as a tide comes in. He did not lift a hand to his face. The light of the Solar House stood on him from every side and burned, and he went through it as a man goes through rain in a country he does not mean to stay in.

In his own column at the foot of the dais Andrew stood and watched the light of his house do to the Sovereign of the Coast what the nursery had always promised it would, and the white stood in his eyes, and he did not look away.

Caleb was off the step.

He did not remember leaving it. He was on the white marble at the foot of the dais with the column that had blazed over Andrew at his back, and the court drawing away in front of him like silk off a table, and the whole of the noon of the Throne Hall standing between him and the west doors in two long ranks of light, and he lifted his hands.

At the Vales he had learned to let his light go, and on the Coast he had learned, in a bedchamber where no lamp had ever been lit, that it would come when it was called. He had called it then to stay low, and warm, and it had stayed. He asked it now for something else. He asked it to lean.

And the noon of the Throne Hall leaned.

It went from the dais to the doors. Column after column of light, that had stood on that floor as straight as the trunks of a beech wood since before the Solar House had a name, bowed outward from the middle of the hall, slowly, the nearest first, like the reeds along a canal in Lowmere leaning back from the prow of a barge, until a lane of shade lay the whole way down the white floor. It was narrow, and cool, and the colour of a cellar between its casks, and it ran from the foot of the dais to the west doors, and it was the first shade that had lain down the middle of that floor at noon in a thousand years. The lilies at the foot of every column turned their heads after the light. The reflection of the hall in the polished floor went dark down the middle, as a mirror goes dark where the silver has been rubbed from its back.

It was heavy. The weight of it came into his arms and his back and his teeth, the whole noon of a hall built to hold the noon, and for one breath he thought that he could not, and something at the bottom of him that was older than his light, and had last stirred among the roses at Old Meridian, opened one eye and put its shoulder under the weight beside his.

He walked down the lane.

The court stood on either side of it in the bent light with their faces gold and their mouths open, and he went between them in the black of the Coast with his hands lifted and the shadow going before him, and nobody in all that hall said a word. At the far end of it, in the shade, Arian had stopped.

He stood on the dark marble with the smoke going up off his shoulders in threads, thinner now, and his face red from the brow to the jaw and broken along the cheekbone where the blister had run, and the black lacquer of the box gone soft and puckered under his arm, and his eyes were on Caleb, as they had been from a garden doorway on the first day, on his face and the gold in it, as if nothing else in the hall had been lit.

Caleb stopped a pace from him.

“I told you to stay in the dark.”

“You did not say for how long.” The frost had begun to come back. It came out along Arian’s cheekbone over the burn, a grain at a time, white over red.

“Five days seemed to me implied.”

“I did not care to be read to. I wished to look.”

Caleb could find nothing to say to that. He looked at the blistered knuckles, and the scorched silk, and the face that had never once been turned up to the sun, and he held out his hand.

“You have something of mine.”

Arian looked at him. Then he opened his coat.

It lay inside, over the left side of his chest, folded close in clean linen in Vesna’s brisk squares. Arian took it out and laid it in Caleb’s hand, and the linen fell back from it, and the noon that Caleb was holding off the two of them came through the lane of shade at its edges and touched the silver thread, and every line of silver drank it and lit, and at the collar the little sun of his house woke and shone.

“I said until I wanted it. I want it.”

He shook it out. It was a cloak that would not have met across a twelve-year-old’s chest, and he held it up in his two hands, high, by the shoulders, and turned it so that the sunburst faced the glass, so that whatever it drank of the noon it would give back to the sky and not to the man beneath.

Arian bowed his head.

He was taller by a head. He bowed it, there in the lane of shadow in the middle of the Throne Hall of the Solar House, before the court and the envoys and the *Lantern* and the High Paladin and her Guard, as Caleb had seen him bow it to nobody, and Caleb reached up and held the grey cloak over his bent head and his shoulders as the canopy is held over a bride at a wedding in Lowmere, on four poles, by her brothers, so that the rain shall not touch her on her way to the door.

“Stay in the dark,” Caleb murmured.

He had said it once from a sling of white silk going up past a lit doorway, with his hands taken from him and the light gone out of them, to keep a man from walking into a fire. He said it now with the dark in his own two hands, held up, and the fire bent back from it on either side the whole length of the hall.

Under the cloak the frost came back on Arian all at once. It came out on his hair and his lashes and his shoulders and the blistered backs of his hands, white, and took the gold coming off Caleb’s seams, and lit with it, and in the shade of a child’s cloak in the middle of the noon he stood glittering as a spider’s web glitters in a hawthorn on a frosty morning when the sun finds it.

“Yes,” Arian breathed, from under it.

They walked.

They walked up the lane of shadow from the west doors to the dais, slowly, in step, because that was the only way to do it, Caleb with his arms up and the cloak held over the bowed black head and the silver of it shining in the noon on either side, and Arian beneath it with the lacquer box under his arm and gold in every grain of the frost on him. The columns leaned away from them as they came. Caleb could feel the other heart going under the thread beside him, a hand’s breadth away after five days with a whole country between them, slow and even, keeping his count, and he kept the light bent with it, beat for beat, as a boy keeps step with a drum.

Behind them, in the west doors, someone else had come into the wedge of shadow the door threw.

Caleb could not turn his head. He heard Matilda instead, behind him, flat as a parade ground.

“Cloaks.”

He heard twenty white cloaks come off twenty pairs of shoulders at once with a sound like a flight of doves going up off a roof, and when he and Arian had come up the lane to the foot of the dais and he could turn, he saw the Guard coming up behind them in step in their shirtsleeves and mail, with their halberds held up and their white cloaks hung over the points and drawn together, so that they carried among them a long white awning of wool, like the awning of a market stall being carried home at the end of a fair day. The noon came through it thin and white as milk through muslin. And under it, in the middle, in his dove grey with his long hands folded and a faint thread of smoke rising from his cuffs, walked the Consigliere of the Coast, as composed as if he were walking down to the lamp room on a month-day.

He looked once at the High Paladin as he came up level with her.

“Paladin.”

“You are the uncle,” said Matilda.

“I am,” said Enzo, and the Guard set the awning down round him at the foot of the dais like a tent pitched in the snow, and he stood inside it, and was still.

On the dais, the Queen of Aloria had risen from her chair.

She stood in her white beside the empty throne with one hand gone to the arm of it, and she was not looking at the vampire, nor at the Guard, nor at her elder son. She was looking at a child’s grey cloak held up over a bowed black head in her younger son’s two hands, at the little sun on its collar shining back at the glass, every ray of it, and her other hand had gone to her own breast and closed there.

She did not sit down again.

Andrew had not moved from his column. He stood in it with the white still in his eyes, and he looked at the lane of shadow that went down the middle of his crowning from the doors to his feet, and the light bowed away from it on both hands, and the two men standing in it under a cloak.

“You lit this hall,” he said, “at a year old.”

His voice was low, and it had left the dais behind. Caleb thought that nobody but the blood on the steps and the woman in the gilt chair could hear it.

“And you have put it out for him.”

“No,” Caleb answered. “I have only asked it to stand aside.”

At the foot of the dais Arian, under the cloak, set the box down on the dark marble of the lane.

The shade it lay in carried it. It went across the floor to the foot of the steps by itself, without a sound, as a skiff goes off a beach on the turn of the tide, and stopped at the hem of Eleanor’s dark blue, because she was the one who kept the books. She came down the steps and took it up, with the lamps of the dais brightening over her. She had sat at his left hand once at a long table on the Coast with a warrant under her gloves. She looked at the burn on his face, and the frost lit gold under the grey wool, and her brother holding the grey wool up.

“Sovereign.”

“Your Highness.”

The lid of the box was black lacquer with the Ashward’s thorn inlaid on it in jet, and the jet drank the edge of the light and gave back nothing.

“Don Salvatore,” said Andrew.

He said it from his column, as a host remarks an empty chair at dinner. Arian kept his eyes on Eleanor.

“Don Salvatore is dead.”

The coin lay silent on its ribbon. In the gilt chair Oriane’s pen stopped, and went on.

Eleanor opened the box on her arm, as she would have opened a despatch case in a tent. On top lay the Kessel compact on the Holds’ grey paper, heavy with the Chamber’s wax, and beneath it three folded letters, each closed with grey wax and a little sun pressed into the wax, neat as a thumbprint.

“That is his seal,” said Eleanor to the hall. “His own, and not the Throne’s. He has used it since the Vales.” She unfolded the first letter and looked at it. Her face did not change. “And that is his hand. I have read the Treasury’s returns in it every quarter for eleven years.”

“Read it,” said Arian.

She read it. It was short. It gave the road, the Fault along the Holds’ ledge, and the night, and the hour, which was dusk, and the number of the carriages and who rode on their roofs. *The Sovereign in the second*, Eleanor read, in the Chancellery voice, and the dwarf of the Holds laid down his pen. *My first clause stands above all the rest.*

The coin at Oriane’s throat was silent.

Caleb stood with his arms up and the cloak in his hands and heard his brother’s handwriting read aloud to the courts of the world, and it went into him slowly, as cold goes into a man who has stood too long in a river, from the feet. The man who had ridden in the second carriage, and taken the fifth blade under his ribs, stood beneath his hands with his head bowed. He did not let the light waver. He kept it leaning.

“The first clause also,” Arian added.

Eleanor looked at him. Then she turned back through the grey sheets of the compact to the top of the first, and read it. *His Royal Highness the Prince Caleb is not to be harmed.* There was more, about weapons and payment and where things were to be done, and she read that too, down to its last words, which were *alive and whole and unafraid*.

She lowered the sheet.

Nobody in the Throne Hall said anything at all. The columns of light stood bowed back on either side of the lane, and the lilies at their feet had turned their heads to follow them, and the priest held the sun over an empty patch of marble at the foot of the dais with his arms gone white at the knuckles.

“I wrote it first,” Andrew insisted. “Above everything.”

“I read it twice,” Arian answered.

From inside the white awning of the Guard’s cloaks, at the foot of the steps, a dry voice spoke.

“Your Highness.”

Andrew turned his head.

Enzo had put out one long white hand between two of the hanging cloaks into the edge of the light, and the light took the back of it at once, and he did not draw it in. A thread of gold lay across his palm. It ran off the edge of his fingers in a loose bright curl, and swung there, and caught the light.

It was a chain as fine as a hair, a chain for a lady’s locket, with one link at its end wrenched open.

“This was in her fist on the cellar stair, with your brother’s signet on it.” The light lay on the back of his hand, and the skin there had reddened and lifted, and one blister had broken and begun to run between the knuckles. “The fire had closed her hand. I opened it.”

His voice had not altered by a hair.

Andrew’s hand had gone to his throat.

It went up of itself, as it had gone up in a tower beside a blade of light, and the fingers felt beside the steel chain of a key for a finer one that had gone, and closed on nothing at the hollow of his throat.

Enzo drew his hand back into the shadow of the white cloaks, and the chain with it.

“At supper,” said Caleb.

His brother looked at him.

“In the spring. You asked. It stuck at your knuckle, and we laughed.” His arms ached. He kept them up. “I gave it to you.”

He had said it once before, to the dark, on a coverlet on the Coast, as a clerk reads back a sum he has got wrong. He said it now to his brother’s face, in the noon of the Throne Hall, before all of them, and it was not that sum any longer.

He had meant to say more. He had carried the rest of it for eleven weeks, across the Coast and the Fault and back, every word of it true, and had laid them all out in order in the dark like the stones of a case. Now his brother stood in front of him with his hand at his throat and the gilt going off his face, and Caleb reached for the words and could not pick up one. They were all true. He had never been able to say a true thing only because it would hurt, and he could not learn it now, before the whole court, for Andrew.

“Andrew,” he said, and that was all of it.

Andrew stood in his column with his hand at his throat, and the gilt of the Dust going off his face at the edges, grey coming up under it.

“I never wanted you hurt,” he said.

He said it to Caleb as if there were nobody else in the hall, quite low, without the voice of the dais or any other voice, as a boy of twelve might have said it at the foot of a throne with a baby asleep on his shoulder.

At the foot of the dais the copper coin hung still on its green ribbon in the light.

It was true. It had always been true.

“Who do you suppose,” Andrew asked, “sent the fae to his door?”

Under Caleb’s hands, under the grey wool, Arian lifted his head.

“Walker told me of the flower on the night it opened,” Andrew went on. “He told me how many your heart missed in a hundred, and that the stones in that garden were holding it half shut. I had read the commentary at the Vales. I knew how long it gave you.” His hand had come down from his throat. “And Don Salvatore had told me himself at the Kessel Pass, to comfort me, that the Ashward keeps a cure for everything it makes, in a place under the Holds that does not exist, so that nobody it pays need ever be paid twice. I could not go into the Holds’ iron after a Caruso cure, and the Coast would have poured a cure from my hand on the floor.” He looked at the bowed black head under the cloak. “So I wrote to the one creature in the world who would go down under the Holds for you and not come up again without it. I wrote it in the hand the Treasury teaches its clerks, which is nobody’s. I sealed it in my own wax and left off the sun, and sent it to Chalmont by a fae who did not know me, with her fee in my glove.”

The court did not breathe.

“Madame de Chardon,” said Caleb.

Oriane’s feathers had gone bronze from the collar to the hem.

“It was grey kid. The glove. Lined with white silk, and stitched inside the cuff, where nobody looks, with a little sun, by a glover in the upper town who makes for one household in the Citadel and no other.” She looked at Andrew. “I knew the glove, Highness. A glove is not a hand, and I do not print what I cannot swear to.”

“You may print it now.”

“Then I shall,” said Oriane, and wrote it down.

Caleb stood in the lane of shadow with the weight of the bowed noon on his back and looked at his brother, who had given away a road in one hand and a cure in another, at two tables, under the same grey wax, and had meant both.

“You went,” said Andrew to the cloak. “Walker told me that too. Down into the Holds’ own iron in a cask, with your hands on silver.” His voice had gone thin. “I hated you for it every hour until Walker told me he had woken.”

Arian said nothing. Under the grey wool, the frost on his shoulders had gone quite still.

“Come home, Caleb,” said Andrew.

He came out of his column.

He crossed the three steps of marble between them in his white and gold with his hand out, and the white had come into his eyes with the last of the Dust, hard, and he took Caleb by the wrist.

He took the left wrist, the one held up to the cloak, with the iron ring on the hand above it. His fingers were chill, as they had been in the tower, with the chill of a Solar child kept from the sun, and then they were not.

The light came up through them.

It came up out of Andrew in a white rush at the touch, as it had come up into the oak of his table two dozen times, and went into the first thing it found. The black silk at Caleb’s cuff scorched. Under it, the skin of his wrist went white, and then red, and then lifted in the print of four fingers and a thumb, and the pain went up his arm into his shoulder as clean and bright as a struck bell, and a sound came out of him, and the cloak tilted in his hands.

And the light stood up.

He felt it go. He had been holding the whole of that noon bent back from the middle of the floor with his will and the old thing’s shoulder under it, and at the burn it went out of his hands as a rope goes out through the hands of a man who has been struck, and the columns swung back. They came upright down the whole hall in one long sigh of light, from the doors to the dais, and the lane of shade was gone, and the noon came down on the man under the cloak from every side at once.

Arian came out from under it.

Caleb did not see him move. There was the grey wool sliding off black shoulders, and the frost going off him in one white rush, and then Arian was past him in the full light with the smoke pouring off him, and his hand was round Andrew’s throat.

He lifted him. He did it with the one hand, as a man lifts a lamp to see by, and the heir of the Solar House came up off the marble of his own crowning until only his toes were on it, and then not those.

The hall went up. On the dais the Queen of Aloria screamed her son’s name, and the envoy of Lys was shouting something, and the Guard along the walls had come off the walls with their halberds levelled, all of them, and stopped, because the High Paladin had put out one arm and said *Hold* in a voice the Guard heard in their boots.

Every shadow in the Throne Hall came to him.

There were not many. At noon in that hall there had only ever been the black pools at the foot of every column and every courtier, no longer than a step. They came out from under the feet of the court and the foot of every column all down the hall and ran across the white marble to him from every side, as crows come down out of a winter sky onto the one field where something lies still, and climbed him, and went into him, and came out larger. Where they had crossed the floor the lilies at the feet of the nearest columns had gone black at the edges, as a garden goes on the morning after a hard frost, and hung their heads. The eyes had gone crimson, all the way across, as they had gone in the Fault. The skin of his face was blistering in the noon from temple to chin, and lifting, and breaking, and there was no frost left on him to cover any of it.

Andrew hung from his hand. His face had gone from gold to red under the Dust, and from red to the dark of a bruise, and the gilt on it crazed as glaze crazes on a pot taken too soon from the kiln. His mouth stood open, and nothing came up it. Under Arian’s fingers something in his throat had begun to give, with a dry creak, as wicker creaks under a boot before it goes.

His light came up white against the hand at his throat, in rushes, each one fainter than the last, as a lamp leaps at the bottom of its oil. The white of it went into Arian’s palm and burned there. Caleb could smell it. The palm blackened where the light met it, and the skin split across it, and Arian’s fingers did not open. Andrew’s hands had come up and closed on his wrist. They were smoking, and Arian did not look down at them.

There was nothing in Arian’s face at all. He held the Crown Prince of Aloria off the floor with the noon taking the skin from his cheekbones, and his face held no more than a farm wife’s face holds in the yard behind her kitchen with a hen in her two hands for the pot.

Matilda went herself.

She came across the marble with her sword out of its sheath, and three strides from him the air stopped her. It stopped her as the wind stops a man at the top of the Highland road in the first gale of winter, with his cloak flat out behind him and his boots going nowhere, and the frost ran up her blade from the point to the guard and furred it white, and she stood braced in it, a halberd’s length from the Sovereign of the Coast, and could come no nearer. Inside the white awning Enzo had not moved at all. He was looking at Caleb.

On Arian’s wrist Andrew’s hands had begun to slacken. His feet had stopped looking for the floor.

Caleb felt every grain of it down the thread. He felt the noon on Arian’s face as if it were on his own, and the white of Andrew’s light in the palm of his hand, and under all of it the heart, the other heart, going high and hard and fast, as it had gone once on the fourth night, a country away at the far end of the thread, not keeping anybody’s time.

He reached for the hand.

He laid the burned one, his left, with the iron ring on it that Arian had put there on the first night, flat over the back of Arian’s fist where it was closed on his brother’s throat. The first time he had done it he had been eight years old, kneeling in the roses. The skin under his palm was burning and cold at once, and the knuckles stood up white, and he did not take his hand away.

“Arian.”

The crimson did not go.

“He is Eleanor’s to judge,” said Caleb.

He said it low and plain, as he would have said it to a patient, and he felt it go down the thread, and he felt the heart at the far end of it hear him.

It came down from the high hard place it had gone to, slowly, as a hawk comes down to the glove when the falconer has stood still long enough, and fell in with his count again.

The fingers opened.

They opened one at a time under his palm, the first and then the second and then the rest, as a fist had once opened under a child’s hand among the roses, and the shadows went out of Arian down his arm and onto the floor and back across the marble to the feet of the columns and the court, every one to its own, and lay down where they belonged.

Andrew came down out of the hand onto the marble, and his knees went from under him.

He knelt there in his white and gold dragging at the air, with a sound in his throat like a saw going through wet wood, and the marks of five fingers coming up dark on his neck, and the light went out of his eyes as if somebody had shut a door on it, and the gilt went off his face. It went all at once, the hour spent, as the gold goes off a cloud when the sun is behind the hill, and under it in the full noon of the Throne Hall of his fathers his face was grey, and thin, and older than any face of the Solar House is ever allowed to be.

Caleb asked the light to lean.

It came harder this time. His wrist was burning and his arms were shaking and the old thing at the bottom of him had lain down again, and he asked it all the same, and the columns bowed back again one after another down the hall, and the shade came down over Arian where he stood smoking on the white marble with his burned hand open at his side, black across the palm where it had held.

He had not looked at Andrew again. He was looking at Caleb’s wrist.

Somebody had picked up the cloak.

It was Cecily. She had come down off the steps with her hair coming down on every side of her head at once, and picked up the grey cloak off the marble and shaken it out, and taken a halberd out of the hands of the nearest of Matilda’s Guard before he had understood that she was asking for it. She hung the cloak by its collar from the point. Then she stood on her toes a halberd’s length from the Sovereign of the Obsidian Coast, a whole head and more below him, and held the shaft up in her two burned arms as high as they would go, so that the cloak hung over his face with the sunburst turned to the glass, as she had seen her brother hold it.

“I have it.” Her voice was shaking. “Bend your head. I cannot reach.”

Arian looked down at her, at the woman who had set the fifth blade twice, and bent his head.

Eleanor stood at the foot of the steps with the lacquer box open on her arm, and every lamp on the dais was burning white over her head, as lamps burned in any room she was angry in, and she looked down at her brother on his knees on the marble.

“Tilda.”

“Here.”

“The Crown Prince will go to his rooms under the Guard of the High Paladin. His own rooms. With the shutters as he likes them.” Her voice was perfectly level. “Tomorrow, at the hour of the Eclipse, at Old Meridian, before witnesses of the Holds, the Solar Throne will hold the inquiry it required of the Coast. It will hear what was done in a black gallery, as it said it would.” She closed the lacquer box. “And it will hear what was done in a vineyard. Every house asks for an inquiry, I am told. Ours has never yet held one. It will begin with its own.”

At the foot of the dais, inside the white awning of cloaks, the Consigliere of the Coast inclined his head to her, once, the full depth owed by the Coast to a power of the Solar Throne, and a little lower.

The Queen had come down from the dais.

Nobody had seen her leave her chair. She came down the steps past her daughters in her white, and past Caleb with the burn on his wrist, and she did not stop for any of them. She went down on her knees on the white marble beside her elder son, in the full light, as he had once gone down beside her chair in a shuttered room with a ruined broadsheet in her lap, and she put her two hands on his face, above the marks on his throat.

Andrew looked at her.

“I have two sons,” said the Queen, to nobody, to the hall.

Then she got up, and he got up with her, because she would not let go of his face until he had, and four of Matilda’s Guard came round them in their shirtsleeves and mail, and they went down the hall together towards the west doors, the Queen of Aloria in white and the Crown Prince in white and gold, through the columns of noon, and the court drew back from them on both sides and let them go. At the doors Andrew stopped and looked down at his own two hands, which had burned his brother. Then the Queen said something to him, and he went out.

The high priest of the Citadel temple was still holding up the sun.

He had held it through all of it, over the empty marble where an heir had knelt, with his arms shaking and his face gone the colour of the lilies, because nobody had told him he might stop. He looked at the lowest step, where Caleb had gone back without noticing and stood with his burned wrist held against his chest.

“Caleb of the Solar House,” said the priest, faintly. “Will you stand under this sun?”

“No,” said Caleb.

The priest turned his head, slowly, to the step above.

“No,” said Cecily, from under her arms, with the cloak held up and her hair all down her back.

“No,” said Eleanor, with the box under her arm.

The priest looked up at the highest step. It was empty. Then he looked round, as he always looked round, and found the High Paladin standing at the foot of the dais with her Guard about her and mud to the knee.

“No,” said Matilda.

The priest lowered the sun.

He brought it down slowly, with great care, because his arms had stopped answering him some while since, and laid it on its cushion of white velvet on the step of the empty throne, where the King’s mantle lay folded down the stair, and it lay there and blazed at the high glass with nobody’s head under it. Then the high priest of the Citadel sat down on the lowest step of the dais beside Caleb’s boots, without asking anybody’s leave, and put his face in his hands.

At the end of the long west gallery, behind a door with no light under it, the King of Aloria was still King.

\* \* \*

The noon still leaned away from the middle of the hall, and in the lane it left, under a child’s grey cloak held up on a halberd in the burned arms of the Grand Alchemist of the Solar Throne, the Sovereign of the Obsidian Coast stood with his head bent and the frost returning over the blisters.

Caleb looked at his right hand.

It hung at Arian’s side, open, with the burn of Andrew’s light in the palm of it, and on it were two rings of old heavy gold, and beside them the third finger, bare. Arian’s thumb had gone to it. It went across to the root of the finger, as Caleb had watched it go in a carriage and a cave and at a window in the morning, and turned there, a quarter turn, at nothing.

Tomorrow, under a black sun, the Nine would look at that hand and ask the shadows about it, and in Dornhöhle a dwarf with three plaits would bring a hammer down over a casket with nothing in it.

*Not in the hall*, he had thought, in a cellar. *At the door.*

He had been carried up past the door in a sling, and there was no door now. There was the Throne Hall of his fathers with the noon bent back from the middle of it, and the courts of the world in their silks, and his three sisters, and a lycan woman in her best boots holding her son’s two big hands in hers. There was nowhere left to do it but here.

He did not mind in the least.

“Tilda. Your knife.”

Matilda did not ask what for. She drew her guard knife from her belt, the horn of its grip rubbed pale by years of her hand, and turned it, and gave it to him hilt first.

He undid the top three jet buttons of his coat with his right hand, because his left would do nothing he asked of it, and the court watched the youngest prince of the Solar House unbutton the black of the Coast at the foot of his father’s throne and did not know where to look. He put his hand inside, under the crescent, and found what his palm had found on nearly every night since Dornhöhle, a weight no bigger than a coin and colder than the silk, held there by a row of Vesna’s stitches, close and even and patient.

*Nobody’ll touch it but me*, Vesna had said, in the lilies.

He was sorry to cut them. He cut them anyway, one after another, with the point of his sister’s knife, as he had once cut the stitches out of a healed wound on a stable boy’s scalp at the Vales, and they gave, and something heavy and cold slid down into his palm.

He took it out into the light.

It was heavy old gold, its edges rubbed soft by the hands of every Sovereign who had worn it. In the iron of the Seventh Chamber the stone had been dull. Here in the noon of the Throne Hall it was the stone of the first night, on the hand of a stranger coming towards him out of the dark, so black it resembled a hole in the world. The light of the columns went down into it from either side as the tide pours into a cave mouth at the flood, and the stone drank it, and stayed black.

And every shadow in the Throne Hall turned to look.

At noon there was one at the foot of every soul in the hall, short and black, and two hundred of them came round on the white marble all together, under the feet of the court and the envoys and the Guard and the gilt chair, as a whole bed of sunwort turns its faces together when a cloud goes off the sun, and pointed. They pointed at Caleb’s hand. The court looked down and saw its own shadows leaning, and somebody in the second rank said *oh* aloud, faintly, and took a step back from his own feet.

Under the cloak, Arian was looking at the ring in Caleb’s palm. His face had gone entirely still, and the frost that had been coming back on him grain by grain stopped coming, and stayed where it was. His thumb had stopped at the root of the bare finger. It did not turn.

He said nothing.

“Your hand.”

Arian gave it to him.

He took the wrist. It was cold, and it was burned across the palm, and he held it in his own right hand as he had held it once to heal it, and turned it over, as a physician turns a hand, and was not in the least clinical about it. He laid the ring at the tip of the third finger. It went over the knuckle without any trouble at all, as it had been made to, and came home at the root of the finger, where a quarter turn of a thumb had been looking for it every day for nearly four weeks.

And under his fingers, against the inside of that cold wrist, something beat.

On the first night, in a hall on the Coast, he had felt it under a stranger’s fingers on his own wrist, faint and slow and unmistakable, and told himself it was his own. He felt it now, and it was. It went under his fingers slow and even, in the time he had first counted for it, on his knees in a glasshouse, and his own heart kept step with it, and he could not have said, holding that cold wrist in the noon, which of the two he was counting.

“Wear it. Do not take it off.”

The ring woke.

The black of the stone did not change. But all round it, on the white skin of Arian’s hand and up his wrist under Caleb’s fingers, the frost came out thick and white and fine as it had not come since a carriage on the high moor, and every grain of it took his gold and kindled. The shadows of the hall leaned off the ring in Caleb’s palm and went to it on Arian’s hand, all of them, as every face on a quay turns to the one sail it has been waiting for. And the thread that the Holds had hammered through his chest went tight, sudden and sweet from the breastbone outward, as it had at the sealing, and he felt it twice, once in himself, and once coming back to him from the other end.

Arian looked at his hand.

He looked at it a while, at the ring where it belonged and the burned palm and the frost, as a man looks at a letter in his own hand that he does not remember having written. Then he looked at Caleb.

“What did it cost?”

He asked it plainly, as a man asks the price of a thing already in his hands, meaning to pay it whatever it is.

“Everything I had.”

The hall was so quiet that Caleb could hear Oriane’s pen stop.

“I had a portion. It was settled on me on my twelfth day by my mother’s clerk, and kept in the Seventh Chamber, and it was the only thing I owned in the world that nobody had given me for a reason. It came to the price of your ring, and a little over. I paid the price out of it, and the little over to the Chamber, for its silence, and closed the account.” He kept hold of the wrist. “I have nothing at the Seventh Chamber. I told your uncle so, across his desk, the night we came home, and it was perfectly true.”

From inside the white awning at the foot of the dais there was a sound, short and dry, that might in anybody else have been a laugh.

Under his fingers the beat in the cold wrist had quickened, which was as near as Arian would come to asking, and Caleb answered it.

“At the last hammer, the night before we left Dornhöhle. Master Brandt bowed to me as deep going out as coming in, to the hair, which I am told the Seventh Chamber does not do.” Caleb felt his mouth move, and let it. “I sewed it into my coat that night. You held it on the Fault road, through the lining, when you were burning, and did not know. You put your hand under my coat in your own study, on the night of the cloak, and I put your fingers on my wrist instead and told you it was the usual place.”

The frost on Arian’s lashes held the gold in grains.

“I meant to say it at the door,” Caleb admitted. “On the sill, tonight, on the Coast, where nobody else could hear.” He looked round him, at the bent light and the court and the leaning shadows and his sister on her toes with her arms shaking under the halberd. “I am afraid I have had to say it here.”

Arian’s thumb went across to the ring, and found something there at last, and stayed.

“I paid it in seven drafts. I asked Master Brandt if it might be cut into seven, and he said the Chamber had no objection to arithmetic.” He could feel Eleanor at his shoulder without looking round, as he had felt the lamps brighten for her all his life. “My sister had asked me a question, in the pence of a complaint about some crystals. I had not answered it. I could not, then. I had it written on a paper in my coat with something else on the other side that was not true any longer. So I answered her in the pence of seven drafts, and burned the paper at the Chamber’s lamp.”

Arian waited.

“Willing,” said Caleb.

It went into the Throne Hall and lay there, in the white light, among the leaning shadows, and the copper coin at the throat of the *Lantern* was silent.

At the foot of the steps, with the lacquer box under her arm and the lamps burning white over her head, Eleanor had put her gloved hand over her mouth.

Arian stood under the grey cloak with his wrist in Caleb’s hand and did not move, and down the thread Caleb could feel all of it held in him, as the Holds hold a pledge, and none of it spent.

“You told me once, at the back of a sheep cave, under a vein of glass, that you did not want to be paid.”

“Yes.”

“You have been,” Caleb teased. “So there.”

Somebody in the court laughed, once, high and astonished, and stopped. At the foot of the halberd, Cecily made a wet sound and did not let her arms come down.

Arian lifted Caleb’s other hand.

He did it slowly, the left, the burned one, with the iron ring on it that he had put there himself on the first night and told him not to take off. He turned it over in his own burned palm, so that the scorch on the inside of the wrist lay uppermost in the shade of the cloak, raw and red where Andrew’s light had gone into it. And he bent his head, there in the Throne Hall of the Solar House before both courts and the *Lantern* and the envoys of the Dominion, and laid his mouth against it, on the inside of the wrist, over the pulse, where a physician counts.

It was cold. The cold went into the burn as snow goes onto a hearthstone, and the heat went out of it at once, and did not come back.

He lifted his head.

“Thank you.”

Caleb looked at him, at the burned face and the frost lit gold and the black eyes under the grey wool, which took that gold and kept it, as the stone on his hand had taken the noon, and his own eyes stung, and he let them.

“That is eight. You will never catch up.”

“No,” Arian murmured. “I do not mean to try.”

The light stood back on either side of them.

Caleb had held it bowed for longer now than he had ever held anything. His wrist had stopped burning, and his arms had stopped shaking, and still the light leaned, because he asked it to, and it seemed it had decided that it would. He lifted the cloak off the halberd’s point. Cecily gave the halberd back to its owner with a sound of pure relief and sat down on the lowest step beside the high priest, who moved up for her, and began, with great concentration and no success at all, to put up her hair.

Caleb held the cloak up himself, over the bowed black head, and turned the little sun at its collar to the glass.

“I owe you notes. On the noon of the Citadel. I wrote them on the backs of my brother’s speeches, every morning for five mornings, and I could not find a colour for it in any of them. I looked at it all day. I think I told you it was the white of a page.”

Arian lifted his head under the cloak.

He lifted it slowly, as a man lifts his face to weather he has heard of all his life and never stood out in, and looked up past the grey hem of a child’s cloak and the silver shining in its weave, at the high glass of the Throne Hall, and the columns of light standing bowed back from him on either side all the way down the hall to the doors, white and blazing, as the noon of the Citadel had stood on that floor at every hour of every day for a thousand years, the light that every Umbral child is taught, before anything else, will kill him.

He looked a long while.

Caleb did not look at the noon. He had seen it every day of his life and never once found the colour of it. He looked at Arian, at the white face turned up into the edge of the light under the cloak with frost in his lashes, and at the noon lying on him, where it lay on the frost and lit it, and he saw that it was gold.

It had been waiting, after all, for somebody to come and look at it who had never seen it before.

“Gold,” said Caleb.

Arian did not look away from the light.

“Write it down.”
