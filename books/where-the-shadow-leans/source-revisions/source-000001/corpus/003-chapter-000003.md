## Chapter 2: Chapter One - The Ash of the Solar Crest

*Where the blood of an Umbral house is shed upon Umbral ground by a hand of the Solar House, the wronged may claim the debt in blood, or in a hostage of equal rank. The hostage passes wholly into the keeping of the wronged, and his own house shall have no further claim upon him.*

— The Twilight Accord, Article Nine (translated from the Lyssois)

\* \* \*

Caleb woke to the taste of ash.

It was the stale, powdery taste of a hearth no one had swept in centuries, and it settled on his tongue like the dust of an old library. He lay still and allowed the world to arrange itself about him by degrees. First came the low hum of light-engines beneath the deck, then the ache behind his eyes, then the cool silk of a chaise beneath his cheek. His last clear memory was a glass of Citadel white, pressed into his hand by his mother’s chamberlain, and the chamberlain’s face as he drank, composed into a blankness so courteous that it ought to have warned him.

*Ah,* he thought. *So that is how we are to do it.*

He sat up. The sky-yacht’s cabin was all pale ashwood and polished silver, with the royal sunburst worked into every panel and fitting lest he forget whose property he was. The sunbursts had been made to drink the Citadel’s noon and give it back, and out here, with nothing to drink, every one of them had faded to the tired yellow of a pear left too long in the bowl. Beyond the porthole there was no sky at all, only a ceiling of volcanic cloud rolling over itself like a disgruntled sea, and beneath it a coastline of black stone that fell away into canyons so deep their floors were lost to sight, pricked here and there with violet light like embers in a banked fire.

The Obsidian Coast. He had seen it on maps his whole life, inked in black along the bottom edge of the known world beneath a line of immaculate Alorian script. *Here the light does not reach.*

On the cabin’s display panel, a holographic broadsheet turned in slow, gilded circles. His own face smiled out of it, borrowed from last season’s portrait, the one in which the painter had so generously improved upon his jaw. Beneath it ran a royal decree in his father’s florid hand. *His Royal Highness Prince Caleb has departed upon a sacred and solitary pilgrimage to the outer temples. The Crown asks that his privacy be respected in this season of reflection.*

He read it twice. Then he laughed, once, and it did not come out right.

“A season of reflection,” he announced to the empty cabin. “How very poetic of them. And I suppose they are not wrong about the solitude, at least.”

His father had come to his rooms the evening before, which his father never did. The King had stood by the window with his back to the room and said that Caleb’s signet ring had been found at a vineyard in the Marches, among the dead. Caleb had waited for the rest of the sentence. There had been no rest of the sentence. There had only been the chamberlain, and the wine.

His signet. He had lent it to his brother Andrew weeks ago, laughing, because Andrew had asked and Caleb had never refused Andrew anything. Andrew would have given it back. Andrew must have given it back, and Caleb had simply misplaced it, the way he misplaced gloves and hours and the names of minor ambassadors.

He set the thought aside like a letter in a familiar hand, and did not open it.

He rose and straightened the silver-threaded lapels of his coat. He saw that his hands were not steady, and folded them behind his back until they were, and went to the door.

Paul Walker was standing outside it, as he had stood outside every door Caleb had slept behind since Caleb was fourteen. The Accord allowed a hostage one sworn attendant, and someone had seen to it that the attendant was Paul. Caleb smiled at him with the whole of his face, as a traveller smiles at the first lit window on a long road home.

Paul stared at the floor.

\* \* \*

They brought the yacht down into a courtyard of black stone cut into the mountain’s flank, and when the hatch folded open the Coast’s twilight came in to meet him. It was violet at the edges and ash-dark at the heart, and it neither brightened nor deepened but only hung over everything, so that the gold of the yacht’s lamps spilling across the flagstones behind him looked suddenly gaudy, like a kingfisher that has blundered into a rookery. The air pressed close and heavy. It smelled of volcanic stone and crushed velvet and, beneath both, the faint copper of very old blood.

The hall into which they delivered him had been carved rather than built. Its walls rose in ridged black columns of volcanic glass that caught what little light there was and swallowed it. Crystal lamps hung on chains from a height he could not see, each burning a low and bruised violet, enough to see by and nowhere near enough to feel. After twenty-eight years beneath the Citadel’s eternal noon, Caleb’s skin took the gloom for cold, and he wanted, with undignified urgency, to chafe his arms.

He kept them where they were. He was a prince of the Solar House, and there was an audience.

A lycan stood sentinel by the doors, Marches-born by the look of him, grey-flecked and monumental, his Conti black stretched over a body that plainly would rather have gone without it. His nostrils flared as Caleb passed. Then they flared again at Paul, who had followed two paces behind, and the rumble that rose in the sentinel’s chest had nothing welcoming in it. A lycan in Alorian livery was evidently a thing to be despised on this coast.

Against the far wall a fae in a coat of iridescent feathers sat perched on the arm of a chair, walking a thin copper coin across her knuckles. It chimed, softly and sweetly, each time it turned. Beside her stood a vampire in dove grey, with a face that looked no older than thirty and eyes that looked a great deal older. He wore the pained, upright bearing Caleb knew from every minister who had ever lost an argument with his father.

And at the far end of the hall, behind a desk of black stone, someone sat in the dark.

Caleb could not see him. He could feel him, as one feels the edge of a cliff in darkness. The Star-Grace beneath his skin, that quiet golden thread of healing magic he had carried since birth, drew in on itself like a flower closing in sudden shade. He had heard the stories every Alorian child heard, of the pull the old vampire bloodlines exerted, of grown knights swooning in the presence of a Sovereign. What he felt was the cold and the drop and the dark, and nothing in the least like swooning.

He reminded himself that every creature in the room could hear his heart, which was beating like a finch against the bars of a gilded cage, and there was nothing to be done about that. There was a great deal to be done about his face.

He allowed his gaze to travel the hall, from the columns to the chains to the unrelieved dark, and let his face settle into polite, well-bred suffering, as if a friend were showing him round some regrettable renovations.

“How perfectly atmospheric,” he said, and the hall gave his voice back to him, silken and unhurried. “I confess myself curious. Is the darkness an aesthetic conviction, or has someone in this household forgotten to pay the chandler? I ask only because I should hate to walk into a pillar and provoke a diplomatic incident.” Then, as if it had only that moment occurred to him, he added, “Another diplomatic incident, I should say.”

The fae’s coin stopped mid-turn. The sentinel made a sound suspiciously like a laugh, and hastily turned it into a cough. The grey-suited vampire shut his eyes and appeared to pray for patience to a god he had stopped believing in some centuries ago.

From the far end of the hall came the scrape of a chair.

\* \* \*

Arian Conti was younger than Caleb had expected.

The fae broadsheets that fluttered through the Citadel’s lower markets, the scandalous ones that no prince of the Solar House was permitted to read and that Caleb devoured with his breakfast, called him the Sovereign of Vesperia, the Youngest Don, the Knife of the Obsidian Coast. Youngest or not, he was a vampire, and Caleb had furnished his imagination with someone ancient, gaunt and faintly mildewed. The man who came out of the dark looked scarcely older than Caleb himself.

He resolved from the gloom like ink running backwards into water. He was a full head taller than Caleb, lean and broad through the shoulders, and he wore a shirt of heavy black silk buttoned to the throat without a crease anywhere on it. His hair was black, worn a little long and swept back from his brow, save for a single lock that had escaped across his forehead. Three rings of old, heavy gold sat on his right hand, one of them set with a stone so black it resembled a void.

Every child in Aloria was told that the Umbral houses were beautiful, in the same breath and the same tone in which they were told that nightshade was pretty. Caleb had grown up among beauty, and was generally held to be a rather dazzling example of it himself, though he had always privately suspected that people mistook his good humour for good looks. Elven beauty was a warm thing, lit from within, the kind that flushed and laughed and turned its face towards the sun. This was the other kind. It was the beauty of marble in a cold gallery, or of a blade laid out on velvet, flawless and unapproachable. Arian’s face was all severe, clean angles, with a jaw that might have been cut from the obsidian around him and cheekbones a court painter would have wept to be allowed near. His eyes were so dark there was no telling iris from pupil. It was, Caleb thought with considerable annoyance, a face entirely wasted on its owner, who plainly neither knew what he had nor cared.

The candles in their iron sconces guttered as he passed, each flame shrinking down into its wick like a child ducking under the bedclothes at a tread on the stair, and the air cooled a degree at a time.

He crossed the hall without hurrying, as a tide comes in, and stopped an arm’s length away. It was near enough that Caleb had to tip his head back to meet Arian’s eyes, and Caleb resented that immediately and with his whole heart.

And now, at last, Caleb felt what the stories spoke of. It was a pull, low and steady and without malice, like the tug of deep water at the end of a pier, or a compass needle turning helplessly to the north. Some quiet, treacherous part of him wanted to take one step nearer. He did not. He set his heels into the black stone as his sister Matilda had taught him to set them against a charge, and stayed where he was, and hoped devoutly that it did not show.

People generally looked at Caleb’s hair, or his famous smile, or the headlines that trailed behind him like a train. Arian studied his face the way a scholar studies a difficult text. His gaze went down to Caleb’s hands, still clasped behind his back, and then to the faint shimmer along his jaw where the Star-Grace lay nearest the skin. Caleb knew with a jolt that the light there was dimmer than it had been that morning.

If Arian’s face had an opinion on the matter, it kept it. Yet under that dark regard the faint gold along Caleb’s jaw brightened for a moment, as a candle does when a door opens somewhere far down a passage, and then lay quiet again.

“Your Royal Highness.” The voice was low and even, with the roll of the Coast running under the Alorian vowels like a current under still water.

“Sovereign.” Caleb inclined his head as far as protocol required for a foreign head of state, and not one hair further. “I would offer you my hand, but I have been reliably informed that your people bite.”

“Only when invited.”

He said it so flatly that a full second passed before Caleb understood it had been a joke. By then Arian had moved on.

“You know why you are here.”

“I have formed a working hypothesis.” Caleb let the words take their time. “It concerns my family, a vineyard I have never set foot in, and a considerable number of people who would very much like me to stop asking questions. Oh, and a glass of wine, drugged, which I consider the greatest crime of the lot, since it was an exceptional vintage and deserved a far kinder end.”

He had meant it lightly. It emerged with rather more feeling than he had intended, and Arian’s eyes dropped for an instant to his throat. To his pulse, Caleb understood, and his face went hot, for naturally his own pulse was reading out his every feeling to the room, like a herald at a banquet announcing guests nobody had invited.

“I know you were not at the vineyard.”

Caleb opened his mouth, found nothing in it, and closed it again.

He had spent the rest of that flight bracing for this room, and had prepared answers for accusation and for contempt, for a cell, a chain, a slow and inventive death, and not a single word for being *believed*.

“You know,” he repeated.

“Yes.”

“Then —” His voice had started to climb. He caught it and hauled it back down. “Then with the greatest respect, Sovereign, what in the Sun’s holy name am I doing here?”

Arian regarded him for a long moment.

“They sent you,” he answered, and seemed to consider that a full explanation.

The grey-suited vampire shifted his weight. Arian did not look at him.

“Your Royal Seal.” Arian held out his hand.

\* \* \*

Caleb’s fingers went to his breast pocket before he realised they were moving.

The seal had lived there since his coming-of-age, a medallion of sun-forged silver the size of his palm, with the crest of the Solar House on one face and his own name on the other. It was always warm to the touch, as a sleeping animal is warm. It was his passport and his immunity and his signature. With it he could walk into any embassy of the Dominion and be known. Without it he was a beautifully dressed elf with no name at all.

He drew it out reluctantly. It lay in his palm, glowing a soft and living gold, the brightest thing in the hall.

“Do try not to scratch it. The scribes of the Citadel are glacial in the matter of replacements. I should be waiting six months at the very least, in queues I can only describe as unspeakable.”

Across the hall, the fae’s copper coin began to sing, a thin, high, insistent note that did not stop, like a wet fingertip drawn around the rim of a glass. The fae abandoned her idle play and held the coin up between two fingers, head tilted, delighted.

“Oh, it’s *singing*, Sovereign.” She savoured the words like sugared almonds. “And ever so loudly.”

Caleb glanced at her and back down at the seal. The seal was worth a king’s ransom, or at any rate a prince’s. Perhaps her coin had an eye for valuables. He let it fall into Arian’s waiting palm.

Arian’s fingers closed over it.

For a moment nothing happened. Then the air around his fist changed. It thickened and darkened, and every shadow in the hall leaned in towards him to see. The seal’s light flared gold between his knuckles, bright enough that Caleb had to narrow his eyes, and there came from it a sound he would remember long afterwards, a thin and rising keen, like a living thing in terror.

Then the keening stopped, and the light died with it.

Arian opened his hand. What was left of the seal ran from between his fingers in a slow, bright thread and pattered onto the black floor, where it hissed and cooled and dulled to the colour of a winter sky. The palm beneath was red and blistered, as if he had closed his hand on a burning coal, and he did not so much as glance at it. The fae’s coin fell silent in the middle of its note.

Caleb looked down at the grey pool at his feet.

He had known since he woke that he was not going home. The drugged wine, the decree, the crew who would not meet his eye, and Paul. Yet he had known it only as a child knows the ending of a tale before the last page is turned, and now he had watched the last thing in the world that bore his name run molten across a stranger’s floor, and his heart turned over painfully in the silence of that hall.

He could not look at Arian yet. His eyes went to the fae instead, and then to the grey-suited vampire, who had barely glanced at Caleb until now. The vampire was staring at him openly, at Caleb’s face and hands and the last of the gold along his jaw, and the colour had drained from his own cheeks.

“Well,” Caleb said to the floor, when he trusted his voice to hold. “Eleven years of absolute sovereignty, Don Conti, and it seems no one has ever shown you how a seal is used. It is the wax one is meant to melt, you understand, not the seal itself. How delightfully unrefined of you.”

\* \* \*

“Enzo.”

The grey-suited vampire came forward. He carried a casket of dark oiled wood, carved with the square, angular runes Caleb knew from the dwarven vault-banks of the Marches, and he opened it with a reluctance so plain it might have been a third person in the conversation.

“Sovereign.” Enzo kept his voice low. “Once more, I beg you. Consider what that ring will say to every house on the Coast —”

“I have considered it.”

Enzo’s jaw set. He held the casket out at the full length of his arm and said nothing more.

From it Arian lifted a ring. It was a plain band of black iron, heavier than it had any right to look, with a single line of dwarven runes cut around its face. A tiny closed crescent was engraved into the iron, the crest of House Conti, and beside it sat an empty setting where a stone was meant to go and had not yet arrived. To Caleb’s eye it was a thing fit for a favourite hunting hound.

“Your hand.”

“I find I am rather attached to it.”

Arian waited. It became clear that he was prepared to wait for the rest of the night, and quite possibly the century.

Caleb held out his left hand, palm down, as he might to a courtier he disliked who was angling to kiss it.

Arian took his wrist.

His grip was cool. Caleb had expected cold, and it was only cool, like stone that has lain a long while in shade. He turned Caleb’s hand over with a care that was almost clinical and slid the iron band onto his ring finger. It was too large. Then the runes met his skin and it was not. A thread of light ran once around them, quick as a minnow turning in a shallow stream, and the metal drew in, snug and painless, until it sat on his finger as if forged for no other hand.

And under Arian’s fingers, where they rested against the inside of his wrist, Caleb felt a pulse.

The beat was faint and slow, and unmistakable. He had been taught to heal by counting heartbeats before he had been taught to read. He went still.

Vampires had no pulse. It was the second thing the temple tutors taught about them, straight after *they burn in the light*. *Their hearts do not beat.*

It must be his own, then. His own blood, racing and loud, coming back to him through the contact. That was all it could be.

Arian let go, and the feeling went with him.

“Wear it. Do not take it off.”

Caleb looked at the black iron on his finger, at the Conti crest and the empty setting, and humiliation arrived all at once, scalding and complete. The seal of his own house was slag on the floor, and in its place he had been given a collar. Property, duly marked.

“How exceedingly thoughtful.” He was rather proud of how bored he sounded. “Is there to be a matching lead, or shall I expect that with the morning post?”

Nobody laughed this time. The sentinel at the door was staring at the ring. So was the fae, who had gone motionless on the arm of her chair with her lips parted, like someone who had just been handed the finest secret of her long, long life. Enzo had turned his face away altogether.

Caleb saw none of it. He was looking at Arian, and Arian was looking back at him with the same unfathomable stillness.

“You will have rooms in the east wing. Ask for whatever you require. You will not leave the fortress.”

“And should I try?”

“You will not get far.”

“How reassuring. Tell me, Sovereign, is that meant as concern for my welfare, or as a threat?”

Arian looked at him a moment longer.

“Yes,” he said, and turned away.

\* \* \*

The sentinel walked him out, with Paul silent at his back. Caleb went with his chin up and his shoulders square, just as Matilda had drilled into him on a hundred mornings in the practice yard, and his left hand closed into a fist at his side, the iron on it warm now and heavy.

At the threshold he heard Arian speak behind him, low, to Enzo, in a voice not meant to carry. But the Solar House had always bred for keen ears.

“— to the vault-masons at Durnhollow. Tonight. Tell them I require glass.”

“Glass, Sovereign?”

“Tonight, Zio.”

The great doors swung shut on the rest.

Caleb walked the long black corridor to the east wing, counting the violet lamps as he passed beneath them. At the third he held his hand up to the glass, out of pure stubbornness, and it gave him about as much warmth as a lamp painted in the margin of a book. He counted them so that he would not think about the pulse under Arian’s fingers, or the way Arian had looked at his hands, or the fact that the Sovereign of Vesperia believed him innocent and had kept him anyway, or the signet he had lent his brother weeks ago, laughing.

He thought instead about the queue at the Citadel’s seal office, which wound three times around the Hall of Petitions and moved at the pace of a tortoise in mourning, and how he would never again have to stand in it. Somewhere between the eleventh lamp and the twelfth, he caught himself laughing, quietly and not quite steadily. The sentinel kept his eyes ahead. Paul, at his back, said nothing.

\* \* \*

Behind him, in the emptied hall, the shadows that had leaned in to watch drew back one by one and went home to the columns, and the Sovereign of Vesperia stood over a pool of cooling silver with his burned hand open at his side, and did not move for a long time.
