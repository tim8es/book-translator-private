## Chapter 1: Prologue - The Long Eclipse

*Every child from the Jötun North to the shores of the Middle Sea knows how the story ends. Almost no one knows how it began.*

— *A History of the Long Eclipse*, by Aurèle Vasseur, historian to the Court of Lys

\* \* \*

On the day the sun went out, Livia Conti walked beneath the open sky at last, and she did not look up once.

She had meant to. All her long life she had heard the elders describe it, the day that came but once in twenty years, when the great light faltered and the children of the Umbral houses might stand in the world as other people stood in it. Every twenty years the Sovereign’s delegation had gone to meet it, and every twenty years she had stayed behind to keep the Sanctuary. This time the Sovereign had brought his boy, and asked her to come too. As a girl she had imagined that she would spend it with her face turned upward like a flower. Instead she spent it with her face to the ground, reading the ruins of Old Meridian for the Sovereign’s son.

The Summit had come apart an hour after noon.

When the first cry rang out, Livia had been in the great silk tent where the Vesperian delegation received its guests, pouring blood-wine for a dwarven envoy who had talked of nothing but tariffs since the eclipse began. A second cry followed, and then the high, bright scream of silver leaving a sheath. By the time she reached the fountain court the Sovereign lay on the flagstones with three blades in him, his guards dead around him. The men who had done it had vanished into the mist. Before that night was out, her husband Enzo would swear that he knew whose men they were. He would swear it for twenty years, but never once be able to prove it.

Enzo had been kneeling in the blood. He looked up at her with a face she had never seen him wear and said one word.

*Where?*

The boy. He had been at his father’s side not a quarter of an hour since, bored and scowling in his first formal coat, twelve years old and furious at being made to stand still.

Now he was nowhere to be found.

\* \* \*

Enzo had the delegation to hold and the Council to face and a dead Sovereign to keep from becoming a dead dynasty, and so it was Livia who went into the ruins.

Old Meridian had been a city once, before the realms were sundered, and it had never quite agreed to stop being one. Its streets still ran where they had always run, paved in a white stone the centuries had worn to the colour of old teeth. Its arches still stood, though nothing passed beneath them now but fog. The mist of the Marches lay over all of it, thick and cool and faintly luminous, and on this day of all days it glowed with a light that belonged to no hour at all.

The sun had become a black coin rimmed with white fire. Everything beneath it had turned the colour of a pearl held up to a candle. The elves in their silk tents across the valley had gone quiet as their great crystals dimmed, and for this one day the crystals’ glare could not reach her. The whole valley lay in one shadow, so there was none to hide in and nothing to burn her.

She had never been so frightened.

He was bleeding. That much she knew within a hundred paces of the fountain court, and she knew it as any mother knows her own child’s cry through a wall. She had not borne him. She had held him all the same, the night his mother died giving him to the world, and she had held him ever since, most of all on the nights he was too proud to ask. She would have known his blood in a sea of it.

It was on the stones of a narrow street that ran uphill from the old market, and then on the base of a broken column where he must have leaned, and then on a doorframe, a smear the size of a child’s palm, and her own hands shook so badly she had to close them.

There was sun-forged silver in it. She could smell it, bright and sour, under the iron. A blade forged under Aloria’s noon leaves a wound that does not close. She had seen grown men of the Umbral houses die of such a cut in the time it took to call a physician, and he was twelve.

She ran.

\* \* \*

The trail ended at a glasshouse.

It stood at the top of the hill, on the edge of what had once been a garden and was now only a wilderness of briars. It must have been beautiful when the city was young. Its ribs were iron, fine as the bones of a bird, and they rose into a dome that had once been all glass. Most of the panes were gone now. Those that remained were clouded and cracked and hung at angles, and the mist drifted in and out through the gaps like a tenant.

The door stood open. There was blood on the threshold.

Livia went in.

It was warmer inside, and that was wrong. The air under that broken dome was soft and still and faintly golden, a summer afternoon someone had left there and forgotten, and the clouded panes that still clung to the ribs were warm as new bread, under a sun that had given no warmth since noon. The briars grew thinner here. Among them, pale against the dark soil, a few old rose bushes had put out new leaves, tender and absurd, the only living green she had seen in the whole of Old Meridian.

He was lying in the middle of them.

She was on her knees beside him before she knew she had crossed the floor. He lay on his side in the soft earth with his knees drawn up, as he had slept when he was little, and his first formal coat was black and wet and torn open at the side where the blade had gone in. There was so much blood around him that the soil had turned to mud.

She pulled the torn coat aside, dreading what she would find, and found nothing. The wound was closed. Where the silver had gone in there was only smooth skin and a thin seam of gold light that faded as she watched, like the last line of a sunset going under the sea.

She stared at it. No physician of the Umbral houses could close such a wound, and no magic she had ever heard of could do it.

Then she saw the cloak.

It was wrapped around his shoulders and tucked beneath his chin with tender care, and it was not his. It was a child’s cloak, too small for him, of pale grey wool so fine it was nearly silk, and it was stitched all through with silver thread that caught the light of the eclipse and held it. At the collar, worked in gold, was a sunburst.

Livia knew that sunburst. It was known in every realm under the sun.

It was the crest of the Solar House.

\* \* \*

She had scarcely taken it in when she heard the footsteps.

They were light and quick and receding. She turned her head, and through the broken glass on the far side of the dome she saw a figure running away from her into the mist.

It was a child, younger than her boy, running with his arms pumping and his elbows out, as children run before anyone has taught them to run like gentlemen. His hair was a warm dark brown, but a faint golden glow clung about him all the same, and it moved through the mist like a lamp being carried away. He ran down the hill towards the elven tents, where she could hear voices now, many voices, calling a name the mist would not let her catch.

At the edge of the briars he stopped, and looked back.

She could not see his face. He was too far away, and the mist was too thick. She saw only that he stood a moment, turned towards the glasshouse, unwilling to go until he was sure someone had found her boy. Then the voices called again, closer, and he turned and ran, and the mist closed over him.

It never occurred to her to follow. She had her boy.

\* \* \*

He woke as she was lifting him.

His eyes opened, dark and unfocused, and his hand came up and closed hard on the cloak at his throat, in case she meant to take it.

“Hush,” she murmured. “Hush, *tesoro*. It is Zia. I have you.”

He looked at her for a long moment. Then his gaze went past her, over her shoulder, to the empty place among the roses where he had lain.

“He was here.”

“Who was here?”

“He was warm.” His voice was thin as thread. His hand did not let go of the cloak. “He wasn’t afraid of me. He should have been afraid of me, Zia, I told him what I was, and he wasn’t, he just put his hands on the hurt and it got warm and he stayed —” He stopped. His breath caught. “Where did he go?”

“Home,” said Livia, because she did not know what else to say.

The boy closed his eyes.

She carried him out of the glasshouse and down the long white streets of Old Meridian, under the black sun, with his head against her shoulder and the little grey cloak still clutched at his throat. He said nothing more, and she thought he had gone back to sleep.

Halfway down the hill, he whispered into her collar, “Help me find him.”

She had no answer to give him.

\* \* \*

The sun returned. It came back first as a thread of white fire along the rim of the black coin, and the thread swelled to a sickle and the sickle to a blaze, and every shadow in Old Meridian drew itself back out of the pearl and lay down again where it belonged. The elves went home to their light. In the Vesperian tent Enzo sat for an hour with his head in his hands, and then he rose, because a dynasty does not wait upon grief.

Livia put the boy to bed herself. She sat beside him in the dark, as she had sat on a thousand nights before, and rested her hand upon his chest to feel him breathe.

Beneath her palm, faint and slow and impossible, his heart was beating.

The children of the Umbral houses have no heartbeat, and every one of them knows it. It is the first thing they are taught about themselves.

Livia sat motionless in the dark for a long time, with her hand over the heart of a child who had been healed by the Solar House, and she told no one. She did not tell Enzo, or the Council, or, for many years, the boy himself.

When at last he let go of the little silver cloak, she folded it with great care and put it away somewhere safe. Then, in the quietest way she knew, she began to help him look.
