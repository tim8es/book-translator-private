## Chapter 8: Chapter Seven - The Eclipse Transit

*A promise of marriage sealed before a Master Shaper of the Holds, both parties being present and each consenting aloud, shall bind them as iron binds. No crown, council or court of any realm shall have power to loose it. Only the two who made it may unmake it, and only together.*

— The Bond-Law of the Deep Holds, Article the Twelfth, in the Alorian translation

\* \* \*

*The Eclipse Marches.*

The Radiant Guard had been standing in the snow for five weeks, and Matilda had not once let them see her cold.

It was simply so, like the order of march or the weight of her sword. Every morning she broke the skin of ice on her washing water with the heel of her hand and washed in it. Every morning she buckled on her white mail, which had spent the night drinking the cold like a cellar wall and gave it back to her at the throat and wrists, and walked out to the edge of the camp at first light in her white cloak, and stood there, and looked south towards the Coast. The entire Radiant Guard looked at her looking. From the cooks at the fire pits to the scouts coming in off the high passes with frost in their beards, they knew that as long as she was standing there, nothing had yet gone wrong.

There were two toes on her left foot that she had not felt since the second week. She had mentioned them to no one. She set her jaw against the cold each morning until it ached at the hinges, and each evening, when she let it go, the ache went down into her neck and lay there all night like a stone. None of this was of the least importance.

She had been doing it for thirty-six days. She had counted.

The camp lay on the Alorian side of the Pas de la Dame, in a high white bowl of the mountains where the road from the north came down between two shoulders of rock and went on south into the mist. Beyond the mist, a day’s hard march away, the ground began to fall towards the Vesperian foothills, and beyond that, under the ash, lay the Coast. Matilda could not see it. Nobody could. But on clear mornings, when the wind had scoured the valleys clean and the air was so cold it rang, there was a smudge on the southern horizon like a thumbprint on glass, darker than the dark, and she looked at that.

Her captains thought she was watching for the enemy. She let them think it.

This morning the wind had not scoured anything. The mist lay in the valley below the camp as thick as milk, and the snow was coming down again out of a sky the colour of pewter, in fat slow flakes that settled on the shoulders of her cloak and did not melt. The tent ropes creaked with frost. Down the lines a farrier was at work on a shoe, and the ring of his hammer came up to her flattened and soft, like a sound heard from under a quilt. Along the tent lines the Guard’s lanterns burned on their poles, each a second-yield stone in an iron cage, and under every one the snow had given up trying and left a round of wet black ground, as if the light had sat there all night to keep it warm. Her second, Sir Hugh Ashby, came up through all of it at a jog, with his breath smoking and a sheaf of papers under his arm and a face like unwelcome news.

“Report,” ordered Matilda.

“The scouts are back from the Col de Serre, Paladin.”

“And?”

Ashby hesitated. He had served under her for nine years and she had never once seen him hesitate before.

“The Vesperian post is still snowed in,” he finally spoke, sounding reluctant and unsure and very unlike him. “Has been all week. But there’s a man there now. One of theirs. A vampire, on a mule. Came up the high road in the night on his own and has been digging ever since.” Ashby examined the toes of his boots. “With a shovel, Paladin.”

Matilda looked at him. “A shovel,” she repeated flatly.

“Yes, Paladin. The scouts watched him through most of a night. He went in under the post’s broken roof before dawn and came out again at dusk. He did not appear to be armed. He was, they said, making very good progress.” Ashby held out the top paper of his sheaf at arm’s length. “And this came up with the supply train from Lutèce last night. The quartermaster thought you might wish to see it.”

It was the *Lantern*.

Matilda did not read the *Lantern*, or not where anyone could see her. She considered it beneath the notice of a serving officer, and she had said so, often and at volume, at a great many family dinners, and every time Caleb had waited until she had quite finished and then read her the most scandalous item aloud, eyes enormous over the top of the sheet, in a voice of solemn wonder, until she had to leave the table with her napkin pressed to her mouth. She took it now because she had seen the name on the front of it before Ashby had finished unfolding it.

*THE PRINCE SPEAKS.*

She read it standing in the snow at the edge of the camp, with the flakes settling on the paper and melting into the ink, so that the letters began to soften and bleed at their edges under her thumbs.

It was his voice, every word of it, and for a moment she could not see the page. She could hear the little pause he always left before the best part, and the lift at the end of a sentence when he was pleased with it, and she had to stop and look out at the mist until the page came back.

Below that, in the correspondent’s own hand, was an account of a morning before the Council of Nine, and a war that had not been started over a snowdrift, because the Solar prince had stood up in the middle of the Vesperian court and explained what his sister meant by standing her soldiers on her own side of a line.

*That is not a threat, Sovereign,* the prince was reported to have said. *That is a letter. It is the only kind of letter the High Paladin has ever known how to write.*

Matilda stood motionless in the snow.

Every night she had lain on her camp bed under three blankets and her cloak, listening to the canvas crack in the wind, and talked to him in the dark, and he had never once answered.

Now the dark had answered her in her brother’s voice.

Ashby, who knew her well, took two careful steps backwards, and then another two, and discovered that the snow on the far side of the command tent required his immediate attention.

It began under her breastbone, in the place where she had kept the cold and the counting and the nights, and it came up through her before she could set her jaw against it, into her shoulders and then her breath, which went out of her in a great white gust, and the paper shook in her hand, and the gold she had kept down under her skin for five weeks came up all at once, so that the falling flakes went to water before they could land on her, and the snow on her lashes melted and ran, and the cold air burned all the way down into her chest, and still it would not stop.

The High Paladin of Aloria stood alone at the edge of her camp with a fae broadsheet in her hand and laughed until she had to put her other hand over her eyes.

When at last she took it away, her palm was wet. She told herself it was the snow.

\* \* \*

Eleanor came up the north road that afternoon in a closed carriage from the yacht field at Chalmont, with a letter in her glove and four boxes of ledgers strapped to the roof.

Eleanor never sent word ahead. The carriage came out of the mist with its horses steaming and its lacquer crusted grey with frozen slush to the axles, and drew up at the gate. Eleanor arrived at the camp like weather, in a travelling cloak of dark blue wool with a collar of white fox, and stepped down into the snow as if it had been laid there for her convenience. The guards at the gate, who had faced down Vesperian raiders and lycan wolf packs and on one memorable occasion a frost giant who had lost his way, looked at the Arch-Chancellor of Aloria and fell back without a word to let her through.

She found Matilda in the command tent, among the maps weighted down with stones and the smell of wet wool and lamp oil. The lamp on the tent pole brightened as she came in, as lamps had brightened for Eleanor since she was in the nursery, out of what Caleb always maintained was pure fright. She dismissed the aides with a glance, and waited until the flap had fallen closed behind the last of them. Then she looked at her sister, once, from her boots to her hair. She hooked the leg of the brazier with the toe of her boot and drew it a foot nearer to where Matilda stood, and drew off her gloves, and took out the letter, and held it out.

“It came to the Chancellery two days ago. By the Coast’s own courier, under the Consigliere’s seal, with the Treasury’s bills. It was addressed to all three of us. I thought you should read it before anyone else did.” She glanced at the tent flap, which was shut, and lowered her voice all the same. “I have not shown Father.”

Matilda took it.

*My dearest Matilda, my dearest Eleanor, my dearest Cecily.*

It was his hand. The long loops, the capitals that leaned back as though they were laughing at a private joke, the ink gone thin at the end of a line where he had forgotten to dip his pen because he was in a hurry to get to the next thing. She put her thumb on her own name and left it there a moment.

It was not long. He was well, he wrote, and in the light, and eating a great deal, and he had been given a garden, whatever that meant, and of the Sovereign of Vesperia there was not one word.

At the bottom there was a column of figures.

*Eleanor will wish to know how I am managing my household, so I enclose my accounts for the month, which she may audit as severely as she likes.*

“He has never kept accounts in his life,” said Matilda.

“No.” Eleanor came and stood at her elbow, and laid one long finger on the column. “Look at the pence.”

Matilda looked at the pence. “Tell me.”

“A is one. B is two. I taught it him the winter he was six, to make him do his sums, and he has been sending me rude notes in it ever since.” Her finger went down the column. “Nineteen. One. Six. Five.”

SAFE. DO NOT COME.

Matilda set the letter down on the camp table gently, and put both hands flat either side of it, and bent her head. The canvas top gave a little under her weight. Her hands were trembling, and she let them, and the lamp threw their shadows across the paper so that they seemed to be holding it down.

“He is safe,” she said, to the table.

“He says so.”

“He would not say so if it were not true. Not to you.” Her voice was not steady. There was nobody there but Eleanor, so she did not try to make it. “And he says do not come.”

“Yes.”

“He knows I would. He knows me. He knows that if he had asked, I would have taken every banner in this camp across that line tonight and burned the Coast to the waterline to get him back, and damn the Accord.” She straightened. “And he has told me not to. Why?”

Eleanor considered her. “I asked him two questions. In the pence of my complaint to the Sovereign. I asked if he was safe. And I asked if he was willing.” She touched the letter again. “He answered the first. He did not answer the second.”

The brazier ticked. Outside, somebody was shouting about mules.

“He did not say no,” said Matilda slowly.

“He did not say anything.”

“Caleb has never in his life been unable to say something.”

“No,” said Eleanor. Her face was composed. Only her fingers, resting on the edge of the letter, had gone white. “I had noticed that.”

They stood on either side of the letter. For five weeks Matilda had imagined him in irons, in a cell cut into black rock with water running down the walls, thin and frightened and calling for her. On the worst nights she had imagined him dead, and had got up the next morning and broken the ice on her basin and gone out to stand in the snow regardless. Not one of those nights had shown her this, a letter in his own laughing hand that answered one question and left the other lying open on the table between them.

“The *Lantern* says he stood up in front of the whole Council of Nine,” she said at last, “and told them what I meant.”

“I read it.”

“He wore a Conti ring. They say it is a betrothal ring. They say the Sovereign kissed him in front of his whole court.” She heard her own voice harden. “If that creature has laid a hand on him —”

“Then he would have told me,” interrupted Eleanor. “In the pence. He would have found a way.” She drew a long breath. “He told the fae he was not afraid. Did you see that? At the very bottom, in the correspondent’s note. The correspondent asked him whether he was afraid of the Sovereign, and he said *not any longer*.”

Matilda picked up the *Lantern* from the camp bed, stiff now and cockled where the snow had dried on it, and found the line.

“Not any longer. Then once he was.”

“Yes.”

Once, in that place, her brother had been afraid, and she had been standing here in the snow looking at a thumbprint on the horizon, and he had been afraid without her.

She put the broadsheet down.

“Does Cecily know?”

“I sent her a note last night. Only that he is well, and has written, and says he is safe. I did not tell her the rest.” Eleanor hesitated. “She has not left the laboratory in five weeks, Tilda. Not for a meal, not for a bath, not to sleep. Her people say she is making something for Andrew. She will not tell anyone what it is. When I went down to see her she would not let me past the outer door.”

Matilda looked at her.

For a long time they had spoken of Andrew to each other only in the voice they used in public, the careful voice of three younger sisters speaking of the heir. They had learned it as children, along with the distance that went with it, and that it was safer not to cross. Caleb had never learned it. He had walked straight across that distance as if it were not there, and been gathered up on the other side of it, and been loved.

“What else have you brought me? You did not come all the way up the north road in the snow to show me a letter you could have sent by courier.”

Eleanor smiled then, and it was the smile that foreign ministers had learned to dread from across a treaty table.

“Four boxes of ledgers. Sit down, Tilda.”

\* \* \*

It took her most of the afternoon, and Matilda understood about half of it. She understood men and horses and weather and ground, and when a line would hold and when it would break, and she had always been content to leave the rest to Eleanor.

The ledgers had brought the Chancellery with them, ink and dust and the cold dry smell of a strongroom, and every so often a slab of snow let go of the canvas overhead and slid down it in a soft grey shadow, with a hush like a page being turned. Eleanor worked as she always had, with her sleeves turned back two neat folds and a pencil behind her ear that she never once used, running one finger down column after column so fast that Matilda could not follow it, and stopping dead, now and then, on a single figure, like a pointer in the heather.

“Andrew’s household accounts are in faultless order. These are not in his name. They are not in anyone’s name.” She laid her finger on a column. “Money comes up out of the Underglow refineries every week, a little at a time, the way water comes up through a floor. It goes south through three Lowmere houses and an exchange broker in Lutèce, and it comes to rest in the Seventh Chamber of Durnhollow, which keeps its books in Holdish and does not answer my letters.”

“It could be anyone’s.”

“It could,” Eleanor agreed. “It is not.” She turned a page. “Now look at this. Van Houten and Daughters, of Lowmere. Twelve crates of refinery machinery, by air, from the Citadel wharves to the Marches. The consignor’s name is blank. The carriage was paid out of the same account.”

“Refinery machinery. To the Marches. There are no refineries in the Marches.”

“No.”

“Then what is in the crates?”

“I do not know.” Eleanor closed the ledger. “But I know that they left the Citadel four days ago, and that the Lowmere yacht that carried them set down at a dwarven vault-station at the Kessel Pass, and that four nights before that, according to every informant in the Marches, a Solar yacht with no colours set down at the same place in the dark.”

Matilda went still. “My scouts saw it,” she said slowly. “That yacht. Ashby logged it. I have the report. A light hull, Citadel-built, fast, no markings. It came in from the north after midnight and lifted again before dawn.” She looked up. “I thought it was a smuggler.”

“And on the same night,” said Eleanor, “the Crown Prince’s household drew three hundred crowns from the Treasury for the expenses of the Crown Prince’s journey to the Eclipse Marches, to inspect the positions of the Radiant Guard.” She laid down a single sheet of paper with the Treasury’s own seal on it, and turned it round, and pushed it across the table. “Did he come to inspect your positions, Tilda?”

Matilda looked at the paper, and went on looking.

She had read Ashby’s report by this same lamp, in his neat sloping hand, and set her initials at the foot of it. Some part of her had been waiting all that week for the heir’s pennant on the north road. It never came, and she had been glad of it, and had not asked herself why.

“No. He did not come.”

The lamp hissed. Outside, the snow had thickened, and the light through the canvas had gone the colour of old slate, and the camp had begun the long business of settling for the night, with the sound of horses being walked and fires being fed and men calling to one another across the lines.

“We cannot prove it,” said Eleanor at last. “Not one line of it. Every ledger I have could be explained away by a clever clerk in an afternoon. And if we tried, and failed, he would know that we had tried. And Father would take his side, as Father always has. And you would lose the Guard, and I would lose the Treasury, and Cecily…” She stopped.

“I know.”

“He sent Caleb in his place, Tilda.” Eleanor’s voice went lower instead of louder, and colder, and held steady. “I do not know how. I do not know why. But the signet was Caleb’s, and Caleb was never at the vineyard, and Andrew must have known it, and Father, and Mother. They put him on a yacht in the middle of the night, asleep, with Mother’s own chamberlain seeing him aboard, and told the whole world he had gone on a pilgrimage. Andrew stood beside Father in the Throne Hall the next morning and never said one word.”

There was nothing to say that both of them did not already know. Above them on the tent pole the lamp flame had drawn itself up thin and white, and stood so without a flicker, and Matilda’s hands had closed on the edge of the table, and for the moment she could not persuade them to open.

“Mother sits in Caleb’s rooms,” said Eleanor, after a moment, in a different voice. “Did you know that? Every night, after the court has gone to bed. The maids say she does not light the lamps. She just sits there in the dark, in her white, on the end of his bed. They say she has taken to sleeping there.”

Matilda closed her eyes.

She could see it, the shutters down against the white noon of the spires, and his books lying open on every surface as he always left them, with their spines cracked and his pencil marks in the margins, and the scent he wore gone faint in the curtains by now and fainter every night, and in the middle of all of it, in the dark, their mother in her mourning white, lying down on the end of his bed like a girl. Their mother, who had sent her own chamberlain to carry him sleeping to the yacht.

“What do we do?”

Eleanor gathered her ledgers back into their boxes one by one and squared the corners, all but the thinnest, which she left on the camp table with her hand laid flat upon it.

“Copies. Keep them somewhere Andrew would never think to look.”

Matilda looked at the thin ledger, and then at her sister, and nodded once.

“You stand in the snow,” said Eleanor, drawing on her first glove, “where he can see you. And I follow the money. Then we wait.” She drew on the second, and fastened it at the wrist. “He told us not to come, Tilda. He has never once in his life told either of us what to do. I think we should do him the courtesy of believing he has a reason.”

At the flap of the tent she stopped, and turned, and put her gloved hand for a moment flat against Matilda’s cheek.

“You are frozen.”

“I am not.”

“No,” said Eleanor. “Of course not.” And she went out into the dark, and behind her the lamp on the tent pole sank back to its ordinary yellow with what Matilda could only call relief.

\* \* \*

That night, when the camp was asleep and the only sound was the snow sifting down on the canvas like flour through a sieve, the High Paladin of Aloria sat alone in her tent with a lamp and a pen and wrote her report for the day, in the plain square hand that the Guard’s clerks had long since learned to read.

*Positions unchanged. Weather poor. The Vesperian post at the Col de Serre remains snowed in. One Vesperian observed digging, unarmed, and making good progress. No movement across the line from either side. The Arch-Chancellor visited the camp and departed after dark. Morale good.*

She looked at it for a while.

Then, because there was nobody in the world to see her, she put the pen down and held both her hands to the lamp, and the flame bowed down into her cupped palms as if it had been waiting all day to be asked. She held them there until the feeling came back into her fingers in a long, slow, burning ache, and her eyes stung with it, and she did not take them away.

When she could hold the pen again, she added a line at the bottom, in the same plain hand, which no clerk of the Guard ever read, because she cut it off with her knife before she sealed the report, and folded it twice and twice again, and put it inside her mail, against her heart.

*He says do not come. So I shall stand guard here.*

\* \* \*

The betrothal had to be sealed at Durnhollow. Caleb had known that before Enzo told him. When he first read the treatise on the bond-law he had thought it a shame that he would never need to know any of it, and turned the page.

“A betrothal made before the Council binds the Council,” said Enzo, in the study among its ranks of candles, with the treatise open on the desk between them and his long finger resting on the page. “Elsewhere, a promise made to a prince held under the Accord may be found to be no promise at all.” He turned the page. “Under the bond-law of the Holds, no crown may loose it.”

“Only the two who made it,” finished Caleb, “and only together.”

Enzo looked up at him over the book.

“I read it on my eleventh day. I was very bored.”

Enzo’s finger went down the page by one line, and rested there.

Caleb knew.

*Both parties being present, and each consenting aloud.*

Arian stood by the fire he could not feel, with his back to the room and his hands clasped behind him, and had not said a word since Caleb came in. The fire had been built high, and it roared and snapped in the grate, and its light ran up and down the long black coat and found nothing in it to warm.

“And if I do not consent?” asked Caleb. “Aloud. Before this Master Shaper of yours, in Durnhollow, with the whole of the Holds looking on.”

Arian turned round. “Then it is not sealed.”

“And then?”

Arian said nothing.

Along the desk every candle flame had leaned the same way, towards the hearth where he stood, like children leaning in to hear the end of a tale, and there they stayed.

Caleb thought of the folded paper in the lining of his coat, beside his heart, with its two words in Eleanor’s pence. Without meaning to, he put his hand there, and felt the stiff edge of it through the silk.

“When do we leave?”

\* \* \*

They left four nights later, in four black carriages, with a dozen lycans riding the running boards and the roofs and the whole of Castel Nerezza turned out in the great courtyard to see them go.

Caleb had expected a quiet departure by some side gate, in the small hours, as befitted a hostage being carted across the continent to have his captivity notarised. Instead there were torches, dozens of them, pouring pitch smoke up into the violet dark and throwing long red light across the flagstones, and the lycan guard drawn up in two long lines in their Conti black, and clerks and housemaids and cooks and grooms crowding the galleries above. The air was sharp with cold and resin and the hot metal smell of the carriages, which stood humming at the foot of the stair like great black beasts asleep. When he came down the great stair in his travelling cloak, a murmur went round the courtyard that it took him a moment to recognise as approval.

Vesna was waiting at the foot of the stair with a basket.

“Scones on top. Pie under. Tea at the bottom, the Wildmarch, and His Highness will drink it every morning and not make that face.” She pushed the basket into his arms. “I packed it myself. I wouldn’t trust this household to feed a sparrow.”

Then she reached up and settled the collar of his cloak, her rough warm fingers brushing his jaw, and went off into the crowd at a pace that left no room at all for thanks.

Mirek was to ride on the box of Caleb’s own carriage, with a short sword at his hip that he had evidently been practising with, since there was a fresh bandage on his left hand, and had not stopped grinning since morning. Paul was coming too, as the Accord required, in the last carriage with the baggage. When Caleb looked for him in the torchlight he found him already seated there, looking straight ahead at nothing.

Enzo was staying. “Someone must hold the Council,” he had said, and the Council had been noticeably quieter ever since. He stood at the top of the great stair in his dove grey with his hands behind his back, and when Caleb reached the carriage door he came down three steps, which for Enzo was all but an embrace.

“Highness.”

Caleb waited.

“He has not left the Coast in eleven years,” Enzo murmured.

Eleven years was the whole of the reign. Enzo looked past Caleb at the first carriage, where a tall figure in black was standing by the open door, waiting. “Look after him.”

“I am the hostage, Consigliere. I had understood that the looking after was meant to go the other way, and I confess I had been rather looking forward to it.”

“Had you?” Enzo went back up the stair.

\* \* \*

The road went down from Castel Nerezza through the black valleys, in the dark, for most of the first night.

Caleb had imagined, insofar as he had imagined anything, something like a hearse. The carriage was long and low and lacquered black inside and out, and it had no horses. It ran on four great iron wheels, driven by an engine beneath the floor that thrummed softly through the soles of his boots and glowed faintly violet through the slats, and it moved so smoothly over the broken volcanic road that he could have set a glass of wine on the seat beside him and not spilled a drop. The windows were smoked glass, and the shutters could be drawn over them from inside. The seats were a deep grey velvet so soft that he sank into it to the wrists.

Arian sat across from him, with his back to the direction of travel, and looked out of the window at nothing.

For the first hour neither of them spoke. Caleb had spent seven weeks in a fortress in a mountain, and the fortress had been vast and the garden beautiful, but it had had walls, and now it did not. He sat with his cheek almost against the cool glass and watched the Coast go by, the jagged black valleys and the canyons with embers far below them, breathing red in the dark like coals under a grate, and a village clinging to the side of a ravine with its windows lit violet and its streets full of people going about their business in the endless dusk, and a night market where an old lycan woman was selling lanterns shaped like moons, and a bridge over a gorge so deep that he could not see the bottom of it, only the faint red glow of a slow river of fire moving down there, with the heat of it coming up through the floor of the carriage as they crossed.

He had wanted his whole life to see the world. He had asked, year after year, to be let go on embassies, and on the Radiant Guard’s summer manoeuvres, and been told that the youngest prince was needed at court, and that Matilda would not take the responsibility. He had once asked to visit Lutèce, and been told that the Court of Lys was no place for a prince of the Solar House, and that he would only get into the broadsheets. He had got into the broadsheets anyway, without leaving his rooms.

And now here he was, seeing it at last, from the window of a vampire’s carriage on the way to his own betrothal.

He laughed. He could not help it.

Arian turned his head.

“Forgive me,” said Caleb. “It only struck me that I have wanted to travel all my life, and nobody would ever let me, and it has taken my being kidnapped, drugged, and sold to a foreign power to arrange it. I was thinking that I ought to write my father a note of thanks.”

Arian looked at him for a moment, and in the dark of the carriage Caleb thought the corner of his mouth moved. He could not have sworn to it.

“Where did you want to go?”

Caleb had not expected to be asked. “Everywhere. Lutèce. The canal cities of Lowmere. The Deep Holds. I wanted to see a giant. I wanted to sail through the Thalassan straits and see the merfolk in the harbour at Nauplion, and eat those little fried fish they sell on the quays, and be sick on the boat home. I wanted to see the fae spring festival, when the whole of Lutèce hangs lanterns from the bridges and nobody sleeps for three days.” He turned the ring on his finger. “And I wanted to see the night. Properly. With stars in it.”

Nothing in Arian moved. “You have never seen the stars.”

“There are none in Aloria. The crystals burn noon at every hour. We have night only by the shutters. I have seen pictures, of course, and orreries, and the painted ceiling in the Hall of Petitions, which is very fine and entirely wrong, I am told. But I have never seen a real one.” He looked out at the ash cloud rolling over the black valleys. “And there are none here either. I did look.”

Arian looked out of the window too, for a while, at the cloud.

“Tomorrow.”

\* \* \*

In Vesperia there was no day to stop for, and the carriages ran on without halting, down the mountain and across the strait on the night ferry and up the long black spine of the Coast through a twilight that never changed. On the ferry Caleb stood at the rail in his cloak for an hour, with the deck lamps burning violet above him and the black water sliding past below, slow and heavy as oil and smelling of salt and sulphur, until Mirek came out to him, with great respect and some anxiety. “The Sovereign says will you come in out of the wind, Highness.”

Towards the end of the second night the road climbed out of the last Vesperian foothills into the high passes of the Marches, and the ash cloud, which had hung over them like a lid for the entire journey, began to thin. Caleb woke from a doze with a crick in his neck and the grey velvet printed on his cheek, and saw that the cloud above the pass had gone ragged at the edges, like old silk, and that through the rents in it a few points of light were shining.

He sat up.

The cloud tore, and drew back, and was gone. And above the high white shoulders of the mountains, from one edge of the world to the other, the sky was full of stars.

Caleb put his hand flat against the cold glass, the way a child does, and looked. His breath clouded the pane at once, and he wiped it clear with his sleeve, impatiently, and it clouded again.

Across the carriage, without a word, Arian leaned forward and let down the glass.

The cold poured in over the sill. It was the cold of the high passes, cleaner and harder than any cold Caleb had known, smelling of snow and stone and pine resin, and it went straight through his cloak. He put his head and shoulders out into it, and with no smoked glass between him and the sky, the stars came clear.

There were so many. He had seen them painted, gold leaf on blue in the Hall of Petitions, a few dozen polite lights in tidy constellations. The real sky was wilder. There were thousands of them, tens of thousands, more than anyone could ever count, scattered across the whole black arch of it like salt spilled on velvet, white and gold and faintly blue, and they trembled. Across the middle ran a band so thick with them that it looked like the Citadel’s noon poured out very thin, and it was so beautiful that Caleb felt it in his chest like grief.

Every night of his life he had lain under the lead-lined dark of the Citadel, with the shutters closed against the crystals and the room as black as the inside of a box, and all that time this had been burning and wheeling over a kingdom too bright to see it.

The carriage climbed on through the pass with the engine thrumming under him, and his ears went numb in the wind and his eyes ran with the cold, and the stars went over, turning slowly, and he watched them turn.

When at last he drew his head back in, Arian was not looking at the stars at all. He was looking at Caleb.

There was frost in his hair. The cold that came in at the window had settled on him and found nothing there to melt it, and it lay along his dark hair and the black fur of his collar in a fine pale dust, glittering faintly in the starlight. He did not look away when he was caught. He went on looking, steadily, from the far side of the carriage in the dark, with the starlight on his face.

“There.”

Caleb had to swallow before he could speak. “Thank you.”

\* \* \*

Caleb’s first dawn came up on the second morning, in the high valleys of the Marches, and Arian was not there to see it.

The convoy had pulled in an hour before, when the sky behind the eastern peaks had begun, faintly, to grey, and the last of the stars were going out one by one like candles at the end of a ball. The waystation was cut into the side of the mountain, a low stone building with walls a yard thick and shutters of riveted iron and a sign over the door in square Holdish letters, and the lycans had unharnessed nothing, because there was nothing to unharness. Arian had stepped down from the carriage and stood a moment in the cold, looking east.

“You should go in,” urged Caleb.

“Yes.” Arian stayed where he was.

“It is going to be light. Soon. I can feel it.” He could. He had been able to feel it for an hour, a faint warm pressure behind the eastern mountains, like a fire in the next room. “You will burn.”

“Yes,” Arian said again.

Then he turned and looked for a long time at Caleb, who stood in the snow with his face to the east like one of his own roses. At the iron door he stopped with his hand on the latch, and Caleb saw the long fingers close on it and hold there for the space of a breath. Then he went inside, and the door closed behind him, and Caleb was alone in the valley with a dozen lycans and Mirek and the mountains and the sky.

It was quiet, for the engines had stopped, and the snow squeaked under his boots when he shifted, and the lycans’ breath went up in plumes, and far down the valley a stream was talking to itself under the ice. The grey in the east turned to pearl, and the pearl to a long thin wash of rose, and the peaks stood up black against it, sharp as cut paper.

The sun came up at a quarter past the hour.

It came up over the eastern peaks the way it must have come up every morning for ten thousand years, without any fuss. There was first a line of gold along the top of the ridge, and then a blaze too bright to look at, and then the whole valley was filling with light, pouring down the white slopes and across the snow and into the black mouths of the pines, so that everything it touched turned gold and rose and blinding white. It came down the slope towards him like a tide, and he watched it come, and could not breathe. And it reached Caleb where he stood, and went into him.

He had thought it would be like the crystals. The crystals in the garden had saved his life, but they were only the sun’s heart, cut out and hung on a chain, and this was the sun itself, the living sun. It found his face first, and then his chest through the wool of his cloak, and then his hands, so that he pulled off his gloves and dropped them in the snow to have it on his bare skin. It poured into every part of him at once, warm and golden and indifferent to whether he was a prince or a hostage or a traitor, and under his skin his own gold rose up to meet it, brighter than it had been since the Citadel. Caleb closed his eyes and turned his face up into it and stood there in the snow with his arms out and his head back and let it fill him to the brim.

For seven weeks, even under the crystals, some last part of him had been cold, and he had not known it until now, when it was gone.

He laughed aloud. Behind him Mirek laughed too, and then one of the lycans, and then all of them, the whole escort standing about in the snow grinning like fools at a Solar prince who was standing in the dawn with his arms out, laughing at the sky.

He stood there until the gold went out of the light and the sky turned a clear, cold, astonishing blue, and the snow began to glitter. When at last he lowered his arms, and opened his eyes, and turned round to go in, his face was wet.

He looked up at the waystation.

The shutters were iron, and every one of them was closed but one. On the upper floor, at the corner of the building where the eaves threw the deepest shadow, a single shutter had been opened the width of a hand and no more, at an angle the sun would not reach until the afternoon, and in the dark behind it Caleb saw a pale face, watching him stand in the light.

He stayed where he was in the blazing snow, with his face still wet, and let himself be looked at, as one keeps still for a wild thing at the edge of a wood.

Then the shutter closed.

\* \* \*

He slept through most of that day in a narrow dwarven bed under a patchwork quilt, with the shutters wide open and the sun lying across the floor in a long warm bar that crept across the boards as the hours went by, and up over the quilt, and onto his hands. He had not slept so well since the Citadel.

When he woke it was late afternoon, and the light had gone long and golden and was lying across the mountains opposite like honey, and someone had left a tray at the foot of the bed with bread and cheese and a jug of milk and, beside it, folded neatly, the grey velvet travelling rug from the carriage, which he had not asked for.

He ate, and went downstairs.

The common room was dark as a cellar, every shutter closed and a single lamp burning on the long table. It smelled of woodsmoke and old ale and the iron of the shutters, and after the gold upstairs it was so dark that Caleb had to stand in the doorway until his eyes caught up with him. Arian sat at the end of it with a sheaf of Enzo’s dispatches and a pen. He looked up when Caleb came in and looked him over, face and hands and hair, the way a physician checks a patient, and went back to the dispatches.

“You were out in it for an hour.”

“I was.” Caleb sat down at the other end of the table. “It was the most wonderful hour of my life. I recommend it to you in the strongest possible terms, Sovereign.”

He heard it land. Arian’s pen stopped a hair’s breadth above the page, and the heat went up Caleb’s throat and into his face, and he would have given anything to have it back.

But Arian only said, after a moment, “Tell me what it was like.”

Caleb opened his mouth, and found he did not know where to begin.

“There was a line. Along the top of the ridge. Gold, as if someone had ruled it with a pen. And then it was not a line any more, it was everything, all at once, pouring down the snow.” His hands had begun to move. He let them, sketching the ridge on the air and the sweep of the light down the slope. “And afterwards the sky was blue. I had forgotten. Ours is white at home, always, with noon, and I had quite forgotten that a sky could be any colour at all. And the snow glittered. And there was a bird on the roof, black and white, with a long tail, and it shouted at me.”

“Shouted.”

“Most rudely. I believe I was in its way.”

At some point Arian had put down his pen. He sat at the far end of the table in the lamplight, listening, and when Caleb had finished he was quiet for a while.

“A magpie.”

“You have seen one?”

“In pictures.”

Caleb looked at him down the length of the long dark table, at the shutters closed over every window, and the one lamp, and the pen laid down.

“Tomorrow,” promised Caleb, when he could trust his voice, “I shall take notes, and you shall have all of it.”

\* \* \*

They crossed the high Marches on the third night and the fourth, and the country changed around them.

The Coast had been all black stone and violet lamps and old, old things. The Marches were a borderland, and they had the manners of one. Everything that could not be sold anywhere else was sold here, and everything that could not be built anywhere else was built here, and the result was a country that looked as if every realm in the known world had emptied its pockets into it at once and never come back to tidy up.

Gnome trading posts stood at every crossroads, painted in stripes of orange and white, with little brass weathercocks on their roofs that turned to point at every passing carriage and rang a bell if it looked like custom. At the dwarven toll gates cut into the living rock, under iron portcullises, keepers with square-cut beards read the Sovereign’s papers by lamplight with enormous care and wished Caleb a pleasant journey in stiff Alorian that sounded as if it had been hammered out on an anvil one word at a time.

Aether pylons marched along the ridges, great latticed towers of iron and glass that carried the Holds’ own light from forge to forge, humming faintly in the cold air, with blue sparks crawling along their cables and a smell like a coming storm. A lycan hill town clung to the side of a mountain like a wasp’s nest, all wooden galleries and smoke, and when they stopped there at midnight to take on water, Caleb heard fiddle music coming out of every door. And there was an iron bridge across a gorge, built by the giants of the Jötun North a thousand years ago and never used by them since, so vast that the convoy took nearly a quarter of an hour to cross it, with the wind booming in the girders far overhead like surf.

Caleb saw all of it. He sat by the window with his cheek against the glass and took notes as he had promised, in pencil, in a little book on his knee, writing blind in the dark and trusting to luck. Every morning in the shuttered waystation, while the sun climbed the sky outside, he read his notes aloud across the long table, and Arian listened. Now and then he asked what colour a thing had been.

“Orange,” Caleb would say. “Like a marigold. Like the inside of an apricot.”

And Arian would nod, and put it away somewhere.

It was on the fourth night, at a place called Wolfsgrund, that Caleb understood what the lycan escort was for.

Wolfsgrund was a market town at the top of a pass, where three roads met, the last town before the Kessel road branched off towards the Holds. It lived on the traffic of the Marches, which is to say on everyone and no one, and it had the look of a place where people went to buy things they did not intend to explain. The convoy stopped there to take on fuel for the engines, and Caleb, who had been sitting in the carriage for six hours, got out to stretch his legs.

He had gone ten paces into the square before he understood that he should not have.

It was midnight, and freezing, and the square was lit with torches and crowded from wall to wall with every kind of creature the Marches could produce. It smelled of tallow and wet fur and roasting chestnuts and hot spiced wine, and the noise of it came off the walls in waves. There were lycans in fur and gnome traders in bright wool, and a pair of dwarves arguing over a cart, and a fae with a tray of glass charms that rang in the wind, and more than one vampire in the plain dark clothes of the Marches, with the watchful, sidelong look of people a long way from home who did not wish to be recognised.

And in the middle of it stood Caleb in his travelling cloak, with the gold shimmering on his skin in the torchlight like sunlight on water.

He heard the square notice. It went quiet in patches, the way a field goes quiet when a hawk passes over. He heard someone say *Solar* in Lowish, and someone else say it in Holdish, and a third voice, closer, say it in Vesperian with a word after it that he did not know and did not need to. He saw heads turn. He saw a knot of lycans by the well look at one another and then at him, and one of them put down the jug he was holding.

Then there was a hand at the small of his back.

Arian had come without a sound. There was only the cold of him, suddenly, all down Caleb’s side, and the hand, flat and steady against his spine. The torches along the walls leaned. The shadows of the whole square leaned in at once towards the place where they stood, like water towards a drain.

Arian stood beside Caleb with his hand at his back and looked at the square of Wolfsgrund.

The square looked back at him. Then, a patch at a time, the way it had gone quiet, it began to find that it had other business to attend to. The lycans by the well picked up their jug. The vampires in plain clothes remembered appointments elsewhere. The noise came back in, stall by stall and voice by voice, until the square was as loud as it had been before, and nobody in it was looking at Caleb.

The hand stayed where it was.

“I only wanted,” said Caleb, rather faintly, “to stretch my legs, and perhaps buy a chestnut, and I see now that I have very nearly started a war over it.”

“Stretch them in the carriage.”

He steered Caleb back across the square with his hand still at his back, not hurrying, and handed him up into the carriage, and got in after him, and shut the door. Then he sat down across from him and looked out of the window, and said nothing for the next two hours.

Caleb sat by his own window and watched Wolfsgrund go by, and then the dark pass beyond it. He could still feel the place where the hand had been, a cool print at the small of his back that the warmth of the carriage took a long time to fill.

At the back of the convoy, as they pulled out of the square, he saw Paul by the last carriage, alone, with his head bowed and his hands cupped close to his mouth, as a man does when he is warming his fingers against the cold. Caleb thought nothing of it, for the night was bitter. Paul had not once looked at him in four nights, and Caleb thought, with a pang, that he would try again at Durnhollow.

\* \* \*

They came to Old Meridian on the fifth night.

Caleb had no name for it yet. He knew only that the road had climbed out of the last pass and come down into a high valley, long and narrow and filled with mist, and that the mist was glowing. It glowed faintly, all through, with a pale grey light that came from nowhere, as if the valley had been filled with moonlight and left to set. The carriage lamps made no difference to it. The light lay on the backs of his hands where they rested on the sill, and on the grey velvet, and on the black lacquer, cool and even and without a shadow anywhere. Beads of it gathered on the outside of the glass and ran down. And through the mist, as the road went down, he began to see shapes.

There were arches and columns, and the long white line of a street paved in stone worn smooth, running up a hillside between the ghosts of walls, and a dry fountain with a stone figure in the middle of it whose face the weather had rubbed away, and doorways with nothing behind them, and whole houses standing roofless with trees growing up through their floors. It went on and on, on both sides of the road, up the slopes of the valley and into the mist. It had been a city once, a great one, larger than the Citadel. Caleb could see that from the width of the streets and the height of the arches and the way it had all been laid out, square by square, by someone who had known where everything ought to go.

The city was silent. On the roof above him the lycans, who had been talking and laughing in low voices all through the pass, had fallen quiet, and in their silence the thrum of the engine seemed to fill the whole valley.

He sat up straight. “What is this place?”

Arian did not answer.

Caleb turned from the window to look at him, and stopped.

Arian sat as he had sat for five nights, with his back to the way they were going and his face towards the window, his hands resting on his knees. It was the stillness Caleb had seen once before, in the doorway of the garden, when the light had caught his fingers on the frame, the stillness of someone holding a great weight with care, so that it would not fall.

“Sovereign?”

“Old Meridian.”

His voice was as level as it always was. He kept his face to the window.

The carriage was climbing now, along the flank of the valley, and the ruins were thicker here, crowded together on the slope, and the mist was thinner. Above them, at the very top of the hill, on the edge of what might once have been a garden and was now a black wilderness of briars, a point of light caught and flashed.

It was a glasshouse, or the skeleton of one. Its ribs were iron, slender and frail, and they curved up out of the briars into a dome as delicate as a birdcage, and the dome was empty. A few panes still clung to it, clouded and cracked and hanging at angles, and the mist went in and out between the ribs at its leisure. As the carriage passed below, they caught the starlight and threw it back down the hill, once, like a signal.

Caleb’s chest tightened.

He knew the feeling, like a word on the tip of the tongue, or a stair in the dark that is not where the foot expects it to be. He looked at the empty dome at the top of the hill, and the briars, and the broken glass, and for an instant he smelled what was not there, warm glass and wet earth, and under them, very faint, cold and sour and bright, a smell like a silver spoon left in the rain.

His palms had begun to prickle. A faint warmth was gathering in them, the way it gathered before a healing, and he turned his hands over in his lap and looked at them, puzzled, and closed them.

It was gone before he could reach for it, as it had gone the first time, and he let it go again, because there was nothing there to hold.

“There was a Summit here.” His own voice sounded strange to him, too loud in the hush. “Wasn’t there? At the last Long Eclipse. The one twenty years ago.”

“Yes.”

“I was here.” He had not thought about it in years. But the silence was very large, and he had always filled silences, and his voice went on into it lightly, as it might have gone on at dinner. “We all were, all five of us, the whole family. I was eight. I remember the tents. I remember Matilda put me on her horse and led me up and down the lines, and Cecily cried because she wanted to go too. And then…” He frowned. “I took a fever, the first night. I don’t remember anything after that. I slept through the whole of the Eclipse, they told me, and woke up on the yacht home with a physician from the Harp Vales sitting by my bed, and I was ill for a month. Mother said I had caught a chill in the mist.” He looked at the glasshouse, sliding away now behind them into the dark. “It seems a great pity, to have come all this way and been given the only Long Eclipse of my childhood, and to have slept through the whole of it like a cat on a windowsill.”

The carriage climbed. Caleb turned to look at him again.

Arian was sitting as he had been, his face to the window. But in the starlight from the glass his face had gone so white that it was almost luminous, the white of bone, or of the snow on the high passes, and his hands on his knees had closed. The right one had closed so hard that the knuckles stood up sharp under the skin, and a fine tremor ran through it, from the wrist to the fist and back, steady as a plucked string.

“Sovereign,” said Caleb. “What happened here?”

The engine thrummed. The mist went by. On the roof above them a lycan shifted his weight, and the carriage creaked, and was quiet again.

“My father died here,” Arian said.

The mist had left no shadow anywhere in the valley. Now one gathered in the carriage, about Arian’s feet and up along the seat behind him, close and dark as a drawn cloak.

Caleb went cold.

He had slept through it. He had been eight years old, in a silk tent across the valley, sleeping off a chill in the mist, while a mile away up the hill a boy of twelve had lost his father under the same black sun.

And Caleb had slept.

“I am so sorry.”

Arian went on looking at the window. The glasshouse was gone now, lost behind them in the mist, and he looked at the place where it had been.

He had been taught in the Harp Vales, above everything else, that a vampire was never, for any reason, to be touched. At the garden doors there had been a burn to heal, and that was a healer's work. Nothing excused this.

He leaned across the carriage and put his hand over Arian’s closed fist.

It was the right hand, the one he had healed. The knuckles were smooth now where the burns had been, and hard as river stones, and so cold that the cold went up his arm like winter and set his teeth on edge, but he left his hand there, lightly, the way one lays a hand on a bell to quiet it, and said nothing.

And for a heartbeat, no longer, the carriage was not there. There was a cold hand under his own, and he was very young, and the earth was soaking through the knees of his breeches, and there was broken glass glittering somewhere above him, and he was not going to run away. Then it was gone, like breath off a mirror.

Under Caleb’s palm the fist stayed shut, and the tremor ran through it, and through Caleb’s hand with it, until he could not tell whose it was.

Then Arian breathed out. It was the first sound he had made since he had spoken of his father, and it was not even. And slowly, under Caleb’s hand, the fist began to open.

It opened finger by finger, as a knot gives when someone has worked at it patiently in the dark, until the hand lay flat on Arian’s knee with Caleb’s resting on the back of it. The tremor went out of it. It lay there, cold and open, and let itself be held. Between Caleb’s fingers the gold had come up faintly in his skin, and it lay along the backs of Arian’s knuckles like dawn through the crack of a shutter.

They stayed like that across the narrow width of the carriage, while the valley of Old Meridian went by outside in the glowing mist, and the stars went over, and neither of them said a single word.

When at last the road climbed out of the far end of the valley and into the next pass, and the mist fell away behind them, and the ruins with it, Caleb took his hand back.

Arian let him. After a while he spoke to the window, so low that the engine nearly took it. “Thank you.”

It was the second time he had said it, and Caleb was keeping count.

\* \* \*

He meant to stay awake. There was a whole pass still to see, with an iron bridge in it that the lycans said was nearly as fine as the giants’ one, and he had promised notes.

He sat by the window with the book on his knee and the pencil in his hand, and the dark went by, and his head grew heavy against the glass. Somewhere in the pass the cushion gave beside him, as if someone had crossed the carriage and sat down. The carriage lurched on a bend. His head slid sideways and came to rest against a shoulder, cool and firm, and he waited, half asleep, for it to move away.

It held there, steady as a wall.

Some time later a weight settled over him, heavy and cold, smelling of stone and old paper and, under those, something clean he could not name, like the air after snow. He thought he ought to say something about it. He was asleep before he had decided what.

He woke to grey light behind the shutters, the carriage stopped and the engine silent. He was lying along the seat with his head on the folded grey rug, under a black silk coat lined with fur. The carriage was empty. The shutters were closed over every window, and through the cracks at their edges the light of the dawn was coming in, thin and gold and growing.

Caleb lay very still under the coat and felt the heat climb into his face.

Outside he could hear the lycans unloading the baggage, and Mirek’s voice, and the stamp of feet in the snow. And beyond that, far below, a sound he had never heard before, a great slow grinding hum, deep enough to feel in his chest, like an enormous engine turning over under a mountain.

He gathered up the black coat in his arms without thinking about it. It was cold all through, as it always would be, however long it had lain over him, and he held it against his chest anyway, and opened the carriage door, and looked out.

The convoy had stopped on a ledge high on the side of a mountain, beside a dwarven waystation with iron shutters. The air struck his face clean and bitter, smelling of snow and hot iron and coal smoke. Below the ledge the valley fell away in a single sheer sweep of rock, a thousand feet, two thousand, down to a river that was only a thread of silver in the dawn. And across the valley, the whole face of the opposite mountain had been carved into a city.

It went up the rock in tiers, terrace on terrace, gallery on gallery, so many that Caleb could not count them, each cut from the living stone and faced with iron, and every iron face was cut with square dwarven runes lit from within by a steady, patient, golden fire. Great doors stood in the rock at every level, some of them a hundred feet high. Bridges of iron and glass leapt from tier to tier like the threads of a web, and on them, tiny with distance, moved carts and figures and lamps. Smoke went up from a hundred forges and hung in the still air in long blue skeins. And the whole carven face of the mountain was catching the first light of the sun as it came over the peaks behind him, and burning with it, gold on iron on gold, so that for a moment the mountain itself seemed to be on fire.

“Durnhollow,” whispered Mirek, beside the carriage.

Caleb stood in the carriage door with the Sovereign’s coat in his arms and the sun on his face, and looked at it for a long time.

Then he looked up at the waystation above the ledge, where every shutter was closed but one.
