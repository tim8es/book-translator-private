## Chapter 10: Chapter Nine - The Ambush at the Fault Line

*THE FAULT ROAD, from Dornhöhle south to the Coast. Shorter than the Wolfsgrund road by a night and a half, and quieter, there being no market towns upon it. It passes through the gorge called the Fault, which a carriage may clear in one night if nothing stops it. One waystation, at the southern rim. The Holds keep no watch upon this road, and accept no liability for what befalls there.*

— From *Roads of the Eclipse Marches, with Distances*, kept by the office of the Usher of Distances, Dornhöhle

\* \* \*

Dornhöhle measured its guests out with as much care as it had measured them in.

The Usher of Distances was waiting in the court of the Undergate when the last hammer of the afternoon came up through the stone, with his notched staff in one hand and his iron book on its chain, and behind him the four carriages stood humming on the flagstones with the violet of their engines trembling under their floors. The forge light came up out of the gulf and ran along their black lacquer the way wine runs round the inside of a cup that has been swirled. On every tier above the court the runes had been knocked down to their evening gold, and a great many dwarves who had no business in the Undergate at that hour had found some, and stood along the galleries in their aprons with their arms folded on the parapets, looking down.

Caleb did not understand why until he saw the board.

It stood beside the gate, a slab of black slate as tall as a door in a frame of riveted iron, with every promise sealed in the last seven days chalked on it, one beneath another, in the square hand of the clerks of the Chain. Theirs was near the top. *Caleb of the Solar House. Consented. Nachdrücklich.* Somebody had underlined the last word in a cheerful gnomish yellow. Somebody else, in blue, had underlined it again.

“The Holds,” lamented Caleb, to nobody at all, “have been very unkind.”

“The Holds have been reading, Highness,” answered the Usher, who had come up at his elbow without a sound. “The Holds read everything that is put up to be read. It is why it is put up.”

He opened his book and stood for some while with his white brows drawn together over a page that had not been written on before. At last he shut it, and took Caleb by the sleeve, as a verger takes a latecomer to his pew, and walked him across the court to the old line before the first carriage, where the Sovereign would stand, and stood him on it.

“A promise sealed under the bond-law stands on one line. The book is clear.” He looked at the arrangement with something very like contentment. “The book is not often clear, Highness. It is a great pleasure when it is.”

Arian came down the street from the Coast’s house a little after, with the lycans of the escort round him in their Conti black, and stopped at the edge of the line. He looked at the Usher, and at Caleb standing on it, and at the gold lying in the old cut groove of it between them. Then he stepped onto it, so that they stood side by side with their sleeves not quite touching and the same line under both their boots, and the cold of him came along Caleb’s arm like the draught from a door left open in another room.

The Usher bowed to them both, to the depth the book allowed, and then opened his book and wrote in it, slowly, a single line. Caleb had a strong suspicion that the line was the word on the board.

At the back of the court, by the door with the seven iron bars, a starched lace cap with two stiff wings to it was bobbing above the heads of the dwarves. Under it Maartje Bosboom lifted her ledger on its chain and held it up before her, solemnly, as a standard-bearer dips his flag to a prince going by, until Caleb bowed to her across the court. Then she went pink to the starch and vanished, as a dormouse vanishes into a hedge.

Mirek was already on the box of the second carriage. In three days in Dornhöhle he had acquired a dwarven pocketknife with eleven blades, a cold in the head, and a single Holdish word, *prächtig*, which meant splendid and which he now used for everything, including the cold. He had his short sword across his knees and his bandaged hand in his lap, and when he saw Caleb looking he grinned and touched his forehead with two fingers, as he had seen the lycans of the roof do to their sergeant.

Paul was putting the last of the baggage up into the fourth carriage.

He did it as he had done everything since the Hall of the Chain, slowly and thoroughly and without looking up, and when the last bag was stowed he stood a moment with his hand on the door. Caleb crossed the court to him. The forge light from the gulf lay red along one side of Paul’s face, and the shadow of the carriage lay over the other.

“Ride with us. There is room. Mirek takes up a great deal of the box, but the inside is quite empty except for me and the Sovereign, and the Sovereign does not take up any room at all, unless you count the cold.”

“I’m better with the bags, Highness.”

“Nobody is better with bags. Bags are not a thing one can be better with.”

“The Accord says the last carriage.” Paul looked at the door handle under his hand. “It’s a long road. Get some sleep.”

Caleb thought of the passage under his room last night, and the big head bowed over cupped hands under the one lamp, praying to a moon that was not there. He put out his hand and laid it on Paul’s arm.

“Then I shall see you at the waystation. You may carry my bags in, and I shall complain about the stairs.”

Paul’s arm under his hand went still, the way a horse goes still under a stranger. “Yes, Highness.” He got up into the carriage, and drew the door to.

\* \* \*

The Holds let them out of the mountain by its southern face, through a gate the height of four men that opened onto the dusk.

The sun had gone down behind the western peaks a quarter of an hour before, and the dwarves had judged it to the minute. The shutters on the western side of the carriages stayed closed, for there was gold still lying low and thin in the sky over there. But the eastern side of the valley was already in shadow, and Caleb let down his shutter and looked out at it, and was astonished to find that he knew the colour.

The snow on the high peaks had gone from white to rose while he watched, and from rose to a deep, soft, ringing violet, and the sky above them had gone the same, with one star in it. It was the violet of the Coast. He had looked out at it from the smoked windows of the east wing for three weeks and hated it, and he had not known until this moment, with the forges of Dornhöhle humming behind him and the cold coming in at the glass, that he had missed it.

He sat back in the grey velvet, rather shaken, and said nothing about it to anyone.

The road went down from the gate in long loops across the face of the mountain, and the hum of the forges went down with them, fainter and fainter in the iron of the wheels, until it was only a thing he could feel in his teeth when the carriage stopped on a bend, and then not even that. He had lived inside that sound for three days, and its absence lay over him like a shawl taken from his shoulders in a cold room.

Across the carriage, Arian sat with his back to the road and his right hand on his knee.

The lamp was not lit. In the last of the violet through the glass his hand lay very white on the black cloth, with the two gold rings on it catching what light there was and the third finger bare. As Caleb watched, Arian’s thumb moved across to the bare finger and pressed at the root of it, and turned, once, a quarter turn, at nothing.

It was the gesture of a man turning a ring that had sat on that finger for eleven years. His hand had not yet been told it was gone.

Caleb looked out of the window.

Under his travelling cloak, in the breast of his coat, the ring lay where he had sewn it last night, heavy and flat and cold through the silk. Every time the carriage leaned on a bend it leaned with him and came back, a hard round pressure over his heart like a thumb laid there to keep his place.

He had his notebook on his knee. He opened it to have something to do with his hands.

“I owe you three days. I have been very remiss. You may have Dornhöhle now, if you like, or you may have it in the morning, at the waystation, with the tea.”

Arian turned his head from the window. “Now.”

Caleb found the page. He had written it in pencil, in the light of the bronze well, and the light had made his hand go large and careless.

“*The room at the top of the house,*” he read. “*There is a sun on the floorboards. It comes down a shaft lined with bronze, which hands it from one plate to the next all the way from the mountainside, as a bucket is handed down a line of people at a fire. It stands up from the floor to the ceiling like a pillar holding up nothing at all, and if you put your hand into it your hand is in summer, and the rest of you is still inside the mountain.*” He turned the page. “*The steward says the Sovereign wrote ahead for it.*” He looked up. “That last part is not a description. It is an accusation. I wished it to be on the record.”

Arian did not dispute it.

“You wrote ahead.”

“Yes.”

“Before we had left the Coast.”

“Yes.”

“Nobody told me.”

Arian considered this. “Nor about the lemons.”

Caleb opened his mouth, and shut it again, and looked down at the page in the dark, where his large careless pencil had gone soft and silver and almost unreadable.

“Was it warm?” asked Arian.

“Yes. Very.”

Arian waited.

Caleb began to tell him, and stopped. He had meant to say that it was like standing near a fire, and then that it was like a bath, and then that it was like the inside of a bed on a winter morning when someone has been lying in it before you and got up. Every one of them was warm, and every one of them was a thing the man across the carriage had sat beside, or lain in, without being warmed by it at all.

“I am trying to think what to compare it with. Everything I think of is something you would have to have felt already.”

“I have stood beside a great many fires.”

“And?”

Arian was quiet a moment.

“They were very bright,” he said.

He said it as he said everything, without weight, as if it were an item in a ledger, and turned back to the window. Caleb sat holding his notebook open at a page he could no longer read, and thought of the study on the Coast, with its high fire roaring away in the grate night after night for a man who stood with his back to it and could not feel it.

“Then I shall have to find you something that is not a fire,” he promised. “Give me until the Coast.”

“That is a long time,” murmured Arian, to the glass.

“It is a long road. Paul says so.”

\* \* \*

They came to the Fault a little after midnight.

The Marches had been dark and empty for hours, high moor and snow and black stands of pine, and once a herd of something with antlers that stood in the road and looked at the carriage lamps until the lycans on the roof shouted at it. There were no market towns and no pylons, no gnome weathercocks and no fiddles, and Caleb, who had grown fond of the Marches’ habit of emptying its pockets on every corner, found that he missed the noise. The lycans sang to fill it. They sang on the roofs in the dark, low and in parts, in the slow Wildmarch tongue that Vesna sang in when she thought nobody was listening, and Mirek on the box sang with them, a beat behind and a third too high, and nobody had the heart to tell him.

Twice they passed a hill farm with no light in it. The second was only a chimney, standing up black in the starlight out of a ring of fallen stone, with a dog’s chain running from a staple in its side to nothing at all. In the hill towns, Vesna had said once over the bread, a bad winter took the old people and a worse one took the young men, who went down off the hills to whoever would pay them and were not spoken of again. The lycans on the roof stopped singing as each farm went by.

Then the road came over a rise, and the moor stopped.

It ended. Ahead of the carriage the white ground ran on for fifty yards and then simply was not there, as if the Marches had once been folded like a letter and opened again, and the crease had never come out. Beyond the edge there was nothing, a black so complete that the stars seemed to start at Caleb’s knees, and far away across the nothing, a mile off or more, was a pale line in the starlight that he understood, after a moment, was the other side.

“The Fault,” said Arian.

The carriage turned along the edge and went down into it.

The road had been cut into the wall of the gorge, a long shelf of rock with the cliff going up on one side and going down on the other, wide enough for one carriage and not one inch more. The Holds had cut it, plainly, for there were runes in the stone of its outer edge every hundred paces, unlit, and every one of them had been cut square and true. It went down in long slants across the face of the rock, turning back on itself at the corners where the cliff allowed, and at every turning Caleb looked out and saw the stars go up, and the wall of the gorge climb past the window, and the dark below grow no lighter.

The walls of the Fault were striped. They went up in bands, one above the other, pale and dark and pale again, some as thin as a finger and some as deep as a house, so that the whole face of the gorge looked like the edge of a ledger seen end on, a thousand pages kept in two inks. Frost lay along the pale bands and caught the carriage lamps and gave them back as a glitter, and the dark bands took the light and kept it. Here and there, in the dark, something glinted that was not frost, a vein of old glass gone the colour of weak tea, catching the lamps in a long dull line and then letting them go.

“What broke it?” asked Caleb.

“The Holds say that a giant sat down.” Arian was looking out at the stripes going by.

“And what do you say?”

“That the road is shorter.”

On the box above them Mirek had heard the question through the lacquer, and he called down to the prince that a giant had nothing to do with it, and that everybody in the hill towns knew the moon had dropped a plate.

The singing on the roof had faltered on the first turn, as the drop opened below, and gone on again more quietly, and on the third turn it went on more quietly still. By the time they were halfway down the lycans were only humming, and the hum came down through the roof of the carriage and into the grey velvet, and it was the only warm thing in the gorge.

Far below, there was a river. Caleb heard it before he saw it, a long low pour of sound coming up the walls out of the dark, and when at last the road levelled and ran along a ledge a hundred feet above it, he saw it go by under the stars, pale and fast and the colour of pewter, with ice along its edges in shelves.

Where the ledge turned above a pool, the lamps of the carriage went down the wall and found something on one of the shelves, and gave it to him for the space of a breath. It was the iron ribs of a carriage, stripped of its lacquer, with the river going through it, and on the ice beside it lay something pale and long that the frost had kept, and that he did not look at long enough to know.

Above, between the two walls, the sky had narrowed to a ribbon. It was strewn with stars and laid along the top of the gorge, from one end of it to the other, like a bookmark left in a closed book.

Caleb put his head back against the velvet and looked up at it through the glass, and the carriage went on along the ledge, and the lycans hummed, and the river went by, and he let his eyes close, because there were hours of this still, and he had been promised a waystation at the southern rim and a bed with a quilt.

He did not know how long he slept. He woke because he was warm.

It was a faint warmth, and at first he could not find where it came from. It lay along the right side of his face and his right hand, where it rested on the sill, the way the warmth lies on one cheek of a man who has fallen asleep with his face towards a hearth. He opened his eyes. The carriage was dark. Arian sat across from him, a darkness in the dark, with the cold coming off him as it always did, and the glass by Caleb’s cheek was rimed with frost.

The warmth was coming through the frost.

He sat up. Under his skin, along his arms and up the sides of his throat, the gold had begun to rise, slowly and all one way, as a tired horse leans towards the stable when the road turns for home. It was leaning towards the window. It was leaning ahead of them, down the gorge and up the wall of it, to the place where the road went round a great buttress of striped rock and out of sight.

Deeper than the gold, deeper than the blood, something that had been asleep in him since the garden stirred, and turned over, and lifted its head.

It was the dead of the night, and the sun was on the far side of the world, and he could feel one, ahead of them in the rock, as plainly as he had felt the dawn coming up behind the peaks at the waystation. Only there were a great many of them, a whole cluster, close and hot and hidden, like coals banked under ash.

He thought of Maartje Bosboom on the bench at the feast, with her quick brown fingers on her ledger.

*You could put your hand on a lid and warm it.*

On the roof, the humming stopped.

It stopped all at once, between one note and the next, and in the silence it left, under the long pour of the river, Caleb heard another note. It was high and thin and sweet, and it came from somewhere up ahead in the dark, and it went on, and on, and did not stop.

Something up there was singing.

“Sovereign,” said Caleb. His voice came out very quiet. “There is something warm in the rock.”

Arian was already moving. He had turned his head at the first word, and at the last he was half out of his seat with his hand flat on the wall of the carriage, and Caleb saw the dark of the carriage round him thicken, as ink thickens in the well when the lid has been left off.

“Stop the carriages,” said Arian, not loudly, and the lycan on the roof above them heard it through the lacquer and shouted it, and the shout went down the convoy from roof to roof.

It was too late. Ahead of them, round the buttress, the mountain came down.

It came down in front of the first carriage with a sound like the whole of the Fault clearing its throat at once, a long splitting roar that went up the walls and along them and came back from the far side of the gorge a heartbeat later, and the ledge under the wheels shook. Caleb saw, through the frost on his window, the lamps of the first carriage swing wildly and go out. He saw two lycans of its roof leap clear into the dark, one each way, as cats go off a wall. Then the brakes of his own carriage bit, and the iron wheels screamed on the rock, and he was thrown forward off the seat.

He did not reach the floor. Arian caught him by both shoulders in mid-air and set him back on the velvet as one sets a fallen piece back on its square after the board has been jogged, and held him there a moment, hard.

“Stay in the carriage.”

Then the door was open, and the cold of the gorge was coming in, and he was gone.

For a breath there was nothing at all. The dust of the fall rolled back along the ledge, pale and slow in the starlight, and settled on the glass. The river poured on below. Somewhere ahead in the dark a single stone, the last of them, came bounding down the face of the cliff, knocking from ledge to ledge, and went past the window close enough to touch and on over the edge, and Caleb heard it fall a long way and not land.

And through all of it, patient and sweet, the singing.

Then the walls of the gorge opened their eyes.

They opened all at once, up and down the face of the buttress and along the cliff above the road, pairs and pairs of them, tawny and gold, catching the starlight and the last swinging lamps and throwing them back in the dark, as the eyes of a fox throw back the lantern of a man crossing a farmyard at night. There were twenty of them, thirty, more. Caleb heard the scrape of claws on rock, and the hiss of ropes paying out, and the eyes came down the walls of the Fault at a run, and the first howl went up from somewhere above the buttress and was answered from the other side.

The lycans of the escort answered it too. They came off the roofs of all four carriages into the road with their claws out and their Conti black swallowed by the dark, and Mirek’s voice, cracking, shouted something on the box over Caleb’s head, and the road was full.

Caleb had the door half open before he had decided to open it. He shut it again. *Stay in the carriage*. He knelt on the seat with his face against the frosted glass and his cloak bunched in his fists, and watched.

He could see very little, and what he could see he could hardly bear. The road was a confusion of shapes in the dark, shoulders and arms and the flash of eyes and claws, and the sounds that came through the lacquer were the sounds of beasts. One lamp still swung from the roof of the third carriage like a censer carried too fast up an aisle, and at each end of its swing it showed him something and took it away. It showed him a lycan of the escort with his scalp laid open and the blood coming down over one eye in a sheet, and a rogue on all fours over somebody on the rock, worrying at him, with his muzzle black to the eyes.

In the middle of the road the dark was thicker than anywhere else, and the lamp did not reach it, and the shapes that went into it did not come out as they had gone in.

Caleb saw a rogue leap into it off the roof of the first carriage, all claws and teeth, and stop in the air. A white hand had him by the throat. The frost went up out of that hand and over his face until his open eyes were white with it, and then the hand closed. Caleb heard it through the lacquer. It was the sound a pond makes when it gives under a skater on the last day of the frost, and the rogue came out of the dark in two pieces and struck the wall of the gorge, and where he struck it the ledger of the Fault had a line in a third ink, running.

Another came at the dark from behind with a knife. The dark turned without any hurry and took the arm that held the knife, and the arm came away at the shoulder as the leg comes away from a goose under a carver who knows his business, and the man ran on three steps without it before he understood. Arian did not watch him fall. In passing he set his heel on the neck of a rogue crawling from him across the rock, as a man on a garden path treads on a snail he has not troubled to see, and the crawling stopped.

Every shadow on the ledge had run the same way, towards that place, as rainwater runs to the lowest corner of a yard. The frost on the inside of Caleb’s glass grew while he watched, creeping in long feathers from the corners towards the middle.

He thought, with a kind of wild relief, that they had brought claws to a war with the night, and that there would be nothing left of them in a moment but the sound of their running.

Then somebody on the buttress shouted a word, and the Fault went to noon.

It came all at once, from a dozen places along the road, as the shutters of the Citadel are flung back in the morning by a dozen servants at a single bell. Twelve points of light opened in the dark. They were white at the edges and gold at the heart and so bright that Caleb could not look at them, and their light went up the walls of the gorge and lit every band of rock from the river to the rim, pale and dark and pale, and every vein of old glass in them blazed. The frost on the cliff flashed white. The river below went to molten gold. The blood on the road, which had been black a moment before, was all at once red, as red as the lacquer on a box of Lowmere sweets, and there was a great deal of it. And every shadow on the ledge, every one, the shadows of the carriages and the fallen rocks and the struggling bodies and the bodies that had stopped struggling and the thickness in the middle of the road where the dark had been gathering, was torn up off the ground and flung back up the walls of the Fault and pinned there, flat and thin and black, like washing pegged on a line in a high wind.

Caleb put up his arm against it. Then he lowered his arm, slowly, and looked.

He had been raised under Aether-crystals. He had walked the Citadel’s grid at his father’s side and could name every grade of stone from a street lamp to a spire. He looked at the lights in the road and knew them, in the blood, before he knew them in his head. The gold under his skin had leapt up to meet them the moment they opened, as a child at a window leaps up when a carriage it knows turns into the street, and it was straining towards them now, and deeper than the gold the old thing in him that had lifted its head was standing up.

They were blades. Each was a long sliver of something clear as ice and gold at its heart, no longer than a man’s forearm, held in a hilt of dull black iron. The lycans who held them held them out before them at arm’s length, as men hold torches, with their faces screwed up against the glare and the light running off the blades onto their fists. It was Aether. It was deep-trench Aether, refined further than he had known Aether could be refined, so far that it no longer lit the air but burned a hole in it. And one of them, the third from the left, held by a lean lycan with a torn ear, was singing.

He knew of one city in the world that could refine Aether at all, let alone like this, and he had been born on the terraces above its chimneys.

He put that down. He put it down so hard that he felt it in his hands.

The twelve lights were moving. They had come down off the buttress and out from the rocks in a long line, and now the line was bending at its ends and closing, slow and careful, round the middle of the road, and in the middle of the road, with nothing left round him now but the light, stood Arian.

He stood quite still. His hands were open at his sides. The darkness that had been thick about him was gone up the walls with all the rest, and he stood alone in the blaze on the bare rock with his black coat and his black hair and his white face, and the frost on his shoulders was steaming. Caleb had seen that frost lie unmelted three paces from a forge. He watched it go now, all at once, in a long white breath of steam that the light went through and turned to gold, and under it, along Arian’s cheekbone and the line of his jaw, the skin was reddening, as the backs of his fingers had reddened on the threshold of the garden. His hands were red too, to the wrist, and the light had nothing to do with that. He did not look at them.

The circle of blades closed by another pace. Arian moved.

He was faster than anything Caleb had known could move. One moment he stood in the middle of the circle, and the next he was at its edge, and a lycan was going backwards over the lip of the ledge with his throat opened from one ear to the other and his blade wheeling away over his head like a dropped torch, and then Arian was back in the middle again, and there was a long red weal across the back of his hand where the light had touched it in passing. The circle closed the gap. It closed every gap. When Arian went for one side of it, the blades on the other side came in behind him, and he had to turn, and the turning cost him, and Caleb could see what it cost. The steam went up off him in the glare. The red came up on his skin and rose in blisters along the weals, and the blisters broke and wept, and the light went into what was under them. A smell came through the lacquer that Caleb knew from the burns ward of the Harp Vales. And the shadows hung pinned flat on the walls of the gorge, all of them, straining towards him and unable to come down.

They were herding him. They were driving him with light, the way the shepherds of the Highland March drive sheep with dogs, from one side and then the other, never too close and never letting go, until the sheep is so tired it will stand still for the knife.

Caleb’s hand was on the door again. He did not shut it this time.

But it was his own door that opened first, from outside, and a lycan stood in it.

He was big, and old, with grey all through his beard and a seam of white scar running up into his hairline, and he wore no colours, only fur and leather and a broad belt hung with knives. He filled the doorway and blocked the light. He looked at Caleb kneeling on the grey velvet, at the gold on his skin and the cloak in his fists, and he smiled, as a man smiles who has been sent to fetch a child in from the garden.

“Highness. Out you come. Gently, now.”

Mirek came off the box.

He came down on the lycan from above with his short sword in both hands and a cry that had more terror in it than fury and more fury than sense, and the big lycan turned under him without hurry and caught him by the coat and threw him. Caleb saw him go. He saw Mirek go through the air in the white glare with his sword spinning out of his hand, and strike the iron wheel of the carriage with his leg, and he heard the leg break. It made a sound like a green branch snapped across a knee, and Mirek screamed, and was on the rock.

Caleb did not remember getting out of the carriage. He was simply out of it, and past the big lycan, under his arm, and on his knees on the road with his hands on Mirek, and Mirek was white and shaking and saying *Highness, Highness* through his teeth, and his leg lay at an angle that a leg should not. Caleb’s hands went to it before he could stop them. They knew where the bone had gone. They were warm already, the light pooling in the palms as it always did at the sight of a wound, eager, ready to pour.

Somebody came at them out of the glare with a knife.

This one was younger, rough-haired, with blood on his teeth from somewhere, and he had come round the back of the carriage and seen a boy in Conti black lying on the ground, and was doing the simplest thing. The knife was ordinary steel and long, and it came down at Mirek, and Caleb did the simplest thing too. He threw himself across the boy’s body, and turned as he did it, so that he came down with his back to Mirek and his face to the knife, and the knife came on.

It stopped a finger’s breadth from his heart.

He saw it stop. He saw the point of it hang in the white light over the breast of his coat, so near that it lifted the nap of the silk, and tremble there. He saw the young lycan’s face behind it in the glare, frightened, and younger than he had thought.

“*Not him!*”

It was the big lycan, behind him, and it came out of him in a roar.

“Not him, you fool, not him. Not a hair of his head. You heard the Don. The Don’s own grandson stood in my yard with the purse in his hand and said it to me, and I said it to you, and I’ll have your ears if you’ve marked him.” He had the young one by the scruff and was dragging him back, and the knife went back with him, out of the light. “Not a hair. Not a scratch. Not a fright, if we can help it.” He looked down at Caleb, and the anger went out of his face and the smile came back into it. “Your pardon, Highness. He’s young.”

Caleb lay across Mirek on the cold rock and looked up at him.

The light of the blades lay over everything, over the fallen knife and Mirek’s leg and the long red weals on Arian’s skin. They had come down the walls of the Fault in the middle of the night with twelve suns in their fists for the Sovereign of the Coast, and they had thrown a boy of sixteen against a wheel to break his leg, and they would have cut down every lycan in Conti black on the road without a second look, and they had stopped a knife a finger’s breadth from Caleb’s heart and begged his pardon for it, because someone, somewhere, a long way from this gorge, had told them how they were to treat him.

Someone had sat at a table, a long way off, and thought about his hair.

The cold of it went into him like the air of the high passes coming in at an open window, and stayed.

“And which Don would that be?” he asked.

“Someone who wants you whole, Highness.” The big lycan held out his hand to help him up, quite courteously. “There’s people waiting to take you home.”

*Home.*

Caleb did not take the hand. He got up by himself, slowly, and stood over Mirek, and looked at the lycan, and then past him, along the ledge, at the blades.

“How very kind. You must tell me all about them. But you will forgive me, I hope, if I do not come this minute. I find I have an engagement.”

And he turned his back on the man and walked into the fight.

\* \* \*

Matilda had taught him the sword, and in all the years of it he had never once beaten her.

She had laid him flat in the practice yard on a thousand mornings, and stood over him, and said *Again*. On the rare days when she was pleased with him she told him why he lost, and the reason was always the same. *You fight like a courtier, Caleb. Stop looking at my sword and look at my feet. Nobody ever fell down because of a sword. They fall down because of their feet.*

He looked at their feet.

There was a knot of three by the second carriage, two rogues and a lycan of the escort, and the lycan of the escort was down on one knee with blood running into his eyes. It was the grey-flecked sentinel, who in all the long road from the Coast had said nothing to Caleb but *Highness*. Caleb walked into the middle of them. He did not hurry. The two rogues saw him come into their light and stopped, as the young one with the knife had stopped, with their claws drawn back and their faces gone uncertain. He stepped round the sentinel and stood in front of him, as he had once stood in front of Paul with the sentinel’s claws wet to the knuckle, and looked at the rogues.

“Good evening,” said Caleb.

They backed away. They went back from him a pace, and then another, as men go back from a lamp that has been knocked over in a hayloft, and the sentinel got to his feet behind Caleb and wiped the blood out of his eyes with the back of his wrist and stared at the back of Caleb’s head.

Caleb went on.

It was a strange walk. He went along the ledge of the Fault through the white light of the blades from one knot of fighting to the next, and wherever he went the fighting stopped. The rogues fell back from him, swearing, as if he were hot to the touch, and the lycans of the escort, who were outnumbered three to one and bleeding, found that wherever he stood they could stand too, and closed up behind him, and came on. A rogue who had a lycan of the escort pinned to the rock wall with a forearm across his throat turned his head at the gold coming nearer and let go, and leapt back, and stood there with his hands spread, like a boy caught with his fingers in the jam. Another, with blood to the elbows that was not his own, ducked his head to him as he went by, as a shepherd of the hill towns ducks his head to the young lord on the road, and then looked so appalled at himself that Caleb almost wished him *good evening* too. Another, with a knife, made the mistake of standing his ground.

The temple physicians had taught Caleb where the hand keeps its strings. There is a place on the inside of the wrist, a thumb’s width below the heel of the palm, where everything that closes the fingers runs together over the bone, and a healer who knows it can make a fist open that has been clenched in pain for a week. Caleb took the rogue’s wrist as the knife came up, and found the place with his thumb, and pressed. The hand opened all at once, as if it had remembered an appointment elsewhere. The knife fell. Caleb picked it up by the blade, which was a thing Matilda would have put him in the mud for, and walked to the edge of the ledge and threw it into the Fault, and turned round, and the rogue was still staring at his own open hand.

Under the cloak the gold had come up in him from the soles of his feet to the tips of his ears, brighter than at any time since the dawn on the high pass, and all of him was warm with it except one place over his heart, where the ring knocked against his breastbone at every turn like a knuckle on a door, and lay as cold as a stone in a stream, and would not take the warmth at all.

He came to the edge of the light, and stopped.

The circle of blades had closed to a dozen paces across. Eleven blades now, for the lycan who had gone over the edge of the ledge had taken his with him, and Caleb could see it far below, a spark going down and down into the gorge and striking the river at last in a long hiss of gold steam. The eleven that were left burned round Arian, held by lycans who had wrapped their faces in rags against the glare and left only slits for their eyes, and in the middle of their circle he stood with steam rising off him and the red come up all along his face and his hands, and his own shadow, alone of all the shadows in the Fault, lying at his feet as short and black as a puddle at noon, because the light came at him from every side at once.

Caleb could have ended it.

He knew that as he stood there. He had in him the whole of three days under the bronze well and the dawn on the waystation step and every hour in the garden on the Coast, stored in the blood and waiting, and he knew what he could do with it if he let it go. He could open his hands and turn the light in him outward, as he had been taught in the nursery, and the Fault would go from this cruel white glare to a true noon from wall to wall, the noon of the Citadel at midsummer, and the lycans would fall down blind with their blades ringing on the stone. And the man in the middle of the circle would burn. That was what dawnbending did. It was the oldest thing the Solar House knew how to do, and it had been made, a thousand years ago, for people like him.

So he did not open his hands.

He closed them. He drew the light in instead, as a man draws in his breath before a long dive, until it lay gathered in the cups of his palms, hot and heavy and humming, and then he did what the nursery tutors had taught him to do with it when he was six. He had used it ever since to throw a patch of sunlight across the floor for Cecily’s cat to chase, and once, unforgivably, into the eyes of a young knight who was beating Matilda at the quintain. He had never done it in earnest. He narrowed the light. He drew the glare of the blades into it too, all the white and gold they were spilling across the rock, and folded it in with his own, and pressed the whole of it down and down between his hands until it was no wider than a thread, and then he opened his fingers a crack and let the thread go.

It went across the circle like a thrown line, and into the eyes of the lycan with the singing blade.

The lycan screamed. His eyes, open in their slit of rag and made for the dark like every lycan’s, took the whole of it at once, and he reeled back with both hands over his face, and the singing blade fell out of his fist and rang on the rock. Caleb turned his hands a little and let the thread go again, and again. He did it as carefully as he had once knitted the torn edges of Paul’s lung in the dark of the lower yard. The thread of light went where he sent it and nowhere else. It did not go near the middle of the circle. It went into one pair of eyes and then the next, and the lycans who had been holding the blades went back from them howling, like owls turned out of a barn at noon, and the circle broke open on one side, and Caleb walked through the gap into the light.

Arian turned.

His face was raw. The light had been at it for too long, and the skin along his cheekbones and his brow had gone from white to red and was beginning to blister at the edges. He watched Caleb come through the gap in the light, and for a moment he did not seem able to speak.

“Go back.”

“No.” Caleb came the last steps and turned, and put his back to Arian’s back, so that they stood in the middle of the road shoulder to shoulder and facing out, with the circle of blades round them and the steam of Arian’s skin going up between them in the glare. The cold of him came through Caleb’s cloak from neck to heel, and the light in Caleb went out to meet it and did not burn. “They will not touch me. They have been told not to. Not a hair of my head, I am told. Someone has been at great pains about my hair. So I shall stand here, and they may try to come at you through me, if they like, and we shall see how they manage it.”

Behind him, Arian was silent.

Round them the blades had stopped. Caleb could see them over his own shoulder, and over Arian’s, eight of them still lit and held out at arm’s length by men with rags across their faces, burning in the night like candles round a bier. And not one of them came on. The lycans who held them stood on the rock with their blades lifted and looked at the gold of the Solar prince standing between them and the Sovereign of the Coast, and looked at one another, and did not know what to do.

“Highness.”

It was the old one with the scar. He had come up along the ledge and stood now at the edge of the circle, between two of the blades, with the light lying white on the scar that ran into his hair. He was not smiling any more.

“Highness. Come away from him.”

“I think not.”

“You’ll only be hurt.”

“I was given to understand that I would not be.”

“Not by us.” The lycan took a step into the circle. “But he’ll burn when the light gets into him, Highness, and he’ll thrash when he burns, and I can’t answer for what he’ll do to you then, and neither can you. Come away. There’s nothing for you here.” He took another step, and another, and put out his hand, quite slowly, and took Caleb by the arm above the elbow, as a man takes a child’s arm to walk him across a busy road.

Caleb put the whole of the light in his right hand into his eyes from a hand’s breadth away.

The lycan let go. He went back with a roar, both hands to his face, and stumbled into the circle and out of it and fell to his knees on the rock, and the blades nearest him swung wildly as their holders flinched away from him, and in the swinging light all the pinned shadows on the walls of the Fault jerked and shivered on their lines.

He did not see the first of the blinded get up.

It was the lean one with the torn ear, whose singing blade had fallen from his fist at the first thread of light. He had been on his knees at the edge of the circle all this while with his hands over his face and the tears of the glare running between his fingers, and now he took his hands away, and his eyes were open, and they were blind. They were filmed and white and streaming, and they could see nothing in the world but light. He had found his blade again. He had found it by its singing, and by its warmth on the rock, and it was singing still.

He turned his blind white eyes across the circle, and they found the brightest thing in the gorge after the blades. They found the gold of Caleb, blazing in the middle of the road.

“*Him*.” His voice was thick with weeping. “It’s him did it. He’s put my eyes out. Hang the Don, and hang the Don’s orders.” He came up off the rock. “I don’t need eyes to find you, sun boy. You *shine*.”

He came across the circle at a run.

Caleb saw him come and could not move, for there was no time, the way there is no time when a cup is knocked off a table and one watches it fall. He saw the singing blade come up, white and gold. He saw the blind face behind it, wet with tears. He thought, quite clearly, *it will only cut me*, and that it would hurt a great deal, and that he would need to sit down afterwards.

He did not see Arian move. Nobody could have seen it.

There was only the cold, all at once, all down his front instead of his back, and a black coat where the blade had been, and a sound. It was a short sound, a hiss, the sound a poker makes when it is laid down hot in the snow, and it came from very close to him, from a body pressed against his so hard that he could feel the ribs of it, and then the blade broke. He heard it break, a high bright crack like ice on a pond in the first hard frost, and the singing stopped.

Something went into Caleb under his own ribs on the left side, deep, and turned there.

There was no wound. He knew there was no wound. He felt the place anyway, a white burning point beneath the bone, and he felt the thread that had been drawn tight through his chest at the second stroke of Hild’s hammer pull so taut that it rang, from the black ring on his hand through his chest and out across the hand’s breadth of air to the man in front of him, and on the far end of that thread something had been struck, and the blow came back along it into Caleb, and he gasped as if it had been his.

The blind lycan staggered away from them, backwards, with his fist held up in front of his face. In it was the black iron hilt of the blade, and a stub of light no longer than a thumb, flickering, going out.

The rest of it was not anywhere that Caleb could see.

Arian stood very still with his back to Caleb and his left hand pressed flat to his side, just under the ribs. Between his fingers a light was coming out. It was white and gold, very faint, like the light along the seams of a lid in the dark of a ship’s hold.

Then he turned his head, and Caleb saw his eyes.

In the black gallery there had been only a rim of crimson round them. Now the crimson came up from the black of his eyes as wine comes up through a white napkin, and filled them, and went on filling them, until there was no black left in them at all, nor any white, only red, a deep clear burning red with nothing behind it, and they looked out of his raw white face at the Fault.

The steam stopped rising off him. Frost came down on his shoulders again, all at once, thick and white, as if the night had been waiting.

\* \* \*

The shadows came down off the walls of the Fault.

They had hung there all this while, pinned flat and thin in the glare like washing on a line, and now they tore free. Caleb saw them go. He saw every one of them lift from the striped rock at once, the shadow of every wheel and stone and lycan and of the ledge itself, and come down the walls of the gorge the way the sea comes up a beach when a great wave has drawn back first, slow at the top and then not slow at all. They poured, and they poured towards Arian, and they did not stop when they reached him. They went on up into him and out of him, and came back out of him larger.

The light of the blades went down before them.

The dark put it out, and Caleb watched it happen, and could not have said afterwards how it was done. The eight blades still lifted in the circle burned as fiercely as they had, white and gold, and the dark came over them all the same, and the white of them went down under it to gold, and the gold to a dull red, like the coals of a hearth with a black cloth laid over them. The lycans who held them stared at their own fists. One of them shook his blade, as a man shakes a lantern that is guttering, and the dark closed over the blade and the hand and the arm to the shoulder. The frost went up his sleeve. He cried out, and the cry stopped, and he looked down at his arm, which had gone white and glittering to the shoulder. Then it broke at the elbow under its own weight, cleanly, as a frozen reed breaks when a heron comes down on it, and fell on the rock with the blade still in its fist. Nothing came out of the place where it had been. There was nothing left in him that would run.

Above, along the top of the Fault, the stars went out.

They went from the east, one after another, all along the ribbon of sky, as if someone were walking the length of a great hall at the end of a feast and pinching out the candles as he went. The river below went black and then silent. Caleb heard it stop. The long pour of it that had filled the gorge since midnight simply was not there any more, and in its place there came a pressure, a great soft weight of silence that bore down on the ledge from every side, so that the air tasted of nothing and the breath went into his chest and found nothing in it to take.

It was beautiful.

He would think so for the rest of his life, and never tell anyone. There were colours in it, violet and indigo and the green-black of deep water under a hull, and it moved like silk drawn slowly through a ring, and all through it the frost was forming. It came out of the air itself in the dark, a glitter at a time, white points of it hanging and turning, thousands and tens of thousands of them, so that the night on the ledge of the Fault was full of a second sky, and the stars of it were cold, and they drifted.

And it went over the rogues of the Ashward.

It took the dead first, where they lay at the foot of the wall and in the road, whole or in pieces, and the third ink went from the striped rock as if the Holds had only that morning cut it clean.

Then it took the living. It took them where they stood, and it took them where they ran, the ones that ran, and two did, along the ledge towards the buttress, and the dark went after them along the rock without hurry and was there before them. There was no sound, for nothing was left in the gorge to carry one, and Caleb would have given anything he owned for a sound. The first of the two runners stopped dead, and the frost came up out of him from the inside, out at his mouth and the corners of his eyes in a white breath that did not drift away, and the blood in him went to ice in all its courses at once. It burst him, as a stone jar of water left on a sill bursts in the first hard night of the winter, and he came down on the rock in pieces that rang, and Caleb could not hear them ring. The second runner turned at the buttress and went down on his knees with his empty hands held up and his mouth open on a word, and the dark did not stop for that either.

The young one who had begged Caleb’s pardon stood where he was. His lips were moving on the same two words, over and over, in the Wildmarch tongue, which Caleb did not know, and he knew them. The dark went over him, and his shadow was pulled up off the rock from under his feet and went into the dark with the rest, and what had stood upon it, with nothing left beneath it, sank down into itself as a snowman sinks when the thaw gets into it, and went to frost, and the dark took the frost.

The big lycan with the grey beard was still on his knees where Caleb’s light had put him, with his hands over his burned eyes. He could not have seen the dark come. He felt it. Caleb saw him feel it, the rime gathering white in his beard and along the scar to his hairline, and saw him lift his blind head and turn it, away from the Sovereign and away from the dark, towards the one warm thing on the ledge. His lips moved in the frost of his beard. There was no air to carry it. But Caleb had sat at a great many bedsides, and he could read a mouth.

*Shut your eyes, Highness.*

Caleb did not shut his eyes.

It took the old lycan kneeling, with his face turned to the gold. It took the eight who held the blades in the circle, one after another round the curve of it, and the blind one last of all, with the stub of the singing blade still in his fist. And Arian did not look at any of them. His red eyes were on the place where the stars had been, and he stood in the middle of the dark and let it do as it liked, with no more interest in what it did than a late frost has in which of the blossoms it takes. However each man had gone, the end of him was the same. Where the dark went over a man there was a man, and then the dark went on, and there was frost on the rock in the place where he had been, in the print of his feet, and nothing else at all, as a clerk strikes one clean line through a debt that has been paid in full, and the line is all that is left on the page.

The blades fell. They fell out of nothing onto the frost, one after another, ten of them and a broken hilt, and lay where they fell, and went on glowing under the dark like embers dropped in snow.

Then there were no rogues left on the ledge, and the dark did not stop.

It came on along the road. It came round the carriages and over the fallen stones and along the ledge towards the lycans of the escort, who had been fighting a moment ago and were standing now, all of them, frozen where the end of the fight had left them, with the frost settling on their shoulders and their faces turned up to the place where the stars had been. Caleb saw the nearest of them go down on one knee with his hand at his throat and his lips gone blue. The blood of the scalp cut on the next had frozen on his face where it ran, in a red glaze like the sugar on a Citadel fruit tart. The grey-flecked sentinel went down beside them, and lifted his head, and his mouth shaped *Sovereign* at the dark, as a man calls a name into a burning house when he already knows that nobody will answer. The dark did not so much as turn. Under the third carriage the violet of the engine shrank to a glimmer, and the iron of its wheels went white to the hub. Further back, by the wheel of the second carriage, a pale shape that was Mirek had lifted itself on one elbow, and a bigger shape beyond it was coming along the ledge from the last carriage at a stumbling run, and Caleb knew the run.

*He’ll burn when the light gets into him, Highness, and he’ll thrash when he burns.*

Caleb turned round.

Arian was standing where he had stood, two paces off. The dark came out of him and went round him and back into him, as the tide goes round the foot of a lighthouse, and frost had grown on him so thick that his hair was white with it, and his shoulders, and his sleeves. His left hand was still pressed to his side. The light came out between the fingers, faint and white and gold, the only light left in the Fault. His right hand hung at his side, closed. It was clenched until the knuckles showed through the skin like the teeth of a comb, with the bare finger folded in among the rest, and it shook, finely and without stopping, from the wrist down.

His eyes were red from rim to rim, and there was nobody in them.

The dark leaned towards Caleb. It had kept its distance from him all this while, and not one frost star had settled on him. Now it came close and curled at him. It came about his knees and his hands, cool and soft as the inside of a cat’s ear, and it nosed at the breast of his coat, at the stitched place over his heart, and lingered there, as if it had been called.

The physicians of the Harp Vales had made him learn one rule above all the rest, that no vampire was to be touched for any reason. He had broken it twice already with his hands, once at the garden doors, for physic, and once in a carriage in a glowing mist, for no reason any physician would allow.

He took the two paces. He laid his hand over Arian’s fist.

The cold of it went up his arm to the shoulder and set every tooth in his head aching. It was colder than iron left out all night on the high passes. His palm stuck to it, as a wet hand sticks to the iron of a yard pump in the deep of winter, and his skin began to burn, and under it the shaking ran on and came up into his own hand. Beneath the heel of his hand he could feel the bare finger, and the place at the root of it where a ring had worn it smooth.

He held on.

“Sovereign,” he whispered.

The silence, which had let nothing else through, let that through.

Nothing moved in the red eyes.

“Look at me.”

He had heard those words once before, on the floor of the black gallery with the fire climbing in his ribs, from a white face bent over him in the dark. He said them now as they had been said to him, without heat, as a thing that would be done.

“Look at me,” said Caleb.

Arian looked at him.

The red eyes came round from the empty sky and down, slowly, as if they were coming a long way, and found Caleb’s face, and stayed. Caleb did not know what they saw there. He knew what his own face felt like from the inside, which was cold, and stiff, and wet at the cheeks with something that had not been there a moment ago, and lit gold from beneath the skin, so bright that he could see his own light lying on the frost of Arian’s collar.

Under his hand, the fist stayed shut. The shaking in it went on in his own hand too, and he let it.

He had nothing more to say. He stood on the ledge of the Fault in that dark, with his hand over a vampire’s fist and his light lying on a vampire’s frost, and he waited, as one waits at the bedside when everything that can be done has been done, and there is nothing left but to be there.

The first star came back.

It came out over the western rim of the Fault, in the ribbon of sky, faint and white and alone. Then another came out beside it, and another, back along the ribbon the way they had gone, as if the man who had pinched out the candles at the end of the feast had thought better of it and was coming back down the hall with a taper. Far below, the river found its voice again in the middle of a word, and came pouring up the walls of the gorge. The pressure lifted. Air came back into the air. Along the ledge someone drew a great dragging breath, and then another, and somebody began to cough. Where the gold of Caleb’s skin reached along the rock, the frost on the sergeant’s shoulders went to water and ran, and the lycan with the red glaze on his face put up his hand and found it wet, and began to laugh into his fingers, helplessly, as men laugh at a wake.

And under Caleb’s hand, slowly, the fingers began to give.

They gave as they had given in the carriage at Old Meridian, one at a time, but slower now, much slower, with a fight in every one of them, as if each were being drawn out of a vice. The bare finger came last. It lay out straight along the back of Caleb’s hand when it was done, white and smooth and empty, and the tremor went out of the hand, and it lay open and cold under his own.

The dark went back into the walls of the gorge, and lay down along the bands of the rock where it belonged, and was only shadow.

Caleb looked up. The red was going out of Arian’s eyes. It went out of them as it had come in, from the edges first, as colour goes out of linen laid to bleach on the grass, until there was a rim of it, and then a thread, and then there was only black, and the white round it was white, and they were looking at Caleb from a face so raw and so white and so tired that he hardly knew it.

“Are you hurt?” asked Arian.

His voice came out rough and low, the voice of a man who has been shouting for a long time, though he had not made a sound.

“No.”

Under his palm the open hand began to turn, as if to take hold of his, and stopped.

“Not so much as a bruise,” said Caleb. His own voice was not doing as it was told. “I was assured of it. Several times.”

Arian let out a breath. It went up white between them, and Caleb watched it go and could not understand why, until he understood that he had never once seen Arian’s breath before, on any night, in any cold, and that this one was warm.

“Why did you do that? The blade. You stepped in front of it. Why?”

Arian said nothing.

“It was *light*.” It came out of Caleb much louder than he meant it to, and along the ledge one of the lycans of the escort, getting to his feet, flinched and looked round. Caleb did not care. “It was a sliver of sunlight on a stick. It would have cut me, and I should have bled, and complained a great deal, and healed it in an afternoon. It would have done me no more harm than a carving knife. You knew that. You must have known it. And you stood in front of it.”

Arian was quiet, in the starlight that was coming back.

“It would have cut you.”

Caleb opened his mouth. Nothing came out of it. He stood on the frost with his hand still lying over the back of Arian’s open hand, and found that it had been so long since he had been unable to answer anybody that he did not know how it was done, and he had to look down at the frost until his face was his own again.

Arian drew his hand out from under Caleb’s, gently, and turned to the road.

“Sergeant.” The grey-flecked sentinel came up out of the frost on one knee and onto his feet. “Count them.”

He walked away along the ledge towards the carriages, straight-backed and unhurried, with his left hand flat against his coat, through the frost where the prints were, and did not look down at them.

\* \* \*

Caleb stood among the blades.

They lay all round him in the frost where they had fallen out of the dark, ten of them and the broken hilt, and their light had come back as the stars came back. It was gold again, and white at the edges, and it lay on the frost in long bright pools and made the rime on the rock glitter, so that he stood in a noon of their making in the middle of the night, ankle-deep in light, with no one left to hold it.

Nothing of the rogues was left at all. He had seen their blood go up the wall of the gorge, and the wall was clean. The frost held the prints of their feet, here and there, the long lycan prints with the claw marks at the toes, and in one place two knees, and in another the scrape of something that had been dragged, and that was all. Their knives and their ropes and their buckles and the rags they had wrapped round their faces were gone with them. Only the blades lay there, because the blades had never been theirs.

He looked down at the nearest. It was one of the longer ones. In the light that came off it he could see the hilt clearly for the first time, black cold-iron cut all over with runes, square and neat, and something about the cutting of them caught at him and would not let go, a tilt to the crossbars, a flourish at the end of the long strokes that no dwarf would ever have put there. The blade itself lay in its claws of iron like a long tear of honey that had set. It was warm. He could feel the warmth of it on his cheek, and the gold under his skin leaned down to it, and he held himself upright and did not let it.

He touched none of them.

Down the ledge, the sentinel was counting. Caleb heard him do it in a low voice, name by name, in the Wildmarch tongue, and every name had an answer, though some were faint. Three of the escort had wounds that would keep them off a roof for a month, and a fourth had a scalp cut that bled all down his face and made him look a great deal nearer death than he was. Nobody was dead. The sentinel came to the end of the names and said something under his breath that sounded like a prayer, and then, in Alorian, louder, “They were Ashward men, Sovereign. I knew two of them. One of them was at my wedding.”

If Arian answered him, Caleb did not hear it.

Then Paul came along the ledge with Mirek in his arms.

He carried him as he had once carried Caleb up a stair at the age of fourteen with a turned ankle, high against his chest, with the boy’s broken leg laid along his forearm as a man carries a pane of glass. Mirek’s face was grey and running with sweat, and he had his teeth set in his lip. Paul set him down on the step of the third carriage, slowly, and stayed bent over him a moment with his big hands still under the boy’s shoulders. Then he straightened, and turned, and saw the blades.

Caleb had seen Paul white once before, on the black stones of the lower yard. He had not been as white as this. The glow of the blades lay on his face from below, and there was no colour left in it to meet the light. He stood on the ledge and looked down at the blades, all ten and the broken hilt, and his lips moved without any sound coming from them.

“Paul,” said Caleb. “Are you hurt?”

Paul’s eyes came up to him. It took them a long time.

“No, Highness.” His voice scraped. “Are you?”

“Not in the least. Not a hair of my head.” Caleb tried to smile at him and found that his face would not do it properly. “They were most careful of my hair. I found it rather insulting.”

Paul flinched.

He flinched the whole length of his body, as a man flinches from a blow he has been waiting for, and then he looked down at the frost between his boots, and stayed looking at it.

Caleb would have gone to him. He had taken a step to go. But beyond Paul, at the door of the second carriage, he saw Arian standing in the shadow of it, quite still, with his face turned towards them. He was looking at Paul, without any expression at all, in the light of the blades. Paul did not look up.

Caleb thought he understood. Paul had been sworn to him since Caleb was fourteen, by his own oath and his family’s six generations, and when the blades came out of the dark he had been at the far end of the convoy in the last carriage, with the baggage, and had not come in time. Caleb knew Paul. He knew Paul would carry that like a stone in his chest for a year.

He went to Mirek instead.

The boy was trying not to cry, and nearly managing it. He had lost his cap and his sword and most of the colour in his face, and the leg lay on the step of the carriage with the boot still on it, turned at the shin. Caleb knelt beside it on the frost. He did not need to take the boot off to know. He had set worse in the Harp Vales and on younger patients, though never, he thought, on one who looked at him the way Mirek was looking at him now, as if he had come down out of the sky.

“You were very brave. And very foolish. And the two are very often the same, and your mother will tell you so at great length, and I shall stand behind her and agree with every word.”

“He took my sword off me, Highness.” Mirek’s voice was thin. “And I’d been practising.”

“I know. I saw. You were splendid.” Caleb laid his hands on either side of the break. “*Prächtig*. Hold still.”

His hands were warm already. The light had gathered in the palms at the first sight of the leg, as it always gathered, and it pressed at his skin now, eager, ready to go in and find the bone and coax it home. He let it come down into his fingers.

Mirek caught his wrist.

He did it with his bandaged hand, the one he had cut practising with the sword before they ever left the Coast, and his grip was weak and shaking and would not let go.

“Not me, Highness. Him.”

“Mirek —”

“I can smell it.” The boy’s eyes had gone past Caleb’s shoulder, to the door of the second carriage, and his face had changed. All his lycan blood was in it, and he looked for a moment much older than sixteen. “He’s burning. I could smell it from the wheel. It’s inside him, Highness. It’s still burning.”

Caleb turned his head.

\* \* \*

Arian was sitting on the step of the carriage door, in the shadow of the carriage, with his back against the frame.

He had not been sitting a moment before. Caleb was nearly sure of it. He was sitting now as if he had meant to, with his long legs out on the frost and his head back against the black lacquer and his eyes closed, and his left hand still pressed to his side. The frost had gone from his hair. It had gone from his shoulders and the black of his coat. There was no frost anywhere on him at all, and the rime on the step he was sitting on had melted in a dark ring round him, and was steaming, faintly, in the cold.

Between the fingers of his left hand, the light was coming out.

It was brighter than it had been. It came out white and gold between his knuckles and lay along the black cloth of his coat, and Caleb saw, as he came across the frost and went down on his knees on the step beside him, that it had spread. It was in the fingers now as well as between them. Under the white skin of the hand, along the backs of the fingers and over the knuckles and up into the wrist, there were lines of gold, fine as hairs, branching, the way the cracks run through the glaze of a pot when it is held up close to a lamp, and they were moving. They were growing. While he watched, one of them reached the edge of the black cuff and went on under it.

“Let me see,” said Caleb.

Arian opened his eyes. “It is nothing.”

“Then it will not trouble you in the least to show me.”

Arian looked at him. Then, after a moment, he took his hand away.

Caleb opened the black coat, and the black silk beneath it, and put back the silk.

The wound was a hand’s breadth below the heart, on the left side, under the last of the ribs. It was neat, and no longer than the top joint of his thumb, and it was not bleeding. It was shining. The mouth of it was lit from inside, gold and white, as a keyhole is lit when there is a lamp burning in the room behind the door, and through the white skin all round it Caleb could see what was in there, deep in, a long sliver of light lying slantwise under the ribs, burning. From it the gold went out under the skin in every direction, in those fine branching lines, up over the ribs towards the heart and down across the belly and round under the arm, and where the lines went, the white skin had flushed faintly pink.

He had never seen Arian flush.

He laid two fingers flat beside the wound, as he had been taught, to feel the heat of it. The skin under his fingers was warm. It was warm as Caleb’s own skin was warm, as a living man’s is, and for one moment the warmth of it so astonished his hand that his hand did not understand what it was touching.

“The blade broke,” he said. His voice was a physician’s voice now, flat and level, and he was grateful to it. “The rest of it is in you. It is lying under the ribs here, and it has nothing round it now to hold it, no hilt and no runes, and it is coming undone.” He heard himself go on, very calm, as he would have gone on to a novice at the bedside. “It is giving itself up into you. A little at a time. It is light, and it is in your blood, and it is going wherever your blood goes.”

“Yes.”

“You knew.”

“I felt it break.”

Caleb sat back on his heels on the cold step.

He had the primer by heart from the dark of the east wing, and a decade of the Harp Vales in his hands, and he knew what he was looking at. The physicians had taught him what Solar light did when it got into Umbral blood on the day after they taught him never to touch a vampire, and they had taught him that it did not stop.

Under his knees the step was cold. All round him the Fault was cold, the frost and the rock and the night air coming up off the river. And in the east, behind the buttress, beyond the fallen stones and the dark road and the striped walls of the gorge, he could feel it, faint and sure, the warm pressure at the edge of the world that he had felt on the high pass on the morning of his first dawn, and every morning since.

It was coming. It was perhaps an hour away. Less.

Arian had closed his eyes again. His head lay back against the black lacquer of the door, and the gold went on under his skin, and he lay in the doorway of the carriage, very still, the way he had stood still in the doorway of the garden with the light lying at his feet. After a while he lifted his left hand from the step and laid it flat on his own chest, over the lines of gold, as he had laid it on the far side of the anvil in the Hall of the Chain, and left it there.

“It is warm,” he said.

He said it as he had said *They were very bright*, quietly and without weight, and his face under the burns gave nothing away. But the long fingers on his chest had spread a little over the gold, as a man’s fingers spread to a hearth when he has come in from the cold, and Caleb had held his own hands so once, in the snow below the waystation on the high pass, with his arms out to the dawn.

Caleb knelt on the step with his throat shut, and looked at him sitting in the cold with his hand on his own heart and the sun inside him, and could not say a single word.

The sentinel came up along the ledge behind him, and stopped at a distance, and went down on one knee in the frost.

“Sovereign. The road’s shut ahead. The first carriage is under half the buttress, and there’s rock on the ledge past it from wall to wall. It’ll be a day’s work to clear, and the waystation’s at the rim beyond it.” He looked east, as Caleb had looked, and seemed to feel it too. “It’ll be light in an hour, Sovereign. There’s no shutter in the Fault.”

Arian did not open his eyes.

Caleb turned on the step. “Is there anywhere? Anywhere at all. Under the rock.”

The sentinel looked at him. He looked at Caleb for some time, kneeling there on the frost with the light still coming and going under his skin and his face wet, and then he looked past him at the Sovereign in the doorway, and something in the old grey-flecked face made up its mind.

“There are caves, Highness. In the north wall, back the way we came. A quarter mile, and down. The shepherds of the hill towns winter their flocks in them when the snow comes early. I was born three valleys over. I’ve slept in them.” He hesitated. “They go back a long way into the mountain. There’s no light reaches the back of them. Not ever.”

“Then we go there,” said Caleb. “Now.”

The sentinel looked at the Sovereign.

“Do as the prince says,” said Arian, with his eyes shut.

After that it was all done very fast, and Caleb was afterwards unable to remember the order of it. The lycans who could walk were on their feet. Two of them took the wounded, and Paul took Mirek up again, and someone brought the lamps from the last carriage and shuttered them down to a glow, and the sentinel gathered the blades from the frost by their cold-iron hilts one by one, holding each at arm’s length, and rolled them in the grey velvet rug from Caleb’s carriage, and the broken hilt with them. And Caleb unfastened his travelling cloak from his throat and shook out the frost from it, and when Arian got up off the step, which he did by himself, slowly, with a hand on the doorframe, Caleb put the cloak over his head and shoulders as if he were a child coming in out of the rain, and drew it close under his chin.

He had been given a coat once, in a carriage in the dark, and had kept it for as long as he decently could.

“It is not your colour. I am aware. You must bear it.”

Arian said nothing. But when they went along the ledge, back the way they had come, between the high striped walls of the gorge and above the voice of the river, he walked with his shoulder against Caleb’s, and at the first turn of the road he leaned. He did not lean much. Caleb doubted that anyone but himself would have known it was a lean at all. But the weight of him came onto Caleb’s shoulder and stayed, and Caleb put his arm round him under the cloak, round the narrow hard waist under the black coat, where the skin was warm through the silk, and held him up.

Arian’s right hand came up as they walked, and found the front of Caleb’s coat, and took hold of it.

It took hold of the left breast of it, over the heart, as a man takes hold of anything near him when the ground is uneven and he does not mean to fall. The long bare fingers closed in the silk. Under them, under the silk and the lining and Vesna’s black thread, lay the ring that belonged on one of those fingers, and the fingers closed round it without knowing it was there, and held on.

Caleb did not breathe for ten paces.

The hand kept still. It held, without testing the cloth or feeling for what lay under it, the way a stair rail is held in the dark, and after ten paces Caleb let his breath go, without a sound, and walked on, with the Sovereign of the Coast leaning on his shoulder and the Sovereign’s ring between them and the gold of the sun running in the Sovereign’s veins where Caleb’s arm lay round him, warm, and warmer, under the cloak.

Behind them, in the rug in the sentinel’s arms, ten blades and a broken hilt went on burning through the pile, so that the old lycan walked in a faint gold glow of his own, with his face turned away from it.

Above them, along the ribbon of sky at the top of the Fault, the stars had begun, one by one, to go out.
