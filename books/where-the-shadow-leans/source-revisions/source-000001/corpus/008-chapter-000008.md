## Chapter 7: Chapter Six - The Broker's Gambit

*Appendix C. Crystals of deep-trench Aether consigned for the Citadel wharves in the present quarter and withheld, with the Treasury’s reckoning of the loss to the Crown. The first, four hundred and twelve crowns and nineteen pence. The second, three hundred and eighty-eight crowns and one penny. The third, four hundred and five crowns and six pence. The fourth, four hundred and ten crowns and five pence.*

— From the formal complaint of the Office of the Arch-Chancellor of Aloria to the Sovereign of Vesperia

\* \* \*

The fae came back seven days after the betrothal. Nobody had sent for her.

Mirek brought the news up with the breakfast tray, still out of breath from the stairs. She had come by the fae roads through the mists of the Marches, which run shorter for the fae than for anybody else and which nobody else has ever found, and ridden the last of the way on a hired mule, soaked through, with a satchel of dispatches over her shoulder and her copper coin singing in her pocket, and she was at the gate now, asking the captain whether the Sovereign would see her. She had also asked whether the Solar prince was well.

“Her name’s Oriane de Chardon,” Mirek said. “She’s an informant. The Council pays her for news from the Marches, and she writes for the *Lantern* as well, only she never signs it. My mother says she’s been coming here since before the Sovereign was born. She’s never told a lie in her life, and nobody trusts a word she says.”

“How very efficient of her.” Caleb buttered his toast. “I have met a great many people at court who managed only the second half.”

Beyond the smoked glass the Coast lay in its violet twilight, as it did at breakfast and at supper and at every hour between, and the steam from his tea climbed straight up in the still air of the room and lost itself in the dark under the ceiling.

He had been Arian’s betrothed for a week. A good deal had changed in seven days, and most of it without anyone saying a word.

The lycans at the arches no longer merely straightened when he passed. They put their fists to their hearts, and the soft thump of knuckles on leather followed him from arch to arch like a drum beaten slow for a procession of one. The vampire clerks bowed in a whisper of black silk. An archivist in the east library, with ink on his fingers and eyes older than his shelves, had risen from his desk in a creak of old wood and a breath of vellum and lamp oil, and offered Caleb his own chair, and a volume of Umbral genealogy he had evidently been saving for the occasion. The fortress had begun, in its cold and ceremonious way, to take an interest.

And on the morning after the betrothal a second chair had appeared in the great hall.

It stood at the Sovereign’s right hand, a step below the high seat and a step above the Nine, cut from the same black volcanic glass as everything else in Castel Nerezza and polished so deep that the violet lamps seemed to burn far down inside it. It had no cushion and no ornament. Nobody told Caleb it was his. He had seen it through the open doors of the hall on his way to the garden, empty and catching the light, and he had sat on the water-stair with the water running cool past his shoes and thought about it until the crystals overhead had turned a full circle on their chains.

He had not sat in it yet, because nobody had asked him to.

When Enzo came for him that morning, a little before the Council was due to sit, he understood that he was being asked now.

\* \* \*

The great stair down to the hall was cut from the living rock and lit at every landing by a violet lamp in an iron cage, and it grew colder at every turn, the way a well grows colder as one goes down. By the third landing the ivory silk lay against his arms as cool as linen fetched in off a frosted line, and above and below him the caged lamps hung in their row like patient captive moons.

“It is not required,” said Enzo, on the stairs. “There are consorts in the histories who never set foot in the great hall, and were thought none the worse for it.”

“And there are others, I imagine, who set foot in it rather too often, and were thought a great deal the worse.” Caleb had put on the ivory silk this morning, with the silver frogging, and had spent some time on his hair. “Which am I to be, Consigliere?”

“That,” said Enzo dryly, “is rather the point of the exercise.”

The ash had gone from the Consigliere’s brow since the month-day, and he was back in his dove grey with a sheaf of papers under his arm. Since the lamp room he spoke to Caleb with a new ease, as dry as ever and with no edge in it. Caleb had yet to decide what to call it. He suspected that Enzo had not either.

“Does he know I am coming?”

“He asked for you.”

Caleb stopped on the stair.

His hand found the rail. The iron was cold under his palm, and his heart gave one hard knock against his ribs and went on faster than before.

Enzo went on down without waiting, so that Caleb had to let go of the rail and hurry after him. “*Ask him. Do not tell him.* His words. You may decline.”

“And if I do?”

“Then the chair stays empty.” Enzo stopped at the foot of the stair, with his hand on the great door, and did not open it. “He will not ask twice. He very seldom asks once, Highness. I could count the occasions on one hand.”

Caleb stood on the last step.

The last person to put anything to him before he came to the Coast had been his mother’s chamberlain, and the chamberlain had pressed a glass of Citadel white into his hand and watched him drink it. Since then Caleb had been carried, and kept, and called into the great hall on a card that gave him his full title and then, in four words, told him what it was worth. In Aloria no one but the King and Queen summoned a prince of the Solar House. He had worn his coronation silk to that hall a week ago to remind them what he was, and had walked in and let every one of them stare. In seven weeks on the Coast he had been asked one question worth the name, across a candlelit study, and he had answered *No*.

And now the one who had asked it was asking again.

Beyond the door he could hear the Council, the low murmur of nine voices and the scrape of chairs, and under it a silence he had learned to know, which was Arian not yet speaking, and a thin line of cold came under the door and lay across the toes of his shoes as neat as a rime of frost along a sill.

“Open it.”

\* \* \*

The doors swung inward on their great hinges, and the cold of the hall came out to meet him, smelling of stone and candle wax and, faintly, of blood-wine warming below. It was lit as it was on ordinary mornings, by the violet lamps on their long chains and a single row of candles in iron stands down either wall, every flame standing straight and still in the windless cold, as if it had been painted there by an artist who did not hold with draughts. The talk went quiet when he came in, and the quiet lay cold on the back of his neck.

He took his time. A week ago he had stood forty paces from the high seat and watched the Sovereign come the whole length of this hall to him. Now he went the other way, alone, with his hands clasped behind his back so that nobody would see what they were doing. His heels rang on the black glass floor, and every footfall went up into the vaulting and came back down a little altered, so that he seemed to walk in step with a second Caleb a pace behind. He walked past the Nine in their high-backed chairs and felt their eyes go along his spine one after another.

Some of the men in those chairs had stood in this hall seven weeks ago and heard the Alorian prince blamed for the forty-one dead at the Sanctuary, Donna Livia among them. They believed it still, whatever the Sovereign said. If he faltered now, if he so much as glanced up at the dais for leave, it would be said in every house on the Coast before nightfall that the Sovereign’s sun prince did not know where to sit.

He looked at none of them, and not at the dais. He looked at the chair.

It waited at the foot of the high seat and seemed to grow larger with every pace. When he reached it he turned and sat down without the least hesitation, and settled his cuffs.

The glass was cold. It came straight through the ivory silk, as cold as the floor of the black gallery had been under his back, and for one breath every muscle he had went tight. It passed. He laid his hands along the arms of the chair, where no hand had rested before his, and waited for the glass to take his warmth, and it did. Far down in the black glass under his palms, where the violet of the lamps burned, a thread of gold had begun to burn beside it.

Then he let himself look up.

Arian was watching him. He looked for a moment, without a smile or a nod, and turned his eyes back to the hall. But the shadows around the high seat, which had been leaning towards the door ever since Caleb came through it, settled all at once, like a line of soldiers given the order to stand easy.

Don Salvatore Caruso, in his high-backed chair halfway down the hall, turned the garnet ring on his finger so that the stone winked in the lamplight like a drop of wine spilled on a white cloth, and smiled at nothing.

“Consigliere,” said Arian. “Begin.”

\* \* \*

The morning was long, and given over almost wholly to arithmetic.

Caleb had endured such mornings beside his father’s chair for most of his life, with his face arranged into courteous interest and his mind gone wherever it pleased. This morning it stayed in the hall, with the secretaries’ pens scratching below the dais and the violet lamps ticking faintly on their chains as the air moved round them, and the candle smoke going up from the walls in grey threads to lose itself in the dark of the vaulting. One of the Nine, a lord of the inland vineyards with an ancient voice and a velvet coat the colour of a mulberry stain, had been making the same point since the second bell, and the secretaries had quietly stopped writing it down. Once, far beneath the floor, a cask knocked dully as it was shifted, and he thought of the old vent in the rock and whoever might be standing at the bottom of it, and was obscurely comforted.

He listened to Enzo read the ledgers and to the Nine argue over them, and found that he could follow nearly all of it. The parts he could not follow were the parts where the Nine argued about one matter while pretending to argue about another. He could tell which of them were frightened and which had already been bought, and to his considerable surprise he was enjoying himself.

The debt to Durnhollow still stood, and still fell due at the new moon, and the Ashward still held every note of it. Nobody said so aloud. The Consigliere proposed an arrangement with the Lowmere houses that would defer the payment by a season, and the Council argued about it for an hour. Don Salvatore sat through the whole hour in pleasant silence, and when it came to the vote he voted for the arrangement, which made three of the others change their minds.

Then a secretary came up the hall and spoke low in Enzo’s ear, and Enzo looked up at the high seat and announced, in a voice with no expression in it, “The informant from the Marches, Sovereign. She brings word of the Radiant Guard.”

Caleb kept his face where it was. His fingers had tightened on the arm of the chair, and he made them loosen, one at a time.

“Let her come,” answered Arian.

\* \* \*

Oriane de Chardon came up the great hall as if it were a ballroom and she the only guest who had been told the dancing had begun.

She was not tall, for a fae, and not young, though her face had never been told so. Her skin was the warm brown of polished walnut, her eyes a clear and startling green, and she wore the coat of iridescent feathers she had worn on Caleb’s first night, so that every time she moved the whole of her shimmered from bronze to violet to peacock blue and back again. The rain of the Marches was still in her hair, beaded on it like seed pearls, and she brought the smell of it in with her, wet feathers and cold road and mule.

The copper coin walked across her knuckles as she came, chiming at every turn, and at the foot of the dais she caught it in her fist and swept the Sovereign a curtsey so deep and so fluid that it came up on the other side almost as a dance.

Then she turned, found Caleb in his glass chair, and clapped her hands together once, soundlessly, in pure delight.

“*Oh*. How *lovely*. Highness, you are sitting.”

“It seemed a shame to waste the chair.”

“It would have been a crime.” She savoured the word like a sugared almond. “A great many people would have had a great deal to say about it. I should have had a great deal to say about it myself, and I should have been paid handsomely for every word.” She sighed, happily, and turned back to the high seat. “Sovereign. I bring you news from the Marches, and none of it good. Shall I tell it all at once, or would you prefer it in instalments?”

“All at once.”

“You were always a poor audience,” said Oriane fondly, and unslung her satchel.

She told it well. Caleb had read fae broadsheets with his breakfast since he was twelve, and he knew the manner of it, the lightness and the speed, every sentence running downhill towards the next. She stood at the foot of the dais with the satchel open at her hip and the coin closed in her fist, and laid the news before the Council piece by piece, like cards turned face up on a table.

The High Paladin of Aloria, she said, had moved every banner she might lawfully command into the Eclipse Marches, and had lately moved the foremost of them to within a single day’s march of the Coast’s own border posts.

The Coast’s post at the Col de Serre, which watched the high road from the Marches into the Vesperian foothills, had sent no word in four days.

And three nights ago, a Solar yacht flying no colours had set down in the dark at the Kessel Pass, and lifted again before dawn.

She closed the satchel.

“That is all. I thought the Council would wish to hear it at once. I rode through the rain to bring it. I should not have done that for anyone else.”

For a heartbeat the whole hall was silent.

Then it went up like a dovecote with a cat let into it. Half the Nine were on their feet, chairs shrieking on the glass, the other half talking over them, and at the end of the row one of the Nine was saying over and over, *a day’s march, a day’s march*. Caleb heard *war* from four directions at once. He heard *reprisal*, and *the Col de Serre*, and then, from his left, in a thin voice with a laugh in it, *the Solar harpy*.

His hands closed on the arms of the glass chair until his knuckles ached, and down in the black glass beneath them the thread of gold ran suddenly bright and hot, like wire drawn glowing from a dwarven forge.

Matilda at twelve, with him on her shoulders along the Citadel walls in the full gold of noon, letting him hold her plait for reins and shout at the gulls. Matilda at midnight in the stable yard, with her sleeves rolled to the elbow and her cheek laid against the flank of her favourite horse, while he held the lantern and walked the poor beast round until the colic broke, because she could not bear to be seen weeping over it by anyone but him. She had been standing in the snow of the Marches for weeks, with her banners round her, so that whoever had taken her brother would know that she was there. And these men in their warm chairs above their wine cellar called her a harpy for it.

His jaw hurt. He had set his teeth without knowing it, and he unset them.

A voice sharper than the rest asked what else the Council had expected, with the sun prince sitting in the Sovereign’s own hall wearing a Conti ring, and his sister’s soldiers at the gate.

Don Salvatore sat in his high-backed chair with his hands folded, let the noise go on around him, and looked at nobody.

Caleb looked at Oriane de Chardon. She stood at the foot of the dais with her coin in her fist and her head a little on one side, listening to the uproar with an air of deep contentment, like a fae at a duel in the Court of Lys who has wagered on both men and cannot lose. Every word she had said was true. She had only put three true things next to each other, and stood back, and let the Council of Nine do the rest.

Caleb knew that trick. He had watched Eleanor do it to foreign ministers with nothing but a ledger and a raised eyebrow. He had done it himself, over many a dinner, to people who had deserved it. He had never before sat in a hall and watched it done to his sister, with nothing to hold but the arms of a chair.

High in the vaulting, all together, the violet lamps had begun to swing on their chains, and the shadows went to and fro beneath them across the black glass.

“Prince.”

The noise stopped all at once, like a candle pinched out. Arian had not raised his voice. He had only turned in the high seat to look down at the glass chair, and every shadow in the hall had turned with him, a long cold draught past Caleb’s ankles.

“You know the High Paladin.”

It was not quite a question. Arian was waiting, black-eyed and level and without the least impatience.

Caleb had dressed to be looked at. He had not expected to be asked anything, before the whole Council, in a voice that made it plain the answer would be listened to. His mouth had gone dry. He searched all the long mornings at his father’s side for a single time his father had turned in his chair to ask what Caleb thought, and came up empty.

“I know my sister,” he answered. “Yes.”

“Then speak.”

\* \* \*

Caleb rose. He did it without hurrying, as he did everything in that hall, and took a moment on his feet to settle his cuffs, because his hands needed occupation and it was better that they should have that one.

He went down the single step from the glass chair to the floor of the hall. It felt a good deal further, and when his foot came down on the black glass the whole hall shifted its weight to watch him. He stood a few paces from Oriane de Chardon and inclined his head to her with every ounce of courtesy he had been taught.

She dropped him a little curtsey in return, and her coin chimed.

“Madame de Chardon,” Caleb began. “I have read your broadsheets since I was a boy. I have admired them enormously. I once read an account in the *Lantern* of a ball I had attended the night before, and learned three things about myself that were perfectly true and that I had not known.”

“I remember it,” said Oriane warmly. “You were wearing primrose.”

“I was. I have never worn it since.” He let the laugh go round the hall, and waited for it to settle. “You cannot lie, madame. It is the one quality of your people on which the whole world agrees. I wonder if I might ask you some questions about your report, since I am sure you would not wish the Council to be under any misapprehension.”

“You *are* clever,” said Oriane softly. “Ask, Highness. I shall answer if I can.”

“You said that the High Paladin has moved the foremost of her banners to within a day’s march of the Coast’s border posts.”

“I did.”

“On which side of the border are they standing?”

Oriane’s coin turned once in her fingers.

“Their own.”

A murmur ran round the Nine. Caleb did not look at them.

“Has any soldier of the Radiant Guard, to your knowledge, crossed into the Coast’s lands?”

“I have no knowledge that any has.”

“Has the High Paladin, to your knowledge, given any order to cross?”

“I have no knowledge of her orders at all, Highness. She does not confide in me. She does not confide in anyone. I have tried.”

“I can well believe it,” said Caleb, with feeling. “She has never confided in me either, and I am her brother, and I once saved her favourite horse from colic.” He waited for the second laugh, shorter than the first, and went on before it had finished. “The Coast’s post at the Col de Serre has sent no word in four days. Is it snowing at the Col de Serre, madame?”

For the first time, Oriane hesitated, and as she went still the colours went still in her coat, and settled to a sober, considering bronze.

“It is the Marches, Highness. It is always snowing somewhere.”

“Has it been snowing at the Col de Serre?”

The coin had stopped moving.

“Heavily,” admitted Oriane de Chardon. “For five days.”

“Is the road to it open?”

“I have no knowledge that it is.”

“Did you pass it yourself, on your way here?”

“I did not. I came round by the Durnhollow road. I was told the high road was closed.”

The murmur this time was of a different kind. Caleb heard it change and kept his back to it. At the far end of the Nine, someone laughed shortly and turned it into a cough.

“And the yacht,” said Caleb. “A Solar yacht, flying no colours, set down at the Kessel Pass three nights ago. Were the colours of the Radiant Guard upon it?”

“None.”

“Did any soldier of the Guard leave it?”

“I have no knowledge that any did.”

“Did it carry arms, to your knowledge?”

“I have no knowledge that it carried anything at all,” said Oriane, “except its passengers. And they did not stay long.”

Caleb had a question ready. And at the back of his mind an old courtier’s instinct lifted its head, like a hound at a sound in the next field.

He looked, for no reason he could have named, down the length of the hall at Don Salvatore Caruso.

Don Salvatore sat as he had sat all morning, with his long hands folded on the arm of his chair and his fine face composed and pleasant. He had enjoyed the uproar. Caleb had watched him enjoy it. Now he had gone motionless, and the pleasant expression stayed on his face with nothing behind it. Under his chair his shadow lay close and dark, darker than any other in the hall, like ink poured a long time ago that had never quite dried. Enzo had said in the lamp room that on the Coast a man’s shadow was the oldest thing about him. Don Salvatore’s was a great deal older than his face.

Whatever had left Don Salvatore’s face had left it at the words *Kessel Pass*.

Caleb had never heard of the place until this morning. He put the thought away for later, and turned back to Oriane.

“Thank you, madame. Then I think I have only one more question. Who paid you for this report?”

Nobody in the hall so much as shifted in a chair, and the only thing in the whole of it that moved was the lamplight, sliding up and down Oriane’s feathers as she breathed.

The delight had gone out of Oriane’s face, and in its place was a look Caleb had seen before on old and clever people at dinner, at the moment they understood that the young man they had been humouring was not, after all, a fool.

“I decline to answer, Highness. As is my right.”

“Of course. Thank you.”

He did not look at Don Salvatore again. All down the hall, the Nine were turning in their chairs to do it.

He turned and looked up at the high seat.

“My sister,” he said, “has stood her soldiers on her own side of the line, in the snow, in plain sight, where every informant in the Marches might count them. That is not a threat, Sovereign. That is a letter. It is the only kind of letter the High Paladin has ever known how to write. She is telling the Coast that she is there, and that she is watching, and that she has not crossed. If she meant to cross, I promise you, nobody would have seen her coming, and there would be no report for Madame de Chardon to bring. The Col de Serre is under the snow. And the yacht at the Kessel Pass may have belonged to anybody in the world with the price of a Solar hull and a reason not to be seen, and it is my experience that such people are a great deal more numerous than one would like.”

“The Council may do as it pleases,” he went on. “But if it sends so much as one soldier against the High Paladin on the strength of this report, it will be starting a war that nobody here has any reason to fight, over a snowdrift, and she will win it.”

He heard his own voice ring off the black glass, clear and unhurried, and stop.

The silence came back and went on, and Caleb stood in the middle of the great hall with his heart beating so hard he was certain at least one creature on the dais could hear it. The candle flames along the walls stood straight up in the cold. Somewhere behind him a pen dropped, and rolled, and nobody bent to pick it up.

Arian looked at him for so long that Caleb felt it at the back of his neck and in the palms of his hands.

“The prince is right,” he said, without grudging, and without the grace of a king conferring the favour of his agreement on an adviser who ought to be grateful for it.

Caleb heard it with his whole body before he understood it with his mind, and heat went up his throat, and his eyes stung so sharply that he had to fix them on a lamp high in the vaulting until the violet stopped swimming, and behind his breastbone a fist that had been clenched for seven weeks, and perhaps for years before that, let go.

The letting go hurt, the way blood hurts coming back into a hand that has been asleep.

Along the walls every candle flame bowed once, all together, and stood up again in the cold.

“The Council will send no one against the Radiant Guard. Consigliere. Send a rider to the Col de Serre.”

“Sovereign.” Enzo made a note. His face was grave. “And should the rider take anything with him?”

“A shovel,” said Arian.

The laughter this time was real, and it went all the way round the hall and came back, and even some of the Nine joined in. Caleb stood on the black floor with his hands clasped behind his back, so tightly that the iron of the ring bit into the finger beside it, as it had bitten a week ago in this same hall. His knees were none too reliable.

He went back up the step and sat down in the glass chair. The glass had kept the warmth of him while he was gone, and it received him like an old acquaintance. His hands were shaking, and he laid them flat on the arms of the chair until they stopped, and far down in the black glass beneath them the thread of gold woke again beside the violet, and steadied to a glow, like the first colour coming up in an alchemist’s glass.

Beside him, without turning his head, Arian murmured, so low that it scarcely stirred the air, “Thank you.”

Caleb kept his own head still. He sat with his palms flat on the glass and the words lying warm in him, like the crystal light on the water-stair, and waited for the Council to go on.

\* \* \*

Oriane de Chardon found him in the garden that afternoon.

He had gone down after the Council rose and sat on the water-stair with a book on his knee, and left the great doors open behind him, because the morning had been full of noise and he wanted the quiet of the light. The step was warm under him. The air was thick with the green, sharp sweetness of lemon blossom and the deeper smell of wet earth, and the water slid past his shoes over its stone lip with a sound like someone hushing a child. When he heard footsteps on the iron stairs he knew they were not Vesna’s, because Vesna did not skip.

Oriane was coming down from the mezzanine into the gold in her shimmering coat, with her face tipped up towards the crystals and her eyes closed, like a cat in a window.

“That is *heavenly*. They told me. I did not believe them. Twelve of them, deep-trench, in a spiral, and all for you.” She opened her eyes and looked about her at the terraces, the roses and the lemon trees and the lilies and the running water, and then at Caleb on his step. “He has never done anything like this in his life, you know. I have known him since he was born. He has never so much as sent a woman flowers.”

“I am not a woman.”

“No,” agreed Oriane, and came and sat down on the step beside him without being asked, in a rustle of feathers. “That had not escaped my notice, Highness. Nor anyone else’s.”

She did not burn. The fae went where they pleased, in any light, and the rest of the world had never quite forgiven them for it.

For a while neither of them spoke, and the water went on hushing over its lip, and the coin lay quiet in her pocket, and high above them the crystals turned on their silver chains and drew the light slowly down the terraces, so that the white roses flushed gold row by row and paled again, as unhurried as a reader turning the pages of a long book.

“You did not ask me,” Oriane said at last, “whose yacht it was.”

Caleb turned a page he had not read.

“No. I did not.”

“You were going to. I saw it in your face. You had the question on your tongue, and then you looked down the hall at Don Salvatore, and you swallowed it.” She tilted her head at him. “Why?”

“Because I had a feeling,” said Caleb, “that if I asked, you would answer, and that the answer would be of great interest to a great many people, and that I did not yet know which of them it would be of the greatest interest to. It seemed to me that a question of that kind was better kept than spent.” He closed the book. “Would you have told me?”

“I cannot lie, Highness.”

“That was not what I asked.”

Oriane laughed. It was a lovely laugh, low and delighted, and it made a pair of little brown birds that Caleb had not known lived in the garden start up out of the lemon trees in alarm.

“No. It was not. I could have declined, as I declined the other. But you would have learned a great deal from my declining, and so would everyone else.” She touched the coin in her pocket. “The yacht was not in my commission, you know. I put it in myself. My client did not care for that at all. I expect it will cost him a good deal more, the next time he wants anything from me.”

She smiled at him. “You are not at all what I expected. Do you know that? I have been writing about you for twelve years. I have written about your coats and your horses and your debts and your unfortunate affair with the Thalassan ambassador’s nephew, and every word of it was true, and I thought I knew you. And I did not know you at all.”

“I am told that is the usual experience.”

“It is not *my* usual experience,” said Oriane, a little severely. “I dislike it intensely. I intend to remedy it.” She drew the green leather book out of her coat and laid it on her knee, and uncapped a slender silver pen. “I came to make you an offer.”

Caleb looked at the book.

“The whole world is reading about you this week,” Oriane went on. “In Lutèce and in the Citadel and in every canal house in Lowmere, they are reading about the Pilgrim Prince and his vampire, and they are all deciding what they think. And they are deciding it on the strength of what I write, because I am the only one of my trade who has been in the hall. I shall go on writing it, whether you help me or not. But I can only write what is true, and at the moment the only true things I have about you are the ones anybody could see.” She tapped the book. “Give me better ones.”

“And why,” said Caleb slowly, “should I wish to?”

“Because there is a broadsheet in the Citadel,” said Oriane, “that calls you a traitor, and says the palace sources hint at a darker truth. It is not mine. I print what is true and leave my readers to do the hinting. That sheet prints what someone wishes were true, and does its own hinting. But it has been printed, and read, and it is being read still, and I do not think whoever is feeding those palace sources is your friend.”

She paused. “And because your sisters read the *Lantern*, Highness. All three of them. I have it on excellent authority.”

Caleb was quiet. He thought of TRAITOR PRINCE? in its crimson ink, and of three people in the Citadel reading it at three different tables.

Then he asked, “What would you wish to know?”

Oriane smiled.

It took them an hour. She asked, and he answered, and she wrote down every word in a swift, looping hand, the silver pen darting over the page in the gold of the crystals like a dragonfly over sunlit water, while he chose each one with care. He told her that he had never set foot in the Sanctuary, nor anywhere near it, and that the Sovereign of Vesperia had said so on the first night, in Madame de Chardon’s own hearing, and had never once said otherwise. He told her that he was well, and in the light, and eating a great deal of cake.

He told her that the High Paladin was the finest soldier in the known world and would not cross a border without a better reason than a snowdrift, and that the Arch-Chancellor’s arithmetic was the terror of every treasury on the continent, and that the Grand Alchemist had once turned his bathwater gold so that he could swim in the sun, and that he missed all three of them, in that order and every other order.

He kept the gallery to himself, and she left it alone, in a way that told him she knew there was more and would come back for it. It was almost restful, being questioned by a person who could not lie to you.

When she had finished, she capped her pen and closed the green book and sat a while with it in her lap.

“My price.”

“I thought I had just paid it.”

“Oh, no. That was my commission. My price is another matter. One question, Highness, which you must answer truthfully, as I must. And then I will print every word you have given me, and not one word of it in the wrong order.”

Caleb had a strong suspicion he knew what was coming, and a stronger one that he could not stop it.

“Ask.”

Oriane leaned towards him on the step, so that the feathers of her coat brushed his sleeve and flashed peacock blue in the light.

“Are you afraid of him?”

The water ran on down the stair. Above them, faintly, the crystals hummed. On the second terrace a lemon let go of its branch and dropped onto the path, and rolled a little way, and was still.

Caleb reached for an answer, a pretty one, turned and polished for the *Lantern*, and found nothing in his hand.

He had been afraid on the first night, so afraid that he had called it wit, and afraid in the east wing with a physician’s tidy hand telling him how many weeks he had left, and afraid on the gallery floor with his wrists pinned to the glass, and under the fear there had been a hand cradling his head off the floor, shaking, and a hand at the small of his back that had trembled once and then held before the whole of Vesperia, and *May I kiss you?* asked across a study by someone who did not get up from his chair, and this morning, before the whole Council, *the prince is right*.

His thumb found the ring. He turned it once, and felt the empty setting pass under the pad of his thumb, and stop.

“Not any longer.”

At his wrists, where the lace fell back, the gold had come up under his skin, and the water running past his shoes caught the glow of it and carried it some way down the stair, and lost it in the light of the crystals.

Oriane looked at him until the coin in her pocket gave a single soft chime, like a question. Then she put the green book away inside her coat, and rose, and shook out her feathers, and dropped him a curtsey on the water-stair that was not in the least ironic.

“How very interesting,” she murmured. “Good afternoon, Highness.”

She went away up the iron stairs, humming, with her coin singing again in her pocket and sounding pleased with itself, and the light ran off her feathers in bronze and violet and green as she climbed, until the dark of the corridor took her, coin and all.

\* \* \*

The fifth bell came down through the rock and shivered across the water of the stair in rings of silver, and Enzo came with it, a leather folder under his arm. He stopped several paces back in the dark of the corridor, where the light of the crystals could not reach him, and Caleb went up the stair to him.

“From the Office of the Arch-Chancellor.” Enzo held out the folder. “A complaint concerning the crystal trade. The Sovereign has read it. He thought you might know the Arch-Chancellor.”

Caleb took the folder without a word, and Enzo went back up the corridor.

He read it on the water-stair, with the light pouring over the pages so brightly that the paper seemed to glow from inside. *The Office of the Arch-Chancellor has the honour to remind the Sovereign. The terms of the crystal trade were fixed at the last Long Eclipse. The Treasury has observed with concern. The Treasury has been obliged to note.* It was Eleanor at her most glacial and her most magnificent, every sentence polished until it gleamed and every one of them cold enough to crack a tooth on, and Caleb read it with his throat aching, because he could hear her in every line, dry and clear and faintly bored, the voice that had read him the Treasury rolls on nights when he could not sleep.

Then he came to the appendices.

Appendix C set out, one line to a stone, every crystal of deep-trench Aether consigned for the Citadel in the present quarter and withheld. There were twelve lines. Above his head, twelve crystals turned on their chains.

*The first, four hundred and twelve crowns and nineteen pence.*

He had been six years old, and hopeless at sums. He remembered the schoolroom at the top of the palace with its lead-lined shutters folded back and the noon of the spire-crystals pouring in at every window, because it was always noon in the Citadel, and the smell of hot dust and ink, and the long table scarred by generations of royal penknives. He could still see himself at it with his feet dangling, weeping with fury over a column of figures that would not add up. Eleanor, who was twelve and already did the Treasury’s sums for pleasure, had come in with a ledger under her arm and her plait half undone, and looked over his shoulder, and sighed.

She had sat down beside him on the bench, which creaked, and taken the pen out of his hand. She had written him a sum of her own, a column of crowns and pence, and told him to count the pence on his fingers and find the letter that went with each, one for A and two for B, and see what it said. He had counted it out with his lips moving and his fingers splayed on the table, while Eleanor propped her chin on her hand and watched him do it.

It had said CALEB IS A GOOSE.

He had shrieked with outrage. Eleanor had laughed until she had to put her head down among the ink pots, and he had flung his arms round her neck and called her a goose back, and she had let him.

He had learned his sums that winter faster than any child in the history of the Citadel, because every sum Eleanor set him after that had a message in the pence. They had kept it up for years, in notes under his door in the pence of invented bills, and birthday cards made out as invoices, and once, when he was fifteen and in disgrace for some offence he could no longer remember, a whole Treasury memorandum sent up to his rooms by a bewildered page, which on its face concerned the price of candles in the lower city and which said, in the pence, IT WAS WORTH IT.

Nobody else had ever known, not their parents, and not even Cecily.

He counted now, on his fingers, in the light of twelve stolen suns, with his hands unsteady.

Nineteen, one, six, five. S, A, F, E.

The next line was twenty-three, which was W, and then nine, and twelve, and twelve again, and so on down the page. Twice his eyes blurred and he lost his place and had to begin the line again. When he had finished he put his hand over his mouth, as he had in the cellar, and sat for a while in the light and let his eyes do as they pleased.

SAFE WILLING E.

Pence could not carry question marks, and she had not needed them. Eleanor had read in the *Lantern* that he was betrothed to the Sovereign of Vesperia and kissed before his court, and she had sat down at her desk in the Chancellery and asked him, in the only language in the world that nobody else could read, the only two questions she cared about, and signed it with one letter.

He could see her doing it. The long room in the Chancellery with its windows full of white noon, and Eleanor at her desk with her sleeves pinned back, writing *the fourth, four hundred and ten crowns and five pence* in her most correct hand, and then sitting back and looking at it with her lips pressed together, and then sealing it, and handing it to a clerk without a flicker, and not looking up again until the clerk had gone.

He wanted, suddenly and with the whole of his body, to be home. He wanted the noon and the bells and the smell of hot stone in the palace courts, and his sisters quarrelling over supper, and Cecily’s latest mistake rattling the windows. The wanting bent him over the folder on his knee, with his arms crossed over it and his forehead nearly on the page.

He stayed bent, and breathed, and the light lay across the back of his neck as warm as a hand.

A step sounded in the corridor above. Enzo had come back, and stood watching him from the dark.

Caleb closed the folder. He went up the iron stairs and sat on the mezzanine, just inside the doors, and Enzo stayed where he was in the corridor, a careful distance from the threshold, and looked out at the roses and the lemon trees with the expression of a guest who has been asked to admire a view and is doing his best.

“The Arch-Chancellor,” Enzo remarked, “writes a very beautiful complaint.”

“She always has.”

“I have read the ledgers of four Sovereigns, Highness.” He let a moment go by, as dry as the rest of him, still looking at the roses. “Your sister’s pence in Appendix C are the untidiest I have ever seen.”

Caleb did not trust himself to answer at once.

“My sister,” said Caleb carefully, “has never been untidy in her life.”

“No. I thought not.”

He held out his hand for the folder, and Caleb rose and carried it out to him across the line of the light, into the cold of the corridor. Enzo tucked it under his arm, and then, without looking, drew a folded handkerchief of dove grey silk from his sleeve and put it into Caleb’s hand, as one might pass a cup of tea to a guest. Then he looked at Caleb at last.

“You may write to your sisters. I shall read the letter, as the terms require.” The corner of his mouth moved by the width of a hair. “I am old, and often far less attentive to figures than I would like.”

Then he went away up the corridor, and the violet lamps took him one by one until there was nothing left of him but his footsteps, and Caleb went back into the light and sat down on the mezzanine with the handkerchief in his fist and could not decide whether to laugh or weep, and in the end did a little of both.

\* \* \*

He wrote the letter that evening at the escritoire in the east wing, with a pot of Alorian white tea going cold at his elbow and the lamp turned up as far as it would go, so that its light lay in a warm gold ring on the black paper, and the Coast’s long dusk stood at the smoked glass behind him and looked over his shoulder, as it had every evening for seven weeks, at whatever he was writing.

He wrote it to all three of them together, in their proper order, the way he had written thank-you letters as a child. *My dearest Matilda, my dearest Eleanor, my dearest Cecily.* He told them he was well, and in the light, and eating a great deal, and had been given a garden. He left the Sovereign out, because every true word he could find about the Sovereign would frighten them half to death, and he would not lie to them.

Then he wrote at the bottom, *Eleanor will wish to know how I am managing my household, so I enclose my accounts for the month, which she may audit as severely as she likes,* and he drew up a column.

*To three volumes of Umbral genealogy, from the east library, nineteen pence. To one pot of Alorian white tea, one penny. To lemons, six pence. To strings for the harpsichord, five pence.*

His hand kept wanting to shake, and the purchases he needed to make the pence come out grew more and more absurd. He bought lace at four pence, for cuffs that had all the lace they could bear. He bought candles at fifteen pence, which was robbery in a house with no shortage of candles, and cake at fourteen pence, and more cake at fifteen, so that by the end of the column Eleanor would believe he lived on nothing else and was being cheated in the matter of candles besides. He bought a hat, for twenty pence, that he could not imagine wearing in a place with no sun, and sat over the hat with the pen in his teeth, laughing silently into his sleeve, and then wiped his eyes and went on.

When he had finished, the pence said SAFE. DO NOT COME.

The second question he had not answered.

He sat with the pen in his hand while the ink dried and dulled on the nib, and far below him in the fortress a bell rang the hour, and then the quarter. He thought of what Oriane had asked him on the water-stair, and how far back he had had to go before he could answer her. He did not know whether he was willing, and he would not lie to Eleanor. He looked down at the black iron on his left hand, at the runes and the closed crescent.

He put the pen down, and left the second question empty, like the setting on his ring.

Then he rose, with the garden key that was not a key on his finger, and went down through the dark to the light.

\* \* \*

The great doors were open when he reached them. He had forgotten to close them when he went up.

He stopped where the violet lamps gave out. It was cold there at that hour, the deep, still cold of rock that has never once been warmed, and his breath showed faintly in front of him. Ahead, the gold of the crystals spilled across the iron threshold, thick and warm as poured honey, and ran a little way along the corridor floor, and stopped. At the edge of it, where the light ended and the dark began, someone was sitting on the floor with his back against the doorframe and one knee drawn up, looking in at the garden.

Caleb stood where he was.

He had seen that figure twice before, from the lowest terrace, standing in the doorway high above. Both times the doorway had been empty by the time he shaded his eyes. He had never seen it sitting down.

He must have made some sound. Arian turned his head, and then, without haste, began to rise.

“Please don’t,” protested Caleb. “You needn’t get up on my account. If you stand, I shall have to bow, and you will have to bow back, and one of the housemaids will find us bowing at each other in a corridor at this hour, and there will be no end of talk.”

Arian stopped. After a moment, he sat back down.

Caleb stepped over the threshold into the light, and felt it close round him like warm water. The warmth went into his hands first, which had been cold since the escritoire, and then into his face. Then he turned, considered the matter, and sat down on the iron floor of the mezzanine on the garden side of the doors, with his back against the other half of the frame.

There was perhaps an arm’s length between them. The line of the light lay across the floor in the middle of it, as sharp as if it had been ruled, gold on Caleb’s side and violet dark on Arian’s, and the motes of dust that drifted in the gold went no further than its edge.

“Do you come here often?” said Caleb.

Arian looked at him.

“Every night.”

Caleb had meant it as a joke. He thought of all the nights he had closed those doors and gone up to bed, and of the iron standing shut in the dark, colder at night than anything else in the fortress, and of someone sitting with his back against it on the far side, hour after hour. He looked out at the garden instead. It spread below them in its spiral, terrace upon terrace, roses and lilies and lemon trees and the water running down the long stair, and the twelve great crystals turning slowly overhead, so bright that he could not look at them for long. He supposed that from the doorway it would look like the inside of a jewel, a place one could only ever see from outside.

“Who tends the roses?” he asked at last. “I have never seen a gardener. I have looked. There is a trowel, and the beds are weeded, and the spent blooms are taken off, and nobody is ever there.”

“Vesna’s husband. Tomas. He comes while you read. He is good at not being seen.”

“Why ever not?”

“He is shy.”

Caleb turned and looked at him. Arian’s face gave nothing away. But Caleb had begun to learn the difference between the stillness that meant nothing and the stillness that meant much was being carefully kept back, and this was the second kind.

“A shy lycan gardener,” he said, “whose wife cooks my breakfast and whose son carries my tea, and whose whole family came into your household some months ago, for no reason anyone can give me, and about whom the old families on the Council have talked of nothing else for weeks.”

Arian did not answer.

“Some months ago,” Caleb pressed. “Before I came.”

Arian looked in at the roses, with the light lying across his boots.

Caleb let it go. He had the question and the answer both nearly in his hand, but it was late, and he had closed his fingers on quite enough that day. He put it away beside the Kessel Pass, where he could find it again.

“You asked for me this morning,” he said instead. “In the hall. Before all of them. Why?”

“You knew your sister.”

“Half your Council would have told you the same, if you had asked them. Or very nearly. They were not all baying for war.”

“They did not know her. You did.”

“And you took my word for it. Before the whole Council, and Don Salvatore sitting there watching you do it. You said *the prince is right* as though nothing in the world were easier to say.” Caleb heard his own voice and made it lighter. “I have known a good many kings, Sovereign. My father among them. I have never once known one of them to be persuaded of anything merely by its being true.”

“I am not a king.”

“No. You are a great deal more alarming than any king I ever met.” Caleb turned the ring on his finger. “Why?”

For a while he thought he would not be answered.

“You were right. That is all it needs.”

Arian was looking at the long bed of golden lilies on the first terrace, where Caleb had fallen in the warm earth on the night of the gallery.

“It is not all it needs,” said Caleb quietly. “Not in any court I ever heard of.”

“It is in mine.”

The water ran. Far above them, one of the crystals chimed faintly on its chain, as it turned.

“They are looking for me,” Caleb said. “My sisters. Matilda at the border, and Eleanor in her ledgers, and Cecily, I should think, in her laboratory, inventing something extraordinary by accident. I do not think they knew, when I was sent. They have been looking for me every day since.”

“Yes.”

“You say that as if you know something about it.”

Arian was silent. Then, so low that Caleb was never certain afterwards that he had heard it, he breathed, “I know something about looking.”

Caleb turned his head. On Arian’s side of the line the shadows had gathered in close about him, deep and quiet, the way snow drifts against the one door of a lycan hill farm in winter.

He thought of the dark house, and of someone walking it year upon year, opening one door after another on an empty room. On the floor of the gallery he had wanted, absurdly, to find whoever walked there and tell him he might stop. He had never let himself wonder what that someone was looking for.

Arian had drawn his knee up further and rested his arm across it, and his hand hung loose, the long pale fingers just short of the line of the light. Across the backs of them, faint and silvery against the white skin, lay the marks Caleb had seen made on the night of the garden, when the Sovereign put his hand to the doorframe. They had healed badly, as a burn heals that nobody has dressed, and left a puckered trail across two knuckles that had lasted four weeks.

Caleb had been trained by physicians to look at such things. He looked at this one for some time.

“That has not healed.”

Arian glanced down at his own hand. He seemed to have forgotten it was there.

“It will not.”

“It would, from mine.”

Arian looked up.

“My light,” said Caleb. “The Star-Grace. It mends what sunlight breaks. It is the only remedy that does. They teach us that in the Harp Vales before they teach us anything else, and in the next breath they teach us never, under any circumstances, to lay a hand on one of you.” He considered the hand. “I was an indifferent pupil in a great many respects. Give it to me.”

For a moment Arian kept still. His hand lifted a little from his knee, and stopped, and the long fingers closed once, slowly, on nothing, and opened again.

Then, uncertainly, Arian held out his hand across the line of the light.

It blistered at once. The light took the fingertips first, and Caleb saw the white skin flush red and lift in a fine bloom of blisters, and heard the catch of breath that Arian failed to swallow, and did not wait. He took the hand in both of his own and drew it the rest of the way into the gold.

It was cold. It lay in his two hands like something brought in out of the snow, long and heavy, the fingers loosely curled, and the cold of it went up through his wrists until the bones of his own hands ached. In the gold between them his breath had begun to show again, a faint white mist, as it had in the corridor. He had been carried in those arms, and pinned under that hand, and kissed before a whole court by that mouth, and he had known the cold of Arian the way one knows a winter, from the outside, by what it does to the skin. He had never reached for it himself.

He laid his palm over the old marks, and called the Star-Grace up.

It came warm and willing, and faster than it had ever come. The light gathered in his palm and poured into the cold skin beneath like warm water into a frozen stream, and the cold closed round it, and gave, and let it through. He went in with the light as the temple physicians had drilled him, and felt the damage as the body felt it. The new blisters were bright and simple, and he smoothed them away almost without thinking. The old burns lay deeper, a seam of banked coals across two knuckles, a dull, patient heat that had burned every hour of four weeks while Arian sat against the iron doors with it, night after night, and said nothing.

He persuaded the skin, gently and firmly, of what it had been before, and it was the easiest healing he had ever done. The flesh under his hand seemed almost to know the way already, as if this light had healed it once before, a long time ago. The heat went out of the seam all at once, like a breath let go. And under his fingertips, where they rested on the inside of Arian’s wrist, there was a pulse, faint and slow, and Caleb took it for his own, beating in his hands.

When he took his hand away, the backs of Arian’s fingers were white and smooth and whole, and the gold was already going out of the fine veins beneath them.

Arian drew his hand back, slowly, into the dark, and held it against his chest with the fingers half curled, the way one carries a cup filled to the brim. And he looked at Caleb.

The severe white composure was all in place, the straight mouth and the still face, but far back in the dark of his eyes something had come loose, like a house with one shutter thrown back on a lit room. For the space of a breath, if Caleb did not imagine it, he looked very young. It was the face Caleb had seen once on the floor of the gallery, when Arian saw him weep.

“Consider it,” said Caleb, and heard that his voice was not quite under his command, “payment for the lemons.”

Arian was silent. Caleb listened to the water among the lilies, and to his own heart, and waited.

Then Arian murmured, “Your letter goes tomorrow. My uncle has read it.”

The light was all round him, warm as a bath, and Caleb went cold in the middle of it.

“He says your accounts are a disgrace.” His thumb moved once over the backs of his healed fingers. “Your sisters should know that you are safe.”

Caleb looked at him for a long time, sitting in the dark on the far side of the line of light, with his healed hand held against his chest and his face turned towards the garden he could not enter, and understood that Arian had known about the pence before ever he sent Enzo down with the folder.

The question came up out of Caleb from a long way down, from the place under his breastbone that had been cold since a glass of Citadel white was pressed into his hand.

“Am I?” he asked, at last. “Safe?”

Arian turned his head and looked at him, across the line.

“Yes,” he said.

He said it the way he had said *the prince is right*.

Caleb believed it. The belief went into him slowly, from the chest outward, as the light had gone into the cold hand.

They sat on for a while after that, on either side of the doors, and said nothing more, while the crystals went round overhead and their light moved slowly over the roses and the running water and the black walls of the cavern, and over the iron at Arian’s boots, and drew back again. Caleb sat in the light with his back against the iron and his hands in his lap, and felt that he was not alone in the fortress, and let the feeling be. His palms were still cold where they had held Arian’s hand. He closed them, and kept the cold.

When at last he rose to go up to bed, Arian rose too, on his side of the line, and stood aside to let him pass.

Arian asked for nothing, and said no goodnight. But as Caleb stepped over the threshold and out of the light, the shadows along the corridor leaned gently towards him, all of them together, like grass in a warm wind, and stayed leaning until he had turned the corner and gone.

\* \* \*

The letter was sealed and in Enzo’s keeping, and would go with the dawn. But in the east wing, before he slept, Caleb took a clean sheet of the heavy black paper from the escritoire, and a pen, and sat for a while by the window with the Coast beyond the glass, the jagged black valleys and the far embers in the canyons. The tea had long gone cold. The lamp had burned low, and the room smelled of its hot brass and of the ink.

Then he wrote, in a column, the way he might have added up the price of lemons, fourteen, fifteen, twenty. Twenty-five, five, twenty.

He looked at it for a long time. Then he folded it small and put it away in the lining of his coat, beside his heart, to keep until he knew whether it was true.
