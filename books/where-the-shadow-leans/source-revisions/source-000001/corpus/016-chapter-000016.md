## Chapter 15: Chapter Fourteen - The Gala

*No hostage given under this Accord shall be fed upon, nor his blood drawn, by any of the Umbral houses, for any cause whatever. Should it be done, and be laid before the Council of the Coast in the presence of an envoy of the hostage’s house, the Accord is broken in that part, and the hostage shall be restored to his house, if his house require it.*

— The Twilight Accord, Article the Sixth (translated from the Lyssois)

\* \* \*

The silk came up from the linen room on the sixth evening after they came home, in a long box of black lacquer that two lycan seamstresses carried up the east stair between them, one at either end, with the slow ceremony of women bearing a christening cake across a crowded yard.

He had asked for it himself.

He had gone down to the kitchens on the morning after the cloak, with the gold still standing up in his skin and a scone in his pocket for courage, and found Vesna at the long table with her arms in dough to the elbow. He had asked her, as idly as he knew how, whether anyone in the fortress could make him a coat of the Coast’s own silk. Then, because it was true and she would have found it out by supper, he had added that he could not pay for it. Every crown he had ever owned had gone into a vault of the Holds in seven drafts, and he had not yet grown used to putting his hand in his pocket and finding only a scone.

Vesna had gone on kneading. She kneaded so long that he began to think she had not heard him, and then she told the dough, without lifting her eyes from it, that His Highness had not paid for one thing in this house since the night he was carried into it, and that His Highness was not going to begin now and put out the accounts.

By the second evening the whole of Castel Nerezza knew. The clerks bowed to him a quarter-inch lower and then looked at his sleeves, as if the change might have come over him already like a frost in the night, and Mirek, who was going about the east wing on a crutch cut from one of his own hazel splints, turned scarlet when Caleb asked him why.

“It’s the custom, Highness,” Mirek mumbled, to the crutch. “When somebody’s promised into a house from outside, they go in their own colours. Like you do, all that white. And then one night they come down to the hall in the house’s black, in front of everyone.” He swallowed. “And after that they don’t.”

“Don’t what?”

“Go home, Highness.”

He had plainly been told by his mother to say nothing whatever about it, and having said it, he dropped his crutch.

\* \* \*

The seamstresses set the box on the bed among the carved serpents and the nightshade, and drew back the tissue, and went to stand by the door with their hands folded to watch him look.

The coat lay folded in black.

It was the black of the Coast, which was a great many blacks laid one over another. Under the lamp it was the black of deep water beneath a bridge at night, and where the folds turned from the light it went down into a violet so dark it was only a rumour of violet, like the colour that swims behind the lids when one shuts one’s eyes against a candle. It was cut high at the throat in the Vesperian way, with no lace anywhere, and a row of jet buttons no larger than lentils ran from the collar to the waist. Over the left breast, where a Citadel tailor would have cut a pocket for letters, someone had worked the closed crescent of House Conti in black thread on the black silk, so thick and so close that it stood proud of the cloth like a carving, and the lamp could find nothing in it to catch.

He put it on.

The silk was cold, as everything on the Coast was cold until it had made up its mind about you, and it lay along his arms like the first touch of a pool at dawn. Then it warmed. He felt it take his warmth and keep it, and he looked down at his wrists and stopped with a button half through its loop.

The gold was coming through.

The Coast’s silk would let it through only at the seams, wherever one panel had been joined to the next, in a fine warm line that ran down each sleeve and over his shoulders and round under his arms, as the light of a lit room shows round the edges of a shut door at night. One of the seamstresses put her hand over her mouth. The other said something to her under her breath in the Wildmarch tongue, and Caleb caught a word he had last heard in the courtyard on the night they came home, which was the word for *walked*.

When they had gone, he turned his old coat inside out across his knees, the one he had worn at the anvil in Dornhöhle, and cut his own black stitches with the finest needle in Vesna’s case, and the ring came out into his palm. Outside the cold-iron it had its lustre back, and the stone in its claws was again what it had been on the first night, a black so entire that the lamplight went down into it and did not come back. Every shadow in the room turned to it. They came out from under the bed and the folds of the plum velvet and lay along the floor pointing at his hand, like the needles in a Lowmere chandler’s window all swinging to one north.

He stitched it into the breast of the new coat, between the silk and its lining, behind the black crescent where the thread lay thickest, and buttoned the coat to the throat. In the black glass the gold ran warm down every seam, and over his heart, where the crescent was, there was none at all. Anyone who looked would see the device of the house he had come down in, dark on dark, and nothing behind it.

He thought the ring, at least, would find the arrangement correct.

\* \* \*

Paul was in the doorway when he turned round, in the Alorian livery he had brushed every night for ten weeks as if somebody might inspect it. He looked at the coat. He looked at the crescent over Caleb’s heart for a long time, and something went across his face like the shadow of a bird across a yard, and then he looked at the floor.

“Well?” asked Caleb. “You have stood behind me at every ball since I was fourteen. You may tell me it is dreadful. I shan’t believe a word.”

“It isn’t dreadful, Highness.” Paul’s voice came out rough, as if he had not used it all day. “You look like you’re staying.”

“I am staying.”

Paul’s big hands closed at his sides, and opened.

“Yes, Highness,” he said, and stood aside, and Caleb saw why.

Arian was in the corridor behind him.

He had come up the east stair without a sound, and the cold had come up with him and lay along the black floor of the corridor as far as the threshold, where it stopped. He was in black, buttoned to the throat, with the frost fine on his shoulders, and on his right hand there were two gold rings, and beside them a finger with nothing on it. Paul went out past him and down the corridor, and his tread went away round the turn.

“Your sister’s yacht is over the strait.”

“Then she will see it.”

“Yes.”

“Good,” said Caleb.

Arian came in, and stopped an arm’s length away. Every candle on the mantel had leaned towards the door when it opened, the bright little flames all bowed over in their cups as if a wind had come in with him that none of them could feel.

“You need not wear it tonight. Not before her.”

“Ask me, Sovereign. Do not tell me.”

Arian was silent. The frost on his shoulders did not stir. Then something at the back of his eyes gave ground, as a lycan at an arch gives ground to the one face he has been told to know.

“Will you wear it?”

Caleb spread his arms, so that the gold ran bright down both sleeves to the wrist.

“I appear to be wearing it.”

Arian looked at him, at the seams of light and the high black collar and the jet buttons, and at Caleb’s face above them, for so long that Caleb began to feel it at the back of his neck. Then he said, quite low, as if it were something he had carried all the way up the stair, “Caleb.”

Every candle in the room drew itself up a finger’s breadth taller and burned there, gold and quite still, like a row of children told to stand up straight for a visitor and not to fidget.

Caleb laughed. It was not altogether steady.

“You will have to stop doing that before the Nine,” he teased. “They will want to know what the candles are for.”

“They may ask.”

“They will. The lord in the mulberry coat will ask to see the device.”

Whether anything went over Arian’s face, Caleb never knew, because he had looked away first, at the candles, which were still standing up straight and showing no sign of ever sitting down.

“Come down.”

The shadows went ahead of them down the great stair, past the caged lamps on every landing, and the cold deepened at every turn, and the gold in Caleb’s seams deepened with it, as embers show brighter the darker a room becomes. At the foot of the stair, before the side door to the hall, where the noise of the whole Coast came through the wood like the sea through the wall of a harbour house, Arian put his hand for a moment at the small of Caleb’s back, as he had once before the Nine. Caleb felt it there, cold through the Coast’s silk, and felt the ring lie still against his ribs on the other side of him, and between the two his heart went on as if it had somewhere to be.

\* \* \*

On the rare occasions when the Citadel spoke of the Coast’s guests at all, it called them the underworld, and meant it unkindly. The Coast had taken the word a long time ago and kept it, as it kept everything that was thrown at it, and tonight the underworld had come up the mountain in its best clothes.

The great hall had been made ready for it. Every violet lamp on every chain was lit, and the candles stood along the walls in their iron stands three deep, and under the black columns on either side long tables had been laid with cloths so white that they seemed to float a little above the boards, with silver on them, and black glass, and bowls of white Alorian roses cut from the garden that morning by a gardener nobody had seen, so that the whole hall smelled faintly of a summer it had never had. The middle of the floor had been left clear. It shone there, black glass polished until the lamps hung in it upside down, a second hall of violet moons under the first, as if the guests were to walk on a lake at night and trust it to bear them. Here and there, where the polish went deepest, a dark thread lay down in the glass like weed held fast in a frozen ditch, where something had once run into a crack in another century and had never been got out again.

And they were walking on it. There were dwarves from the Chamber’s factory at the harbour in their good black, with their beards braided flat. There were Lowmere factors in lace caps starched into peaks, and among them, carried up the hall in a chair by two grandsons, a gnome so old and round and magnificently brocaded that Caleb took her at first for a cushion that had been given a seat of honour, with Maartje Bosboom at her elbow in a fresh cap. There were lycan chiefs down from the hill towns in furs and silver earrings, regarding the white tablecloths with deep suspicion, and guildmasters from the harbour quays in coats the colour of wet slate, and fae couriers along the windowsills in their feathers like birds on a roof before weather, and the lesser houses of the Coast in garnet and plum and smoke.

The Sovereign of the Obsidian Coast had been struck at on his own road, and every one of them had come up the mountain to see what was left of him. They had come as ravens come down out of a hard winter onto the walls of the Wildmarch pastures wherever a ewe has lain down in the snow, to sit along the stones and wait, and see whether she gets up again.

The side door opened, and Arian came through it as he always did, without ceremony, and the candles bowed along the walls all together, like the rushes along a millrace when the sluice is opened. The noise of the hall began to drop.

Then Caleb came through the door at his side, in black, and it stopped.

He had never come into this hall at the Sovereign’s side, from the Sovereign’s own door, in the Sovereign’s own colour, with his light running gold in every seam of it so that he came out into the violet of the lamps outlined in fire, like a lantern of pierced iron with the candle lit inside. He felt the hall see it. It went through them from the dais to the great doors as a stone goes through still water, ring after ring, and every ring a little quieter than the last.

Along the walls the lycans of the Sovereign’s guard put their fists to their hearts. It went down both sides of the hall, and the soft knock of knuckles on leather came after it like rain arriving on a roof, and at the far end of the line the grey-flecked sergeant of the escort did it last of all, slowly, and did not take his fist away.

From a windowsill near the doors, a voice he knew cried, “*Oh*,” in pure delight. Oriane de Chardon sat there in her coat of feathers with her green book open on her knee and her coin gone still between two fingers. She caught his eye down the whole length of the hall and laid her hand on her heart, and mouthed something that he was fairly sure was *I shall need a new adjective*.

He sat down in the glass chair below the high seat, and the cold of the glass came up into the Coast’s silk and was taken in and kept, so that for once he barely felt it. Deep in the chair, under his hands, the thread of gold woke beside the violet and lay there, faint, waiting to see what he would need it for.

The Nine sat at the high table below the dais in their high-backed chairs. He found the lord of the inland vineyards in his mulberry velvet, with his ancient voice going on already to the councillor at his left, who had stopped listening some time ago. He wore a flower in his buttonhole, some pale thing from the harbour gardens with a long throat, shut tight in the lamplight like a fist, though everything else in the hall had opened for the evening. He found Enzo at the end of the row in his dove grey, upright as a candle, with one hand resting flat on the breast of his coat, where it had rested a great deal since the night he had taken a broken chain out of its drawer.

And he found Don Salvatore Caruso in garnet, with his long white hands folded on the cloth and his rings lying on the white of them like rowan berries in snow, smiling at nothing, and beside him, in a black so rich that it went red in the folds, with the thorn at her throat, his granddaughter.

Valentina was not looking at Caleb’s coat. She looked at it once, at the crescent and the gold in its seams, with real and generous appreciation, as one guest at a ball admires another’s dancing. Then she looked past him at the high seat, at the Sovereign’s hand on the black arm of it, at the two gold rings on that hand and the third finger, where there was nothing.

She looked at it for some time. Her eyes went from the bare finger to Arian’s face and back, as Eleanor’s went from a total to the column above it to see where it had come from, and then she leaned a little towards her grandfather without taking her eyes from the hand, and said something that did not move her lips. Don Salvatore did not turn his head. He only turned the garnet ring a quarter turn on his finger, as he did when something had pleased him.

Caleb kept his hands flat on the arms of the glass chair, and the gold thread in the glass ran a little brighter under his palms, and he made it go down.

The feast came in on the shoulders of the household in a long procession out of the kitchens, and the hall breathed in, because Vesna had been at it for six days and it showed. There were roast kids from the Wildmarch on beds of rosemary, and pies with hunting scenes worked in pastry on their lids, and a great silver tureen of something red and steaming that the hill chiefs fell upon with cries of recognition. For the high table there was blood-wine carried up from the cellars in black glasses, with the steam going off it in dark threads, and in the middle of the table, set there before anyone could stop it, rose a pyramid of blood oranges on a silver stand as tall as a child, every one cut across the top to show its dark red heart. The juice had crept down from fruit to fruit and gathered in the silver dish at the foot, thick and dark, like the basin a physician sets under a patient’s arm before he takes up the lancet. A card leaned against the stand in a beautiful old hand. *With the compliments of the Ashward, for the Sovereign’s guests.*

The Nine looked at the oranges, and at one another, and then with great care at nothing, and nobody touched them, and they sat in the middle of the table all evening glowing in the lamplight like a heap of coals that nobody had been invited to warm their hands at.

Before Caleb’s chair, on a plate of its own, somebody had set down two scones.

He ate one, because he had been told to, and looked out over the hall while he did it. Across the floor the old gnome of Van Houten’s was watching him over a pie the size of a cartwheel, with the frank and unwavering interest of the very old and the very rich. Caleb lifted the second scone to her, gravely, as one lifts a glass to a lady across a ballroom. She considered this for some time. Then she lifted the pie, with both hands and a grandson, and the hill chiefs at the next table, who had distrusted everything in the hall since the tablecloths, set down their spoons to watch and began to thump the boards in slow approval until the whole of their table shook.

He was still laughing when his eyes stopped, among the harbour guildmasters at the long table on the left, on a man who was not eating.

He was a lycan by the breadth of him, in a slate-grey coat, with a ship’s chain of office round his neck and his hands lying on the cloth on either side of an untouched plate. There was a faint shimmer of gold at the rims of his nostrils, like the dust on a moth’s wing, and the rims were raw under it, eaten pink and wet as a hare’s, and one of his hands on the cloth had a tremor in it that the rest of him seemed to know nothing about. And he was looking at Caleb, without a smile and without a blink, and when Caleb looked back there seemed to be nobody at home behind his eyes, like the windows of a house that has been let for the season.

Caleb put down his scone.

He thought of a girl on a step in the Underglow who had watched him all down a street and never turned her head, and the skin of his arms went cold under the silk. Then the man turned his head, slowly, as a barge comes round on a canal, and the look went off Caleb and away down the hall. When Caleb looked again, a long while later, it had come back, and it did not seem to have been anywhere in between.

At the second bell the steward of the hall struck the floor three times with his staff, the great doors opened, and every lamp in the hall brightened.

\* \* \*

They brightened one after another down the length of the hall, from the doors towards the dais, each violet lamp going pale and clear on its long chain as she came under it, as if a lamplighter walked a pace ahead of her turning every wick up. The candles along the walls stood up straight and burned whiter. And the steward of the hall, a vampire with a voice like a cellar door swinging on old hinges, lifted his staff and gave her to the Coast.

“Her Royal Highness the Princess Eleanor of Aloria. Arch-Chancellor of the Solar Throne. Envoy of the Solar Throne to the Coast.”

She came up the hall alone.

There was a clerk of the Chancellery a pace behind her with her despatch case clutched to his chest, a thin young man who looked as if he would rather be anywhere in the known world, but Eleanor came up any hall alone, whoever walked behind her, and always had. She wore the Chancellery’s own blue, so dark that under the violet lamps it was nearly the Coast’s black, and across her shoulders lay the Treasury’s chain of office, broad flat links of gold like coins laid edge to edge, which the brightened lamps found and lit one link after another as she came. Her gloves were white. Her hair was up in the smooth coil she had worn to every sitting of the Treasury for eleven years, and not one strand of it had dared to come down on the yacht. She walked on the black glass between the reflected moons as if it had been laid for her convenience, with the faint gold of her own blood showing at her wrists above the white gloves, and the whole underworld watched an Alorian princess walk into the middle of the Coast and found that it did not know what to do with its hands.

Caleb had always said that the lamps did it out of fright. He watched the lamps of a vampire’s hall take fright of his sister one after another down its whole length, and loved her so much that for a moment he could not see her clearly.

She was looking at the high seat. Then she was looking at him.

He watched her read it as she read everything, from the top, the glass chair and where it stood, the coat and what it said, and on his left hand the iron ring with its empty setting, which she had read about in the *Lantern* and never seen. Her step did not change. Only her right hand closed a little at her side, as it closed round a pen when a column would not come out, and opened again.

Then she looked up at the Sovereign’s hand on the arm of the high seat.

Caleb watched her find the bare finger, and then watched the Arch-Chancellor of Aloria walk the last ten paces of the Coast’s great hall doing a sum in her head. He knew the sum. He had done it himself under a hanging lamp in a cold-iron room, and she had all the figures.

She stopped at the foot of the dais.

Enzo had risen from the end of the high table and come round it to receive her. He bowed before her on the black glass, and it was the bow owed to the envoy of a crowned house, not a hair short of it.

“Your Highness is welcome to the Coast.”

“I know who you are, Consigliere.” Eleanor began to give him her hand, remembered where she was, and inclined her head instead. “You read my complaint.”

“Twice, Highness. Your arithmetic is flawless in every appendix but one.”

Something happened at the corner of Eleanor’s mouth and was dealt with. Enzo stood aside, and his hand went back to his breast and lay flat over the place where he kept what he had taken out of a drawer. She was Andrew’s sister, and he had bowed to her all the same, and his face showed nothing at all.

Arian had risen.

The Sovereign rose for the envoy of a crowned house, and every shadow in the hall leaned to him as he did it and drew back from the woman at the foot of the dais, as the dark of a hillside draws back a little from a shepherd’s lamp. Eleanor sank into her curtsey. It was the curtsey of the Solar House to the head of another house, measured to the inch, and she came up out of it with her eyes on his face.

“The Coast is honoured,” said Arian.

“The Solar Throne thanks the Sovereign for his invitation.” Her voice was the one she kept for foreign treasuries, clear and dry and pitched to reach the back of a room without seeming to try. “His Majesty asked for a season to consider it. The Crown Prince did not think the Coast should be kept waiting a season, and drew the Throne’s warrant for me himself.” She laid one gloved hand on the despatch case in her clerk’s arms, as one lays a hand on a dog to keep it quiet. “And so I am here.”

Caleb felt the frost before he saw it. It came out along the black arm of the high seat under the Sovereign’s hand, white and fine, and stopped at the edge.

“Then the Coast is in the Crown Prince’s debt.”

“The Coast is in a great many people’s debt, Sovereign.” Eleanor’s eyes went to his hand on the arm of the chair, to the bare finger and the frost round it, and came back up. “I keep some of the books.”

There was a little stir at the high table, a rustle of garnet, quickly stilled. Don Salvatore had laughed softly, as a man laughs at a good move in a game he is not playing.

Then Eleanor turned to the glass chair.

A prince of the Solar House rose for a princess of it, and bowed, and named her, and waited to be named. He came down the single step to the floor, and stood before his sister on the black glass with the whole of the underworld watching, and bowed as he had been taught, and went no nearer, because if he went any nearer he would put his arms round her in front of the Nine and the Lowmere houses and the fae press, and he did not think she would forgive him for it.

“Your Royal Highness,” he said.

“Your Royal Highness.”

She looked at the coat, and the high collar, and the light coming through the seams, and at his face over it, as she had looked at him across a table in the Chancellery on the morning he came home from the Vales taller than she was, like someone checking a total she has been given by somebody else.

“You have come a long way,” said Caleb, “to audit me.”

“I have come to see where twelve stones went.” She did not smile. “The Treasury has been very anxious about them. I wrote an appendix.”

“So I heard. The Consigliere thought the pence a disgrace.”

Her mouth twitched. She shut it.

“The prince will show Your Highness the stones,” said Arian from the high seat. He sat, and the shadows settled round the high seat like a cloak let fall over the back of a chair. “They are in his garden. I do not go in.”

Eleanor looked up at him a moment longer than was courteous, and Caleb watched her add that to the sum.

“Then I should like to see them now, Sovereign,” she answered, “before I have had any of your wine, so that I may be sure of what I see.”

\* \* \*

Paul walked behind them down the long black galleries, and the noise of the hall fell away until there was nothing to hear but their heels on the glass and the clerk’s anxious breathing. Eleanor did not speak. She looked at everything, the violet lamps in their iron cages, the lycans at the arches putting their fists to their hearts as Caleb passed, the giants’ stair going down out of the cold into the warm breath of the mountain, and he could feel her keeping all of it, in columns.

At the iron doors he set his ring to the hollow, and she watched the runes wake red and go to gold, one after another, until the mechanisms turned over inside the iron like a great beast shifting in its sleep. Her hand went out towards the doors, and stopped short of them.

“Your ring opens it.”

“Only mine.”

The doors parted, and the gold came out over the three of them and down the corridor behind, and the clerk said something under his breath that the Chancellery would not have liked. Paul took up his post beside the doors with his back to the iron, as he had on the first day, and looked at the floor where the light lay.

Eleanor walked out onto the mezzanine and put both her gloved hands on the rail.

The garden went down the throat of the mountain in its spiral, the roses spilling white down the terraces and the lemon trees standing in their urns, and the water going down the long stair talking to itself, and the golden lilies at the bottom with their faces turned up. And above it, on their silver chains, the twelve crystals hung in their slow descending spiral, each taller than a man and blazing with the Citadel’s own noon, so that the whole cavern was full of it to the brim and spilling over, and the black walls of the old vent shone as if they had been wet.

The gold rose in her. He had forgotten how the light took her, from the hands first, as if she reached for it, where it took him all at once. It came up through her white gloves at the knuckles, faint as the glow through the shell of an egg held to a candle, and went up her wrists and into her throat and her face, and the hard, clear, tired lines that the yacht had put there went soft in it. Behind her the clerk had sat down on the top step of the iron stairs without being asked, and taken off his spectacles.

She was counting. He could see her lips move.

“Twelve,” she breathed.

“Twelve.”

“Deep-trench. Every one.” She did not take her eyes from them. “The first, four hundred and twelve crowns and nineteen pence. The second, three hundred and eighty-eight crowns and one penny.” Her voice had dropped almost to nothing. “I priced them, Caleb. I sat in the Chancellery with the noon on the page and priced every one of these, line by line, and I put my question to you in what they cost. And all that while they were hanging here, over your head.”

“Since my twenty-first day. Before that I was dying in the east wing in a very good waistcoat.” He came and stood beside her at the rail. “He began keeping them back three months before the vineyard, when there was no hostage to keep alive. Nobody has told me why. I have stopped asking.”

Eleanor turned her head and looked at him.

“Take me down.”

\* \* \*

She walked in the garden as he had never seen her walk anywhere, slowly, between the white roses, with the gold lying on the dark blue of her skirts and her eyes going everywhere. She put out her gloved hand to a lemon as she passed and did not pick it, and stopped once on the path with her face turned up into the light and her eyes shut, as the lilies turned theirs. At the water-stair she sat down on the warm black stone without looking to see whether it was clean, which she had never done in her life, and pulled off her white gloves and put them on the step beside her. There was ink on her second finger. There always was.

Caleb sat down beside her. The water went past their shoes over its stone lip, clear and cold and full of light, and the gold of the crystals lay warm on the backs of their four hands, his and hers, and could not tell them apart.

She opened the reticule at her wrist and took out a sheet of the Chancellery’s cream paper, folded in three. He knew it before she had it flat. It was her copy of the Chamber’s notice, the seven lines Matilda had cut out of her mail by a river in the Fault, only this one had been folded and unfolded so many times that its creases had gone soft as cloth.

“I copied it in the dark before the clerks were in,” said Eleanor, “and sent it up the north road to Tilda without once looking at the pence. I had seen your portion go out of the books in one night, every crown, into the Holds, and I thought —” She stopped. “I thought a great many things, all night, and none of them twice if I could help it.”

“And then?”

“And then Tilda came in at the Chancellery door three nights later with mud to her knees and a scratch down her face, and stood dripping on the Treasury’s carpet, and told me you were alive and well and a fool. She said you had asked her not to tell somebody something, and she was not certain whether I counted as somebody, and would rather not find out by being wrong.” Eleanor looked down at the sheet. “When she had gone to sleep on my floor, I read the pence.”

She laid one finger at the top of the column and drew it down. Twenty-three. Nine. Twelve. Twelve. Nine. Fourteen. Seven.

“You answered me.”

“You asked me.”

“I asked you in the pence of a formal complaint to a foreign power, because it was the only place in the world where I could ask you anything. And you answered me in the pence of your own ruin.” She had gone cold and level, as she went at the Treasury table when a minister’s figures would not bear looking at. “Do you know what I thought, when I had read it? I thought that a pence can be written at somebody’s elbow as easily as a pen can. If I held a man, and wished his sister to stop freezing my treasury, I should have him write WILLING in whatever cipher she liked. When the Coast’s invitation came, I asked Andrew for the Throne’s warrant, because I could think of nothing to do but come and look.”

“And now you have looked.”

“Now I have looked.”

She folded the sheet along its soft creases.

“A fortnight ago, a factor of Van Houten’s sold the Treasury, in confidence and at a scandalous price, the news that the Coast had bought back its debt from the Ashward with the Seventh Chamber’s money, against a pledge. He named the pledge and the price. I did not think any Sovereign of the Coast could be such a fool.”

A white rose on the terrace above let go of a petal. It came down past them through the gold, turning, lit at its edges like a lamp shade, and settled on the water and went away down the stair into the dark at the bottom of the light.

“And then I walked up his hall tonight,” said Eleanor, “and his hand was bare.”

Caleb said nothing.

“I know the price. And I know your portion to the penny, because I have kept your books since you were sixteen and paid your tailor out of it twice without telling you. They differ by four hundred and some crowns, which I imagine is the Chamber’s fee for holding its tongue.” She turned her head at last. “He does not wear it.”

“No.”

“Does he know?”

“No.”

She looked at him a long moment, at the crescent over his heart where no gold came through, and then she looked away at the water, quickly, as if she had been about to read a figure she was not meant to see.

“Do not tell me where it is. I would rather be able to say I do not know.”

“I was not going to tell you.”

“I know you were not. I am telling you not to.” Her hands had found each other in her lap. “And you have not given it back to him.”

“He did not want to be paid. He told me so. He would sell it back and go to his auction with nothing, sooner than owe me for it.”

“Then you are not a hostage.”

She said it to the water, quietly, in the voice in which she read out a total that had come right after three nights of being wrong.

“Nobody holds a man and lets him pay for their crown. Nobody stands at the door of a room he cannot go into and gives the only key to it to his prisoner.” Her voice caught, and she let it. “I came all this way to see whether somebody had stood at your elbow. Nobody stood at your elbow. You did it on your own, in seven drafts, and you did not even think to keep back the price of a yacht home.”

“I had already been told I had no door.”

“By whom?”

“A dwarf. He was very kind about it.”

Eleanor put her hand over her eyes.

She sat like that on the water-stair with her elbow on her knee and her face in her inky hand, and the light of twelve stones she had priced to the penny lay over her bowed head and the gold links on her shoulders, and her shoulders did not move. Caleb kept his hands to himself, because he knew her. He looked up at the crystals turning on their silver chains, white at the heart and gold at the edges, and waited, as she had once sat and waited for him over a column of figures that would not add up.

When she took her hand away her face was dry. Her eyes were not.

“There is something else,” he began. “I should tell you here, because I do not think I can tell you in the hall.”

“The night before,” said Eleanor.

He looked at her.

“I wrote it,” she admitted. “On the slip. I found the warrant in the household purse with her lily beside his sun, and copied it, and wrote that under it, and could not write anything else. And I have told myself every day since that it meant nothing, because it could not be proved.”

“It is proved.”

She went still.

“The Coast’s courier was a fae. He sat in a window outside Father’s study all that day and wrote down every bell of it, because his kind cannot help it. Andrew went in at the first. At the third, Father said the Solar Throne required no inquiry.” His voice had gone level and far away, and he let it. “And Mother said I was to be given something to make me sleep.”

The ink on Eleanor’s finger had gone pale where she pressed it. The gold had gone hard and bright in her wrists, like the light in a lamp turned up too high, and on the warm stone beside her a fallen lemon leaf curled at the edge and browned and began to smoke. She looked at it, and breathed, and the gold in her wrists went down, and the leaf went no further.

“I have kept the Treasury’s books for eleven years,” she said, “and in eleven years I have never once entered a figure that Father did not wish to see. I put things in the pence. Every question I ever wanted to ask anybody, I put in the pence, where nobody would see it but you.” She looked at the folded sheet on her knee. “You did not put yours in the pence. You put the whole of it in the main figures, every crown, where anybody in the world could read it.” She laughed, and it was not much of a laugh. “I have been afraid of Father my whole life, Caleb. And of Andrew, and of the distance one keeps from the heir. I learned it before I could add. And tonight I cannot for the life of me remember why.”

He looked at his sister in the gold, with her white gloves off on the warm stone and her hair still perfect and her eyes red, and the light of the crystals lying in the links of her chain as if it had been looking for her all evening.

She looked back at him for a long time, at his face in that light, and then she asked him the plain question, the one under all the others, which she had not dared to put even in the pence.

“Are you happy?”

The water went over its lip. Far up the cavern, one of the crystals chimed on its chain as it turned.

“Yes,” said Caleb.

Eleanor nodded once, as she nodded when a total came right.

Then she opened the reticule again and took out a tin. It was flat and round, enamelled white and gold and dented at one edge, tied up with a length of blue Chancellery ribbon, and he knew it before it was out of the bag, because he had stolen from it at the age of nine and been caught with violet sugar on his chin.

“For visiting dignitaries,” said Eleanor, and put it in his hands.

He untied the ribbon. Inside, on a bed of white paper, lay the candied violets, purple under their sugar, which glittered in the crystal light like frost on a bed of the real thing, and they smelled of the Chancellery desk, of ink and sealing wax and the drawer in which she had kept them for as long as she had had a desk. Folded into the lid there was a bill.

He knew what it was, and unfolded it anyway, with her watching.

*To violets, seven pence. To sugar for the same, fifteen pence. To the tin, fifteen pence. To ribbon, nineteen pence. To carriage of the above by yacht to the Obsidian Coast, five pence.*

He counted it on his fingers. He could not help it. He counted it with his lips moving, as he had at six with his feet dangling from the schoolroom bench, and beside him Eleanor propped her chin on her hand and watched him do it.

Seven. Fifteen. Fifteen. Nineteen. Five.

“I wrote it on the yacht. I did not know yet what I would find. I thought it would do either way.”

Caleb laughed.

It came up out of him all at once, as the gold came up, and went round the black walls and up into the crystals, and the clerk at the top of the iron stairs put his spectacles back on to see what had happened. Eleanor’s mouth went, and then the rest of her went, and she put her forehead down on her knees on the water-stair of a vampire’s garden and laughed until her shoulders shook, and he put his arm round her at last, because a thing that had been allowed at six was allowed now, and she let him.

“Five pence,” he said, when he could speak. “For a yacht.”

“I had to make it come out.”

“The Treasury will be ruined.”

“The Treasury,” said Eleanor into her knees, “has survived you before.”

He ate a violet, and gave her one, and she ate it, which he had never once seen her do. Then she drew her gloves back on a finger at a time and stood and shook out her skirts, and was the Arch-Chancellor again from her hair to her heels, all but her eyes.

On the mezzanine, with the cold of the corridor coming in at the open doors to meet the gold, she stopped and looked back once down the whole of the garden.

“He built this.”

“Yes.”

“For you.”

Caleb put the tin in the right-hand pocket of his coat, on the other side from the ring, and left it unanswered, and she seemed content to leave it so. She went out past Paul into the cold violet of the corridor, and Caleb followed her, and behind them the iron doors closed on the light, and the runes went dark on their face one by one, from the edges in.

\* \* \*

When they came back into the hall the musicians had begun.

They sat in the gallery over the great doors with viols and a long-necked lute that seemed to have been strung with cobweb, and before them on a frame of black iron hung a row of bells. They were glass, every one, from the size of a thimble to the size of a man’s head, and the player who went along them with two little hammers of felted bone drew from them a sound so clear and so cold that it seemed to come from just behind one’s own ear. And on the black glass of the floor, among the reflected moons, the Coast was dancing.

It danced as it did everything, at a distance.

The *distanza*, the *Lantern* had once called it, in a column on the manners of the Umbral houses, was the only dance the courts of light and the courts of shadow had ever been able to share, because nobody in it touched. The partners lifted their hands palm to palm, a finger’s breadth apart, and kept that finger’s breadth through every figure, turning and parting and coming back, so that from the gallery the floor must have looked like a field of moths that had agreed never once to land. A hill chief was dancing it with the old gnome of Van Houten’s, who had been lifted onto a footstool for the purpose and was dancing it from there with tremendous dignity while her grandsons turned the stool. Down the middle of the floor the lesser houses went turning in their garnet and their plum, their white palms never meeting, and their reflections turned beneath them on the black glass and never met either.

Eleanor went to her place at the Sovereign’s left, where a chair had been set for the envoy of the Solar Throne at the head of the high table, some paces below the high seat, and sat as if she sat at a vampire’s table every night of the week. A lycan footman handed her a black glass with the dark steam going off it, and she looked at it, and set it down on the white cloth, and the lamp above her chair went on burning a little brighter than any lamp near it.

Caleb did not reach the glass chair.

“Highness.”

Valentina Caruso stood before him at the edge of the floor with her two hands lifted, palms out, in the first figure of the *distanza*.

“The Coast asks its guests to dance.” Her mouth moved. “Will you be rude to me twice?”

“I was never rude to you once, Donna Valentina.”

“You bowed a quarter-inch short.”

“I bowed as low as you curtseyed. Not a hair lower.”

“Yes. I noticed.”

He lifted his hands to hers, a finger’s breadth of air between his palms and her gloved ones, and felt her cold come across it, fainter than her grandfather’s and keener, like the cold off a pane of ice on a water-butt at dawn, and the music took them out onto the floor.

She danced it beautifully, with the black of her gown going red at every turn under the lamps and the gap between their hands never once widening or closing. The gold from his seams lay along the white of her gloves, and her cold lay along his palms, and neither did anything to the other. After a figure or two he found he had stopped watching her hands and was watching her face, which he suspected was the point.

“You have changed your colours, Highness.”

“I was given new ones.”

“A garden, and a chair, and a ring.” She turned under his hand without touching it. “On the night I was refused, your collar was open to the throat, so that we should all see there was nothing there to see.” Her eyes went to his collar, high and black and fastened to the chin with jet. “Tonight it is buttoned very high.”

“It is the Coast’s fashion.”

“So it is,” agreed Valentina. “It suits you.”

They turned. Through the moving hands of the dancers he could see Don Salvatore at the high table with his white hands folded before the pyramid of oranges, watching the floor as a man watches a game of chess between two players he has taught.

“The Sovereign has lost a ring.”

She said it lightly, as one might remark that a candle had gone out.

“Has he?”

“He wore three on the night I came. We count everything the Contis have.” They parted, and came back, and her palms hung before his a finger’s breadth away, steady as the pans of a balance. “Tonight he wears two. And my cousin of the inland vineyards tells me that he seals his letters in his own blood, like a lover in a Lyssois novel, with nothing on the seal at all.”

“The Sovereign has always had a romantic turn.”

“Has he?” said Valentina. “I did not notice it, the night he refused me.”

The music brought them round by the long table where the Lowmere houses sat, and as they passed it Caleb heard, quite clearly over the viols, a voice like a cracked bell coming out of a great heap of brocade.

“—every note of the Coast’s paper, I had it from my own factor, who was there. Not a word from the Chamber, naturally, and not a word on the Exchange either. We have kept it in the house. It is worth a great deal more kept. Redeemed by the Long Eclipse, or forfeit, and under the hammer at the Seventh Chamber the same day, and mark me, I shall have my canal house in pawn to bid for it, and so will every house in Lowmere that can raise the —”

“*Mevrouw*,” whispered Maartje Bosboom, in anguish.

“What?”

“*Mevrouw*, the music.”

“I can hear the music perfectly well, Maartje. I am telling the master here that —”

The dance carried Caleb past, and the rest was lost under the bells.

Valentina’s hands had not moved by a hair, and her face was as amused as it had been all evening. But the cold across the gap had changed. It had gone sharper and stiller, like the air of a stillroom when somebody opens the door to the ice house, and the black thorn at her throat caught the lamplight and held it, a single white point, like frost on a bramble.

“The Long Eclipse,” murmured Valentina, as one reads a date out of a ledger. “The day the Accord is renewed. And on that same day the Sovereign’s ring goes under the hammer in Durnhollow.” She turned, and came back. “The Holds are so very tidy.”

Over her shoulder Don Salvatore had not moved. But he had heard. Every vampire in that hall had ears like an owl in a bell tower, and the old gnome had not lowered her voice in a hundred years. Caleb watched him lift his right hand from the cloth and turn the garnet ring on it, slowly, a whole turn, and then another, and lay the hand down again.

“My grandfather,” said Valentina, “has never been outbid in his life.”

Under the black crescent the ring lay cold and heavy against Caleb’s ribs, the one dark place in all the gold of him, a hand’s breadth from her white-gloved palm, while the oldest house on the Coast sat at the high table turning its own ring and pricing it.

He wanted, quite badly, to laugh. He wanted to say *Let him bid. Let him carry every crown in the Ashward to Durnhollow on the day of the Eclipse, and open his purse before the Seventh Chamber, and be told by a dwarf that there is nothing there to buy.*

“Then I hope he enjoys the bidding,” he said. “I am told the Holds make a great occasion of it. There is a sand glass.”

Valentina laughed.

It was out of her before she could stop it, low and short and startled, the first thing she had done all evening that she had not meant to do, and across the finger’s breadth of air her cold gave way by a degree, as the ice on a water-butt gives at noon whatever it resolved at dawn. She closed her mouth on it.

Then she looked at him.

It was the look that had taken his measure to the last decimal on the night of the refusal, and it went over his face again. Then, for a breath, it went down to the black crescent over his heart, and Caleb held his hands where they were in the air, a finger’s breadth from hers, and did not let them close.

The bells finished their figure.

Valentina sank into her curtsey, and this time she gave him the full depth owed to a prince of the blood, to the inch.

“Thank you, Highness. You dance very well, for a man who is about to have a dreadful evening.”

She went back to the high table before he could answer, and sat down beside her grandfather, and laid her gloved hand for a moment on his arm.

\* \* \*

The feast went on round him for another hour, and he watched it from the glass chair and could not have said afterwards what he ate. Mirek had appeared in the kitchen doorway on his crutch, where he had certainly been told not to appear, and was being fed pie by a housemaid with one eye on the door in case his mother came through it. At the Sovereign’s left hand Eleanor talked to the Consigliere about the Lowmere Exchange in her driest voice, and he answered her in his.

At the fourth bell the Sovereign rose.

The candle flames along the walls bowed towards the high seat, and the shadows drew in under the long tables from every side, and the talk went down as a fire goes down under a lid. In the gallery the bell player lifted his hammers from the glass and held them there.

Arian stood at the high seat with a black glass of blood-wine in his hand and the frost on his shoulders, and looked down the hall at the whole of the underworld, and the underworld looked back.

“The Coast thanks those who came home with it from the Fault.”

He did not raise his voice. It went to the back of the hall all the same, as frost finds the far corners of a room.

“The sergeant of my escort, who knew the caves. Mirek, son of Tomas, who had the prince heal me first.” By the kitchen door a crutch fell over with a clatter. “And the prince, who walked.”

The fists went to the hearts along the walls again, and stayed there.

“Those who did not come home,” said Arian, “may be counted by whoever sent them.” He did not look at the high table, and had no need to. “It is not a long sum.”

Caleb thought of the ledge of the Fault in the dark, and the frost lying on the rock in the print of men’s feet, pair after pair along the ledge with nobody standing in any of them, and the blades falling out of nothing onto it, and the scone sat cold in him.

Below the dais, at the head of the Ashward’s own table, Don Salvatore’s grandson Ruggero, with the dust of some long road still on his boots, set down his glass without drinking from it. Frost had come up the stem under his fingers while the Sovereign spoke, and when he took his hand away the tips of them had gone grey as candle stubs, and he sat and looked at them.

Arian lifted his own.

“Sovereign.”

Valentina Caruso had risen beside her grandfather. She stood with her gloved hands resting on the white cloth on either side of her untouched glass, and her voice was low and clear and carried as well as his.

“Before the Coast drinks, the Council should hear a thing that touches the Accord. The Accord has sent us an envoy tonight. It would be a discourtesy to let her go home without it.”

Arian looked at her down the length of the high table, with the glass still lifted.

“Then say it.”

Don Salvatore did not rise. He lifted one white hand from the cloth and let it fall. “Let the Nine sit as the Council.”

“The Council sits,” answered the lord of the inland vineyards at once, as if he had been waiting for it since the second bell, and down the high table the Nine set down their glasses and turned in their high-backed chairs to face the floor, and the shut white flower in his buttonhole nodded with the movement and did not open.

Valentina came round the end of the table and out onto the black glass.

The dancers had gone from it. She walked out alone among the reflected moons to the very middle of the floor, where the Sovereign had stood on the first night with a melted seal running between his fingers, and turned to face the high seat. Behind Caleb, against the wall, he heard Paul draw a breath and not let it go.

“Eleven nights before I came to this hall to be refused,” said Valentina, “His Highness knelt in the lower yard and healed a man the Sovereign’s own sentinel had torn open.”

Along the wall, the grey-flecked sergeant’s fist came down off his heart.

“He fell in the black gallery, and the Sovereign knelt beside him on the glass and drank from his throat. From a prince of the Solar House, held under the Accord.” She let it lie a moment on the black floor among the moons.

Every vampire in the hall looked at Caleb’s throat.

He felt them do it, as one feels a draught find the gap in a shutter. They looked at the high black collar fastened to the chin with jet, and under the silk two points at the side of his neck that had closed weeks ago went cold together, as an old break aches before snow. For the space of a breath he was on the glass again with the lamps swinging over him, and his wrists were pinned above his head in one cold hand and would not move by a hair, and someone was saying one word over and over against his skin. Down the long tables, a lord of one of the lesser houses, in smoke grey, had wet his lips, and did not seem to know that he had done it.

“His Highness lived,” said Valentina.

Nobody in the hall breathed.

“So did the Sovereign. The Council may think that the stranger of the two.”

Every candle in the hall went down.

They bent over in their iron cups all at once, from the dais to the doors, like a congregation going down on its knees, and burned on there sideways and blue at the root, and the shadows came off the walls with a sound like silk torn in a far room and went to the high seat and stood round it, tall and close, and the cold came down the hall from it in one slow wave over the long tables and the white cloths and the faces above them, and the steam stopped rising off every glass of blood-wine on the high table at once.

Caleb sat in the glass chair with his hands on its arms and looked at the floor.

*Say no*, he thought. *You have never once told a lie that I know of, and you might begin tonight, and nobody in this hall would ever know it but your uncle and me, and he would carry it to his grave, and so should I.*

“Is it so, Sovereign?” asked the lord in the mulberry coat.

“Yes,” said Arian.

He set the glass of blood-wine down on the arm of the high seat without hurry, and the frost came out from his fingers round its foot and went down the black arm of the chair in white feathers, and he took his hand away and left it standing there.

“I would do it again.”

\* \* \*

The hall went up like a rookery when the first tree comes down.

The lesser houses were on their feet, and the hill chiefs half out of their seats with their hands gone to where their knives would have been if they had been allowed to bring them, and through all of it the one word went round the hall in every tongue the Coast had, *Accord*, *the Accord*. Somebody had knocked over a glass, and the blood-wine ran across the white cloth towards the oranges in a long dark finger and soaked into the linen and stopped. Somebody among the Nine was saying *elven blood*, high and thin, and another, older, was saying *from the throat, from a living throat*, as one repeats an indecency at table to be certain one has heard it, and a third said nothing at all and looked at the Sovereign as a gambler looks at a card he would have sworn had already been played. Among the guildmasters one man had not risen, and his empty eyes were still on the glass chair.

Into the first lull, from the long table nearest the dais, the lord in smoke grey laughed.

“And how does the sun taste, Sovereign?”

He had half risen to say it, with his glass in his hand, and he did not finish rising. The frost came up out of the white cloth under his hand as if it had lain there all evening waiting to be asked, and took the hand and the glass together, and went up his sleeve to the shoulder, crackling, as rime climbs a reed at the edge of a pond. The laugh stopped in his mouth because his mouth had stopped. His lips went white, and split, both together, as a plum splits on the branch in a hard frost, and what came out of the splits was dark and slow and froze on his chin as it came. Two of the fingers round the stem of the glass had gone white to the knuckle, and Caleb heard one of them go, dry and quick, like a stick underfoot on a winter path. His lashes had furred white, and he could not shut his eyes.

Nobody near him moved. The lesser houses on either side had drawn their chairs away from him without getting up, with a long scrape on the glass, and sat looking at the tablecloth.

At the high seat Arian had not so much as turned his head. His hand lay on the black arm of the chair beside the glass he had set down there, and the frost went on climbing, over the smoke-grey collar, towards the throat that had asked.

“Sovereign,” said Caleb.

He did not say it loudly. He said it as he had once said a frightened man’s own name to him on a table in the Harp Vales, to fetch him back, and he did not know he was going to say it until it was said.

The frost stopped at the collar. Then it went back, down the sleeve and off the hand and into the cloth, all of it, as the bloom goes off a cold pane when a lamp is set on the sill. The man tore his hand free of the glass and left the skin of his palm on the stem behind it, and the end of one finger with it, and sat with the raw hand clutched to his chest and his split mouth open, making no sound at all.

Arian’s face was as white and still as the cloth, and it gave nothing to Caleb or to anyone, but every soul in the hall had seen what had stopped it. His head turned a finger’s breadth towards the wall behind the glass chair. Caleb did not know what he was listening to. Then it turned back.

Eleanor had not risen. She sat at the Sovereign’s left with her gloved hands folded on the cloth and her treaty-table face on, and looked at nobody but her brother.

The lord of the inland vineyards had not shouted, and he had not watched the man in grey. When what was left of the noise had worn itself out against the columns he spoke into it, and it went quiet to hear him.

“The Consigliere will tell the Council what the Accord says.”

Enzo rose, and Caleb saw his hand go to the breast of his coat and press flat and come away. He did not look at his nephew. He looked down the hall at Valentina, standing alone among the moons with her chin up.

“It says what Donna Valentina says it says.”

The hall waited, and he gave it Article the Sixth in his ledger voice, the dry flat voice in which he had read a thousand reports to a thousand Councils, word for word as the Lyssois had it.

“It has been laid. The envoy is present. The Accord is broken in that part.”

“And the Sovereign does not deny it,” pressed the mulberry lord.

“The Sovereign has never in his life denied anything,” said Enzo. “It is a grave failing in a ruler.”

Nobody laughed. Enzo sat down.

“The Article is very plain,” said Don Salvatore.

He had not risen even now. His voice came up out of his young smooth face as dry and old as a leaf blown in at a temple door, and he spoke as if he were reading the terms of a will to a family he was fond of.

“And kind enough to tell us what must follow. His Highness is to be restored to his house, if his house require it.” He turned his head at last, unhurried, and looked along the high table past the Sovereign to the woman in the Chancellery’s blue. “And by the happiest of chances, his house is at table. I understand that Her Highness carries the Throne’s warrant, with full power to receive His Highness into her keeping should the Coast at any time be found in breach of the Accord upon his person.”

He smiled at her.

“I believe those are the words.”

Eleanor did not move for a moment. Then she held out her gloved hand behind her without looking, and her clerk, white to the lips, opened the despatch case and put a paper into it. She unfolded it on the cloth. Caleb saw the Throne’s great sunburst at its foot in gold wax, and the black lines above in a clerk’s hand, and her eyes going down them as they went down every page she had ever been handed, fast, from the top, and stopping near the bottom at a clerk’s caution she had carried across the strait in her own case and never once let out of her sight.

Her eyes went white.

It was there and gone, a white glare behind the blue like lightning behind a drawn blind, and the black glass at her elbow cracked across from rim to foot, quite quietly, and a thread of steam went up out of the wine. Then she had it down. She sat at the high table of the Coast with her hands flat on the Throne’s warrant, and her eyes were blue again, and her face had not changed at all.

Andrew had written to the Carusos.

Caleb knew it as she knew it, in the same breath, as the pence had passed between them since he was six. Andrew had written to the house whose blades had been drawn on the Fault road, and told them what was in his sister’s warrant, and told them about the gallery. He had known about the gallery, and kept it, and sent Eleanor to this table with the words already waiting in her case.

*How had he known?* said something at the back of Caleb’s mind, cold and quiet and clear.

There was no time to ask it. Don Salvatore was speaking again, kindly, to the envoy of the Solar Throne.

“The Council will not keep Her Highness waiting. His Highness may be put aboard her yacht at the first bell, and the Coast may answer to its own Council for its Sovereign’s appetites.” He spread his white hands on the cloth. “It need only be said.”

Under Caleb’s palms, the gold thread in the glass chair woke.

It caught like a wick put to a taper, and came up out of the depths of the glass in a single bright line, like a seam of ore struck by a lamp in a deep gallery, and ran the whole length of both arms of the chair, and he looked down at it and understood that he was angrier than he had been since a tired man smiled at him over a glass of Citadel white. *Put aboard her yacht.* As if he were a parcel. As if he were to be a parcel twice, sent there in his sleep and sent back in it, by people who had made up their minds about him in a room he was not in.

He saw Eleanor see the thread. Her eyes went to it down the length of the high table, burning in the glass under his hands, and stayed there.

Then he stood up.

\* \* \*

He went down to the floor with the gold running in every seam of the Coast’s black silk as he moved, and the candles that had bent over in their cups since Valentina spoke lifted their heads one after another down both walls, and leaned.

He walked out among the reflected moons to where Valentina Caruso stood alone in the middle of the floor, and stopped a few paces from her, and turned to face the Nine.

“Donna Valentina has told the Council what was done to me. She has told it very well. She has left out only what was done for me, which I suppose is what the Accord is for.”

Valentina inclined her head a fraction, as a fencer does to a touch.

“I am Caleb of the Solar House,” said Caleb, “second son of George, King of Aloria. I was given to this Coast asleep, in a cup of wine, by a house that required no inquiry. I have stood in this hall in white silk to be looked at, and in ivory to sit down. Tonight I stand in it in the Coast’s black, and I should be obliged if the Council would look at that as well, while it decides what is to be done with me, because in ten weeks nobody in any court in the world has thought to ask me anything at all.

“Donna Valentina is right. Eleven nights before she came to this hall, I knelt in the lower yard and healed a man who would have been dead before I could finish saying his name. I spent more than I had, in a country with no sun to fill me again, and any physician of the Harp Vales could tell the Council what comes next. Having nothing left to spend, the Grace spends the healer. I was being spent. I lay on the floor of the black gallery with the light pouring out of me faster than I could hold it, and in a quarter of an hour there would have been nothing left of me to send home to my father on a cushion.”

He looked along the Nine one face after another, and came to the mulberry coat and the shut white flower and the ancient eyes above it, and held them. The old lord’s hand rose from the cloth and lay over the flower in his buttonhole, over the place where a heart would have been in anybody but a vampire, and stayed there, as a man lays his hand flat over a letter on his desk when somebody he is fond of comes into the room.

“The Sovereign knew what my blood would do to him. Every child of your houses knows it. He knew what the Accord says, to the letter, as well as the Consigliere does. He went down on his knees on that glass anyway and drank the fire out of me, and it burned him for ten days, and he has never once asked me to thank him for it.” He turned his head to Valentina. “He did not take my blood, Donna Valentina. He took my death, and drank it, and kept it.”

In the gallery over the doors one of the glass bells, which nobody had touched, sounded by itself, a single cold note, and died.

“I was a hostage on that floor. I have not been one since I stood before a Master Shaper of the Holds in the Hall of the Chain and said yes to him aloud, with the iron in the fire, so loudly that the clerk of the Chain wrote down a word after it that the clerks of the Chain are not paid to write. If the Council doubts me, it may send to the Undergate at Durnhollow and read the board. Somebody has underlined it in two colours of chalk.”

Along the wall a lycan of the escort barked a laugh and swallowed it, and beside him the sergeant’s fist had gone back to his heart.

At the high table Don Salvatore’s mouth had moved as well. It was there for no longer than a candle flame takes to duck and stand up again, and it was an old man’s helpless smile at a family supper, when the smallest grandchild climbs on a chair to give the toast and gets every word of it wrong, loudly and beautifully. Then he folded it away with his hands on the cloth, and was smiling at nothing again.

Caleb stood on the black glass with his hands at his sides, and the Nine looked at him, and behind them the lesser houses on their feet, and the hill chiefs and the guildmasters and the dwarves of the Holds and the Lowmere factors in their starched caps, and the fae along the sills with their wings half open, and Oriane with her pen lifted and her coin gone silent, and his sister at the high table with her hands flat on a warrant drawn by their brother, and he felt the whole of the underworld that had climbed the mountain to see what was left of the Sovereign after the Fault look at the Coast’s black silk, and the iron ring on his hand, and the gold that came through the Coast’s own colour at every seam, and he understood, as the candles had understood before him and the shadows before the candles, that he was what was left, and had been for some time, and that every one of them could see it.

The shadows of the hall, which on the Coast had leaned one way for a thousand years, leaned two.

They did it slowly. They slid from beneath the long tables and down off the columns and in from the dark of the vaulting, and some went to the high seat, as they always had, and stood about it. And some came out across the black glass among the violet moons, and lay down round Caleb’s feet, soft and dark and heavy, and stayed, and every soul in that hall who had been born on the Coast saw where they lay.

At the high table Don Salvatore had stopped turning his ring.

“I am his,” said Caleb. “By my own word, given aloud, before the Holds. I would give it again tomorrow, before this Council, or the Solar Throne, or anyone else who cares to ask. I only wish somebody would ask, instead of telling me where I am to be put.”

The bell in the gallery sounded again by itself, and was still.

He knew without looking that Arian had not moved, and that his face was giving the hall nothing at all. And he knew by the thread that had drawn tight in his chest at an anvil in Dornhöhle that something at the high seat had gone so still that it hurt.

Then the lord of the inland vineyards spoke.

“His Highness speaks beautifully.”

His voice came down the hall dry and courteous and ancient, and laid itself on the black glass between Caleb and the Nine like a ruler laid across a page.

He inclined his head, as to a performance. “The Council is moved, Highness. The Accord is not. It does not ask a hostage what he forgives. It was written so that nobody should ever need to ask him anything at all.”

Caleb stood among the moons with the shadows lying round his feet and the gold in his seams, and none of it made any difference whatever, and he had known that before he stood up. He stayed standing.

“The breach stands,” said the mulberry lord, to the high seat, without heat. “The Sovereign will answer it at the Council’s sitting.” He turned his head. “And His Highness goes home. If his house require it.”

“Does it?” asked Don Salvatore gently. “Your Highness?”

The whole hall turned to the envoy of the Solar Throne.

Eleanor rose.

She did it without haste, as she rose at the Treasury when the sitting was over and the ministers were still talking, and the lamps over her head brightened a little out of habit, and she did not look up at them. She folded the Throne’s warrant once along its crease, and again, and laid it on the white cloth and set her gloved fingers flat on top of it.

“The Throne’s warrant gives me power to require my brother. It does not oblige me to.”

Down the high table, the man in the mulberry coat leaned forward in his chair.

“In the spring,” said Eleanor, “the Solar Throne sent my brother to this Coast for a death he had nothing to do with, and asked for no inquiry. Every house on the Coast asks for one, I am told. It costs nothing, and it buys a season. Ours did not ask.” She dropped her voice, and every soul in the hall leaned to catch it. “I do not propose to fetch him back the way he was sent, asleep, on a yacht, with his own opinion left behind on the shore.”

She looked down the hall at Caleb, standing on the black glass in the Coast’s silk.

“The Solar Throne requires an inquiry. Before witnesses of the Holds, within the season, into what was done in the gallery and what has been done since, at which His Highness shall be heard. Until it is held, he stays where he is.”

She did not put it in the pence. She put it in the main figures, aloud, in the great hall of Castel Nerezza before the Nine and the Holds and the fae press and every house of the underworld, and then she sat down and laid her hands on the folded warrant, and her face was perfectly composed, and only her eyes, which had been so white a little while before, were blue and bright and wet, and fixed on her brother.

Don Salvatore looked at her a long moment, pleasantly.

“The Consigliere,” he said, without turning his head, “will tell us whether the Accord permits it.”

Enzo did not rise this time. He sat at the end of the Nine and looked at the Arch-Chancellor of Aloria as Caleb had seen him look once before, at the garden door, over a column of untidy pence.

“Any house may require an inquiry. The hostage does not pass until it is done.”

“And the breach?” asked the lord of the inland vineyards.

“Stands,” answered Enzo. “And until the inquiry is held, the Accord is loosed for both houses alike. The Solar Throne may hold itself released from whatever the Accord forbids it.”

Caleb was looking at Valentina when Enzo said it, because she was nearest, and so he saw it.

It was not much. She stood a few paces from him where she had laid her charge, with her chin up and the black thorn at her throat, and at *whatever the Accord forbids it* her hand went to the thorn and closed on it. Her face did not change by a hair. But her eyes went from Enzo to the high table, to Eleanor with her hands on her brother’s warrant, and from her to her grandfather, who was turning his garnet ring again and smiling at nothing and had not heard what she had heard, and for the space of a breath she looked at him as one looks at a stranger who has sat down in a familiar chair.

Then she let go of the thorn, and the look was gone.

“The Council will sit upon it,” said Arian, “in its own time.”

He had not moved through any of it. He moved now. He took the black glass of blood-wine off the arm of the high seat, where the frost had locked round its foot, and it came away with a sharp crack, and he lifted it. There was ice across the top of the wine. He did not look at it.

“Tonight the Coast has guests.”

He looked down the hall at the gallery over the doors, where the musicians sat with their bows lifted and their faces white, and the bell player held his felted hammers above the glass as if he had forgotten what they were for.

“Play.”

They played.

\* \* \*

The viols went under first, low and slow, and then the bells came in over them one at a time, clear and cold, and Caleb knew the tune before he had heard three notes of it, though he had never heard it in his life. It was very long. It went round and round on itself like the great stair, each turn a little colder than the last, and somewhere in the gallery a voice began to sing it in Vesperian, softly, and he could not understand one word in five. He did not need to. He had been told in a sheep cave what it was.

The dancers did not come back onto the floor. The underworld sat down at its long tables in ones and twos, and picked up its glasses and found it did not wish to drink, and listened to the bell that wished to be a lamp.

Valentina crossed the floor to him.

She came as she had come on the night of the refusal, calmly, through all of it, as if she were crossing a ballroom to claim a dance, and stopped before him on the black glass. The shadows round his feet did not move out of her way. She looked at them, and then at him.

“You have a sister, Highness,” she murmured, “who can add.”

“I have three. Two of them can add.”

“Your brother thought he had one.” She drew her gloves a little tighter at the wrist, one and then the other. “He writes a very beautiful letter. He was quite certain of her.”

Caleb said nothing. The bells went round.

“He did not write,” said Valentina, “what else it would loose.”

She looked at him a moment longer, with something in her face that he had not seen there before and could not read. Then she sank into the same full curtsey as before, and went back across the black glass to her grandfather, and sat down beside him, and did not lay her hand on his arm.

Caleb stood where she had left him, and the cold went down his back.

*Whatever the Accord forbids it.* He thought of slivers laid out on black velvet in a foundry at the bottom of the Citadel, which had gone south in sealed crates by the back roads of the Holds, into the hands of men who could be erased in the dark of a gorge, because the Accord would not let them go openly into the white-gloved hands of the Radiant Guard. Tonight a Caruso had loosed it.

He went back up the step and sat down in the glass chair. The thread in the glass had gone down to a glow under his hands. It did not go out.

Beside him, without turning his head, Arian breathed, so low that it barely stirred the cold between them, “You should not have stood up.”

“You should not have said yes.”

“I do not lie to the Nine.”

“No,” said Caleb. “You only hand them the rope, and show them which beam will bear it.”

Arian was silent. The bells went round, and the voice in the gallery sang the bell’s long wish to the dark, and the shadows lay round the glass chair and round the high seat and would not choose between them.

“Thank you,” said Arian.

Caleb looked straight ahead down the hall at the violet moons on the empty floor, until they blurred and doubled under the lamps.

“That is six.”

“I know.”

The song went on. It did not end well. He had been told that it would not, and he sat in the glass chair at the Sovereign’s right hand in the Coast’s black, with a tin of violets in one pocket and the Sovereign’s ring behind the crescent of the Sovereign’s house, and listened to it all the way to the end. The lamps had been turned down for the singing, and in the lowered light, in the buttonhole of the lord of the inland vineyards, the pale flower that had kept itself shut all evening began to open, slowly, while Caleb watched, and the man who wore it never once looked down.
