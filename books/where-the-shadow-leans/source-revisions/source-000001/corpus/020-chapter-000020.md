## Chapter 19: Chapter Eighteen - The Citadel

*Item, against the Summit at Old Meridian, for His Royal Highness the Prince Caleb in his eighth year, one cloak of the grey wool of the Vales, worked through with silver thread, the sunburst at the collar set in gold by Her Majesty’s own needle. Not to be got muddy.*

*Returned to the wardrobe on the return from the Marches, nothing entered. The whole entry struck through in red, in Her Majesty’s hand.*

— From the wardrobe book of the royal nursery of the Citadel, in the year of the last Long Eclipse

\* \* \*

Somebody had closed his shutters.

He saw it from the doorway, and stood on the threshold of his own rooms with his brother’s men at his back, and could not go in. Every shutter down the long room was drawn to and barred, oak sheathed in lead. In twenty-eight years he had never once closed them. He had slept with them folded back and the white light of the spires lying across his coverlet like a cat that has got in and will not be put out, until the whole palace had given him up and let him lie in the light as he pleased. Now the long room stood in a stale and stopped-up dark, as close as the inside of a jewel case, and the light came into it only at the cracks, in long white threads laid across the carpet like the slits a cook cuts in the lid of a pie to let the steam out.

He went in.

Nothing had been moved. His books lay where he had left them on the evening the King came to his rooms, open and face down on every surface, on the window seats and the arms of the chairs and the shut lid of the harpsichord, their spines cracked in the way Eleanor said ought to be a hanging matter. A pencil with its end bitten lay across a treatise on the draining of the Lowmere polders. The lilies in the tall vase by the door had died standing up and gone the colour of old parchment, and the water under them had gone green and thick, and they had been left where they stood, because nobody had been let in to change them.

And the room smelled of lilies that were not the dead ones. It smelled of white lilies and orris powder, faint and sweet and grave, the scent of a woman who had sat in the brightest window of the palace on every morning of his childhood with her face lifted to the light, as the lilies in her vases lifted theirs. It was in the curtains and the cushions and the coverlet of his bed. On the end of the bed, in one of the long threads of white, the coverlet held a hollow the length of a woman lying on her side with her knees drawn up, and at the head of the hollow, folded in four, there was a white shawl.

He stood and looked at it.

Then he went down the room and threw back the shutters, one after another, as he always had. The bars came up out of their brackets with a groan of old iron, and the heavy leaves swung round and folded back against the walls with a dull sound, like the lid of a linen chest let fall, and the noon came in.

It came in white. It came in at every window at once, off the white terraces and the white walls and the white-hot crowns of the spires that stood up over the palace roofs as they had stood over every hour of his life, and it filled the long room to the cornices as water fills a lock. The dust that had lain on everything for eleven weeks went up into it and turned there, slowly, gold, like thistledown over a field at harvest. It went into him. His blood stood up to meet it, as it had stood on the landing, and the gold rose in his palms and at his throat and ran along every grey-bound seam of the black silk, and he let it, since there was no stopping it, and stood in the middle of the room he had been born to, in the light he had been born in, and could not find anything in it that was his.

He had promised notes. He turned the Lowmere polders over to the endpaper and wrote across the top of it in his long untidy hand, *The noon of the Citadel*, and then sat on the arm of a chair with the book on his knee and looked at what he had written, because he did not know what colour it was. It had always been there. Nobody in twenty-eight years had ever asked him.

\* \* \*

The first bell of the night went over the city a little after.

It began in the great tower of the Throne Hall and went down the terraces from tower to tower, each a breath behind the last and a note lower, until the deep bell of the refineries at the bottom of the city took it up and held it. All down the white hill the Citadel began to go to bed. Shutters closed along the terraces, house after house, with a soft booming like a row of books being shut by a librarian at the end of the day. On the walls the watch called the hour, *First bell, and all’s bright*, as the watch had called it for a thousand years without anybody asking bright compared to what. And above the city tucked up behind its lead, the spires blazed on, white, under a sky as white as it had been at breakfast.

On the Coast it would be the violet hour, a little thinner than the rest. Somebody would be building up a fire in a long room of candles, beside a gold chair with a grey cloak over its arm.

There was a knock at the door, two light taps of a ring on the wood, and he knew it.

The man who came in was long and pale and stooped a little, as if he had spent his life bending to hear people who did not raise their voices, and he wore the Queen’s white livery with a gold key on a ribbon at his breast. Over his arm, in tissue, he carried a coat of white silk so stiff with gold thread that it stood out from his sleeve on its own. He bowed to the depth the book of precedence allows a prince of the blood, and not a hair’s breadth lower, and his face was composed into a blankness so perfectly courteous that Caleb would have known it anywhere, over the rim of any glass.

“Your Royal Highness. His Royal Highness the Crown Prince sends you his love, and desires that you be made comfortable after your journey. I am to take the garment of the Coast.” His eyes went once to the black crescent over Caleb’s heart and came away from it, as a hand comes away from a stove. “To be burned.”

“No,” said Caleb.

The chamberlain waited, as chamberlains wait, for the rest of the sentence.

There was no rest of the sentence. Caleb stayed where he was on the arm of the chair and let him wait, and the light lay on the black silk between them.

“You poured my wine.”

“I did, Your Royal Highness.”

“You looked tired.”

Something happened at the corners of the composed face, and was mended.

“I had not slept, Highness,” admitted the chamberlain. “Nor had Her Majesty. She sat up the whole of the night before with the bottle on the table in front of her, and in the morning she chose the glass, and put it into my hands herself.” He stopped. “I am not to speak of it.”

“Then do not. I find I am not equal to hearing it from you.” He laid the book down on the seat of the chair. “You may tell my brother that I am comfortable. You may tell him that I have kept my coat on, which a prince of the Solar House is entitled to do in his own rooms, and that anybody who wishes to take it off me is welcome to come and try, and I shall be perfectly civil to him while he does, and he will not enjoy it in the least.”

The chamberlain looked at the gold that had come up along the seams of the coat while he spoke, and did not take a step nearer. He laid the white silk across a chair instead, with great care, smoothing the tissue under it as if the coat might take a chill.

“It is for the crowning, Highness.”

“Whose?”

The chamberlain looked at him. Then he understood, and the long years of his service showed for a breath in the stoop of him, and he was sorry.

“His Majesty has laid down the crown, Highness. It was proclaimed in the Throne Hall four days ago, at the fourth bell. His Majesty is King until it is set on another head, as the law is, for the sun does not leave one sky until there is another ready for it. The Crown Prince holds the seals.” He folded his long hands. “The crowning is on the fifth day from this, on the eve of the Long Eclipse. The Eclipse falls on the sixth. The envoys of the Dominion are coming up already for the one, and will stay for the other.”

Five days, and then six. He laid the numbers down beside the others, beside a Leaning and an inquiry and a ring going under a dwarf’s hammer, all on one dark morning, and they lay there and would not be added up.

“And my father?”

“His Majesty keeps his study, Highness.”

“He always has.”

“With the shutters closed, Highness.”

He bowed again, the same depth, and went out. At the door he lifted his feet carefully over something on the floor of the corridor, as a man steps over a dog asleep in a doorway, and was gone.

Caleb got up and went to see what it was.

It was a blanket. It was grey wool, a soldier’s blanket, folded lengthwise and laid across the threshold from jamb to jamb, as it had been laid across every threshold Caleb had slept behind since he was fourteen, and Paul was sitting on it with his back against the white wall of the corridor and his big hands open on his knees. Somebody had given him Alorian livery, clean, and he had put it on, and there was still ash in the creases of his knuckles.

He got up when he saw Caleb, slowly, stiff from the floor, as if he had not felt it until now.

“Highness.”

Caleb had looked at that face on the black stones of a lower yard with his hands in a torn side and a lung knitting under his palms. He looked at it now, under the noon of the corridor, and found that he had nothing at all to say to it.

He shut the door.

Then he went back down the long room and closed the shutters again, one after another, and dropped the bars into their brackets. He had never done it before, and found that he knew how. The noon went out of the room leaf by leaf, as if somebody were folding it up and putting it away in a drawer, until there was nothing left of it but the threads at the cracks, and he was glad of the dark, and did not wish to think about why.

He lay down on his bed in the black coat, on the side away from the hollow, with his boots on and his hand on the crescent. Under his palm the ring lay between the silk and Vesna’s stitches, the one cold thing in the room.

Then, because he had promised, he counted. He counted in the dark under his breath, as the Vales teach, with two fingers on the inside of his wrist, and his heart went on under them steady and warm and his own, and somewhere past ninety he felt for the other, as one feels in the dark for a stair one knows is there.

It was there.

It was faint. It came to him from a long way off, down the thread that two strokes of a hammer had drawn through him in the Hall of the Chain, over the Marches and the strait and the black spine of the Coast, as the stroke of a far bell comes up through a hillside into a man lying on it, from a tower he cannot see. It was keeping his count, beat for beat, a long way south, in the dark.

The thread ached. It ached as a tooth aches in the cold, all along its length, from his breastbone away south into nothing, and he lay and let it, because it was the only part of him in all that city that knew where it was going.

He was at two hundred and eleven when the door opened.

\* \* \*

The white of the corridor came in round the edges of a figure in white, and the door closed on it, and the dark came back.

She had not brought a lamp. She came down the long room without one, as a woman crosses a room she has crossed so many nights that her feet know where the chairs are and her hands know the corners of the harpsichord, and she sat down on the end of the bed. He felt the bed give. He heard her breathe out, long and slow, as if she had been holding it since the morning, and she lay down on her side in the hollow she had made, and reached for the shawl.

Her hand stopped.

In the dark of the shuttered room there was a light. It was faint and gold and drawn in lines, along the seams of a black coat, at the shoulders and the sleeves and down the breast, as if a tailor had chalked the coat out on the dark with a stick of light and gone away to fetch his scissors. Caleb lay inside it and did not move.

His mother sat up.

“I have dreamed this,” said the Queen, “a great many times. You are never wearing black.”

“Good evening, Mother.”

She did not move. Then her hand came across the coverlet in the dark, slowly, as a hand goes out to a moth on a sill to see whether it will fly, and found his ankle, and closed on it through the boot, and held on.

“Andrew did not tell me.”

“Andrew wanted nobody at the step but himself.”

“When?”

“At noon. It was very much like every other hour.”

She did not let go of his ankle. In the gold of his seams he could see a little of her now, the white of her gown and the white of her hands, and her hair, which in all his life he had never seen down, lying loose over her shoulders in the dark like a girl’s. She had her face turned towards him. She had his eyes, or he had hers. He could not see them.

“They told me you had been harmed,” she said.

“Who told you?”

“Eleanor.” The name came out of her as if it had edges. “She came home from the Coast and laid the Crown Prince’s warrant on his desk, and came into the Throne Hall and stood at the foot of the dais and told us, before the whole court, what had been done to you in a gallery. She told it in her Chancellery voice, as if she were reading a column of figures. She left out nothing.”

Her fingers tightened on his ankle.

“Your father heard her to the end. Then he got up off the throne and walked away down the whole length of the white floor, with the court standing, and went into his study and shut the door. In the morning he sent for the seals. He said that he could not sit above that floor any longer.”

“And you?”

“I sit here.”

“I know. You left your shawl.”

She made a sound in the dark that was not a laugh and had once, a long time ago, been related to one.

“You chose the wine.”

The hand on his ankle did not move.

“Yes.”

“Your chamberlain says you sat up all night with the bottle.”

“I chose it out of your father’s cellar,” said the Queen. “A Citadel white, of the year you were born. There were six left. I thought that if you must sleep, you should sleep on something you would have liked.” Her voice did not crack. It had been trained not to from the cradle, as his had. “You did like it. He told me you smiled at him over the glass.”

He had smiled. He had been thinking that the chamberlain looked tired.

“And you read the other warrant first. Twice.”

The bed went still under her, as the deck of a yacht goes still in a lull.

“The household purse, at the second bell of the night, eleven crowns for the register of the Chalmont bridge. Sealed with the sun and the lily. You read everything twice, Mother. You read the laundry book. You correct the spelling in it. And then you find a fault, because there is always a fault, and you send it back.” He looked at the pale blur of her face. “Did you find one in that?”

“No,” answered the Queen. “It was very well drawn. Andrew drew it.”

Somewhere far down the hill a shutter that had been forgotten was closed, late, with a bang, and the watch on the nearer wall called out after it in a tone of mild reproof.

“I knew you had never been at the vineyard,” said his mother, “before your father had finished reading the Coast’s letter aloud. You have never once in your life gone anywhere without telling me first what you meant to eat when you got there.”

He laughed. He did not mean to. It came up out of him in the dark with the gold, and the seams of his coat flared with it, and for a moment he could see her face, all of it, white and fine and wearier than he had left it, with her mouth pressed shut against something. Then the gold went down again and he could not.

“Then why?”

She was quiet so long that he thought she would not answer.

“Because I had two sons, and the Accord asked for one of them, and your father had already said which. There was nothing left in the world for me to choose, except whether you went awake.”

“You might have asked for a season.”

“I asked.” It came low. “Once. Your father said that a season would bring the registers, and the registers would bring the Holds, and the Holds would bring the Coast into the Throne Hall to take Andrew out of it before the court, and there would be no heir, and a war, and you would be dead in it with all the rest of us, a little later and a great deal less comfortably. He said it at the window. He did not turn round.” Her breath went in. “I have never in my life been able to argue with your father’s back.”

“So I went to sleep.”

“So you went to sleep. It was the second time.”

He lifted his head off the pillow.

“The second time what?”

His mother let go of his ankle. She sat on the end of his bed in the dark with her hands in her lap, and he heard the rings on them knock together, softly, as she folded them one over the other. They had fitted her in the spring. Now they slid down to her knuckles whenever she moved her hands.

“That I have chosen you should sleep through the worst of it,” said the Queen.

“You do not remember,” she said. “You never did. I saw to it.”

“Tell me.”

“Matilda carried you down out of that city in her cloak. She was sixteen, and she came down the hill into the tents running, in the middle of the Eclipse, with that black sun over everything and the whole world gone to shadow, and she would not give you to anybody. She would not give you to me. You were so cold that the physician of the Vales had to take her by the wrists and open her fingers one by one.” Her voice had gone level, as a voice goes that is reading aloud from a page it knows. “And you had no cloak.”

He lay still.

“It was a good grey cloak. I had told you four times that morning not to get it muddy.”

He had been told on no fewer than four occasions. He had remembered that much in a cave, in the dark, with his hands on a wound. He had not remembered whose voice.

“And there was blood on your hands,” said the Queen. “To the wrists. None of it was yours. There was not a scratch on you anywhere. It was dark, nearly black, and it had dried in the lines of your palms and under every nail, as the earth dries on a gardener’s hands at the end of a day at the beds.” The rings knocked together once in her lap. “The physician looked at your hands, and then at me, and went out of the tent and stood outside it with his back to the flap. So I washed them. I had never washed anything in my life. I did it in the basin by your cot, with my sleeves turned back, and the water went dark, and I changed it, and it went dark again. I carried it out myself into the mist behind the tents and poured it into the long grass, and stood on the place until it had gone in.”

In the faint gold of his seams his own hands lay open on the black silk. He looked at them. They were clean.

“The physician sat with you three days. On the second night he came out of your tent and told your father and me that you had spent yourself to the root. That a grown healer might do it once in his life, on something that was dying, and not live, and that he had never seen a child of eight do it at all. He asked me what you had healed.”

Her hands moved in her lap.

“I could not tell him. And on that same day the Sovereign of the Coast lay dead in their fountain court with three blades in him, and their Consigliere was going from tent to tent all that night with a lantern, asking every house who had been in the city. And our son had come down out of it cold as a stone, without his cloak, and the sunburst of this house on the collar of it lying somewhere up there in the mist for anybody to pick up.”

“And Father said.”

“Your father said that if a Solar cloak was found in that city on that day, there would be war by the next new moon. And the physician said, low, at the door of the tent, that if it was one of the Umbral houses you had laid your hands on, the Vales would never take you back. You had been there two years. You loved it so.” She drew breath. “So I took my pen, the next morning, and struck the cloak out of the wardrobe book. And your father had the *Lantern* told that you had taken a chill in the mist.”

The dark pressed on his eyes. He could hear his own heart, and feel under it, a long way off, the other.

“And then you woke. On the yacht, on the third day, with the Marches going by under us. And the first thing you asked for was your cloak. And the second thing you asked for was a boy.”

He shut his eyes, though it made no difference in that dark.

“You said there was a boy in the roses in a black coat, with a hole in his side, and that he had told you what he was. You said you had not been afraid of him. So there.” Her voice did something on the last two words that it had not done in all the rest of it, and she stopped, and mended it. “You said he had not got a heart, so you had counted yours for him. You held my hands and asked me to help you find him.”

*Help me find him*, another boy had whispered into another woman’s collar, halfway down a white street, under a black sun. And the woman had had no answer to give him, and had given him one anyway, in the quietest way she knew, for twenty years.

“I said it was the fever. Every time you spoke of him. I sat by you a month, and every time, I said it was the fever, my darling, and stroked your hair, and you went to sleep. You asked me for nine days. On the tenth you asked me whether he had been a dream. And I said yes.” She was silent a moment. “You turned your face to the wall. And on the eleventh you sat up and asked for cake.”

He had read it in a grey-bound volume in a study on the Coast, with a pencil line beside it. *Is sitting up, and has asked for cake.*

He had thought it was the fever, every year of his life, whenever anybody mentioned Old Meridian at dinner and he made the joke about the cat on the windowsill. He had remembered it all on the floor of the Fault with his hands in a sun-wound, twenty years late, and wept for a boy who had lain in the roses saying *Stay*, and thought as he wept that a child of eight forgets, as children forget the name of a dog they loved at a summer house. And now, now that he lay in the dark in the black of a house that had kept a grey cloak for twenty years with its label still tied on, now that the woman who had found the boy was dead in a cellar and the woman who had found him sat at the end of his bed with her rings knocking together in her lap, he understood that the reason he had not remembered had never been the fever at all.

It had been his mother.

He did not say anything for a long time. Neither did she, and the threads of white at the shutters lay across the dark floor between them.

“It is on the Coast,” he said at last.

He heard her go still.

“The cloak. A woman found him in the glasshouse with it round him. She was tall, and she wore black, and when she saw him she dropped where she stood, among the thorns, with her skirts still in her fists. I saw her. I looked back.”

He kept his voice as level as hers had been.

“She washed it in salt water and darned the hem in silver where the briars had torn it, and left the collar as it was, because it would not wash out. She tied a cellar label to it, *Not ours. To be given back*, and put it away, and helped him look for me for twenty years. He put it into my hands himself. It is lying over the arm of a gold chair by a fire on the Coast, because I asked him to keep it for me.”

The Queen said nothing. In the dark the white of her gown did not stir.

“She wrote to him in her last spring that when he brought me home to her, she would feed me.”

“Then I should like to thank her,” said his mother, after a while, in a voice he did not know.

“You cannot. She is the woman in the cellar.”

He felt it go into her, as he had once felt a blade go into a man on the Fault road through a thread in his own chest, a long way off and all at once. She did not cry out. She sat on the end of his bed in the dark, and the rings in her lap stopped knocking together, and that was all.

He had not said it to hurt her. It had hurt her all the same, and it was true, and he lay and looked at the dark and did not take it back.

“I worked that sunburst,” said the Queen at last. “On the collar. The winter you were seven. I pricked my thumb on every ray of it.”

A long way south, the little sun at its collar had woken when it drank his light, and given it back, as every sunburst of the house was made to do. He had thought, holding it in his hands, that some good needlewoman of the nursery had set it.

The thread in his chest pulled once, faintly, as a fish pulls on a line in deep water, and was still.

“Is he kind to you?”

Caleb thought of a great many answers. He thought of a wine cask, and a chain, and a pair of white hands opened to the tendon on a cage of silver, and of a man sitting on an iron sill every night since the garden was made with his back to the frame, at the very edge of the light, and never once crossing it.

“He asks. Every time.”

His mother sat with that in the dark. He did not help her with it.

“Andrew says you are enthralled.”

“Andrew says that whatever I say is proof of it.”

“Yes. I have heard him.” She was quiet. “Be careful of your brother, Caleb. He is not well. He has not slept since the spring, and I cannot make him eat.” Her rings knocked together once in her lap, and were still. “I have two sons.”

“I know, Mother. You told me.”

She bowed her head. He saw the pale blur of it go down, and come up.

“Will you let me stay?”

He thought about it. He made himself think about it honestly, as he would have thought about a patient and not as a son, and found that he could no longer tell the difference.

“You may sit where you have been sitting. I am not going to say anything kind.”

“No. I should not believe it if you did.” She sat upright in her white with her hands folded, as she sat on the dais at a long ceremony when her feet hurt and nobody was permitted to know. “Do your sisters know that you are here?”

“Andrew told nobody.”

She stood up. He felt the bed lift as her weight left it, and saw the white of her gown go up into the dark, and stand there.

“Then they shall know it by the first bell of the day.”

It was the first thing she had said all night in the voice of a queen, and she said it to the shutters as if they had argued with her. Then she sat down again in the hollow, and folded her hands.

He laid two fingers on the inside of his wrist, and found his place.

“Two hundred and twelve,” he said, under his breath.

“What are you counting?” asked his mother.

“A promise.”

She did not ask whose. He counted on to three hundred, with the other heart keeping his time a long way south, and at the foot of the bed, in the dark, his mother lay down at last in the hollow that eleven weeks of nights had worn, with her knees drawn up, and this time there was somebody in the bed.

\* \* \*

The summons came at the second bell of the day, on a silver salver, in a square hand he had known all his life.

*Come up to me. A.*

His mother was gone. The hollow at the foot of the bed was still warm when he laid his hand in it. Paul was on his feet in the corridor with the blanket rolled under his arm, and he fell in behind Caleb without a word, two paces back, as he always had, and Caleb did not turn round and did not tell him not to, and they went up through the palace like that, through galleries of primrose and rose silk that bowed as he passed and stared at the black, with the whole of the morning between them.

The King’s study stood at the end of the long west gallery, where a fae courier had once sat in a window seat with the noon across his knees and written down every bell. The door was shut.

There was no light under it.

In the Citadel there was always light under a door, a white line laid along the bottom of every door in the palace at every hour, so that a child could tell from the far end of a gallery whether a room was empty by whether anybody’s feet broke the line. Under his father’s door the boards were dark. Caleb stopped. He had stood here a thousand times with something to ask, and gone in, and found his father at the window with his back to the room and the light all round him, a shape cut out of the white, and been told to wait a season.

A tray stood on the floor against the wainscot beside the door, under a silver cover, with a napkin folded on it into a lily as the kitchens had folded the King’s napkins for forty years. The cover had misted on the inside, and dried again. Nobody had lifted it.

He lifted his hand.

He thought of opening that door and finding his father sitting in a shuttered room like a man in a cellar, with no light to stand against, so that for once Caleb would have to see his face.

He put his hand down, and went on.

\* \* \*

Andrew kept his study in the east tower at the top of a stair so narrow that two men could not pass on it, and the noon came in at its arrow-slits in white blades that crossed the stair one above another like the rungs of a ladder. At the top Paul stopped and stood against the wall by the door, and Caleb went in alone.

The shutters were open a hand’s width. It was the way Andrew had always kept them, so that one blade of white light lay along the boards, and the long table stood beside it, and a lamp burned on the table that no one in the Citadel had ever needed.

Everything in the room was scorched.

Nothing had been let burn. But the edges of every white sheet on the long table had gone brown, and the Treasury’s returns stacked at its end were singed along their spines, and the oak of the table, where it lay nearest the light, had been charred in a print. It was a man’s right hand laid flat with the fingers a little spread, burned into the wood as a cooper’s brand goes into a cask head, and over it another, a little to one side, and another over that, two dozen of them, until the oak in that place had gone black and glossy, like the bottom of a pan that has stood on the fire every night of a long winter. The room smelled of scorched paper, and under the scorch of something sweet that had begun to turn, like a bowl of plums forgotten on a warm sill.

Andrew sat beside them in his grey with a glass of water in front of him.

He did not look up at once. He had a twist of white paper in his fingers, folded as the apothecaries of the lower terraces fold a dose of powder, and he opened it with great care and tipped it over the glass. What came out was white, and gold, and finer than flour. It did not sink. It hung in the water and turned there, glittering, and the water took a faint gold glow from it, like a glass of the Vales white held up to a window.

Andrew drank it, all of it, steadily, as a man drinks a draught he does not wish to taste, and set the glass down.

For a moment nothing happened. Then the white came into his eyes all at once and stood there, a hard white glare across the table and the papers and the burnt prints, and the blade of light on the boards brightened as if somebody had pulled the shutter a finger wider, though nobody had. A lustre came up on his skin. It spread from his throat and his hands over his face, a fine gold sheen like the glaze on a good pot, and when the white had gone down out of his eyes he looked, for the space of a few breaths, like the portrait of the Crown Prince in the Hall of Petitions that had been painted when he was twenty-five.

Caleb had been a physician since he was fourteen, and he looked under the glaze.

The gold lay over hollows under the cheekbones and did not fill them. The lips were dry, and split at one corner where they had been bitten, and the fingertips carried burns old and new, the neat shiny burns of a man whose own light has got into his fingers without his leave, and two of the nails on the right hand had come away at some time and grown back thick and yellowed and ridged as a hoof. And at the edge of the gold on Andrew’s temple, where the hair began, there was a greyness in the skin, the grey that comes up under the gold of a Solar child kept too long from the sun. He had seen it once before, on his own hands, under plum velvet on the Coast, in the third week.

“Sit down, Caleb,” said his brother.

Caleb did not sit. He stood across the table from him with the blade of light lying between them on the boards.

Andrew looked at the black coat, and at the crescent, and away.

“I have written it out for you.” He laid a sheet of paper on the scorched oak and pushed it across to him with one finger. “You need only learn it. You were always quick.”

It was in Andrew’s square, careful hand, the hand of the Treasury’s returns, without a word crossed out. *I, Caleb of the Solar House, was taken from my father’s house under the Accord and carried into the dark, where I was held against my will, and fed upon by force, and kept by such craft as is not natural to our people. I renounce the Sovereign of the Obsidian Coast, and the ring he put upon my hand, and every word that was taken from me in his keeping. I give thanks to the King, my brother, who brought me home.*

He read it to the end. It was very well drawn.

“At the crowning, when the sun has been set on my head and the courts have done their homage, the youngest prince of the Solar House will stand before the envoys of the Dominion and say that. Lys will be there, and the Holds, and every house of Lowmere that has a ship. The *Lantern* is to have a seat at the foot of the dais. Their correspondent came up from Lutèce three days ago and asked for it herself, and I gave it to her. The fae cannot lie. When it is printed in Lutèce that you said it, every court in the world will know that you said it. And on the morning of the Eclipse the Nine of that Coast may ask their shadows what they like about a Sovereign who keeps a prince of the Solar House by craft. They will have your word to ask it with.”

“The ring he put upon my hand.”

“Cecily can cut it off you. She has tools for cold-iron.”

“It was sealed under the bond-law of the Holds. No crown may loose it.”

“The Holds are not here,” said Andrew. “And I am not a crown yet.”

Caleb laid his finger on the paper, on the words *by such craft as is not natural to our people*, and left it there.

“Vampires cannot enthral me, Andrew. I chose him.”

In the blade of light on the boards the dust turned gold. Far down in the courtyard the guard was changing, and a halberd rang once on the flags. Andrew looked at Caleb’s finger on the paper, and then at Caleb’s face, and when he spoke it was in the voice kept for a child who has woken in the night and is saying something about the wardrobe.

“The fae say that they all say so.”

“The fae who carried me here told me she did not know how to know it. She said so in my garden, in the lilies. She had not been told how.”

“Then I know it for her.” Andrew laid his hands flat on the table on either side of the paper. “You were drugged at our table and you woke at his. You have been eleven weeks in the dark with a creature that can hear your heart across a room and knows to the beat what it will do. You were dying of the Blight in the third week, Walker told me so. And then he gave you light, and you lived. Whatever you think you chose, Caleb, you chose it in the dark, from a man who held the only lamp.”

“I chose it before the Holds,” insisted Caleb, “with the iron in the fire, out loud, so loudly that the clerk wrote a word after it.”

“I know what the clerk wrote. Walker told me that too.” Andrew’s mouth moved. “Walker wept.”

Under the crescent his heart had begun to knock, and far away at the other end of the thread he felt the other quicken with it. He made himself stand still until both had steadied.

Then he put out his hand across the table.

“Give me your wrist.”

Andrew looked at the hand as if nobody had held one out to him in some years. Perhaps nobody had. Then, slowly, he laid his own in it, palm up, and Caleb set two fingers on the inside of the wrist, and counted.

It was fast. It went under his fingers fast and thin and uneven, like the heart of a finch held in the hand, and missed, and ran on faster to catch itself up. And the hand was cold. It was cold at the fingertips, the cold of an elf too long out of the light, and it was cold here, in the east tower of the Citadel, a yard from a blade of noon, with the gold of a dose of dust still shining on the skin of it.

“How long?”

“How long what?”

“The Treasury’s own. In water, so that it does not show at the nose. You never would snuff anything, even at the Vales. How long, Andrew?”

Andrew took his hand back without haste and laid it on the table again, over the burnt prints, and the oak under it began, faintly, to smoke. He left it there. Caleb, who had been taught at the Vales to look for it before anything else, watched him not feel it.

“Nine years.”

“Why were you at the vineyard?” said Caleb.

\* \* \*

Andrew went still, and the lamp beside him burned straight up in its glass.

“You told Father you were there. You never told him why.”

“How do you know what I told Father?”

“A fae sat in a window outside his study all that day, waiting for the Throne’s answer. He cannot help writing things down. He saw you come out at the first bell of the night with blood in your palm.” Caleb looked at the right hand on the oak, and the four faint marks across the heel of it that had healed white long ago. “You told Father that you wore my signet and lost it in the cellars, and that the woman who took it from you was dead. You did not tell him what you had gone down into the cellars for.”

For a long time Andrew said nothing. The lamp burned beside him, useless, and the gold lustre on his face had begun at its edges to go dull, as gilding goes dull on a frame left in a damp room.

“Do you know what the crystals are?” he asked at last.

“Every child knows. The hearts of the old dragons, turned to stone in the southern fires.”

“Every child is told. Nobody believes it. I believed it at sixteen, when Father put the Treasury’s returns into my hands and I read them back to the first year of the grid.” His hand moved on the oak, and a fresh curl of smoke came up. “Every stone we burn is a heart, Caleb. There were only ever so many dragons, and every one of them went south to die under a vampire’s mountain. The trenches give fewer stones every year. In a hundred years the lamps of the Underglow will go dark. In two hundred, the spires. And until then any Sovereign of that Coast may put out the light of the Citadel whenever he pleases, by keeping back a ship.”

He looked up.

“The first kings did not buy their light. The old commentaries of the Vales say so, in the locked room at the top of the library that the scholars showed me because I was the heir. The first of our house carried their own sun. The blood was strong then. It has thinned with every marriage since, as a cordial thins that is watered at every pouring, until we are elves who glow a little at feasts and buy our noon by the stone from the dead.” He turned the empty glass on the oak. “I have taken the Treasury’s own refining every morning for nine years to wake what is left of it. Do you know what it gives?” He held up his hand in the blade of white, and the light went through the edges of his fingers red. “An hour. An hour of what I ought to be. And then it takes back two.”

“And the heart,” said Caleb.

“The Sanctuary had kept the greatest heart ever brought up out of the trenches, for a thousand years, behind a black iron door in a cellar under a vineyard, and never once let one of us look at it. The commentaries say that a heart of the eldest, taken into the blood of their own line, would wake whatever is asleep in it. Not for an hour.” Andrew’s voice did not rise. “In the winter the ships came at half-weight. And again. And again. I sat at this table with the returns and saw what Father would not see, that it had begun. That a Conti could starve us whenever he chose, and that we would give him anything he asked for to keep the lamps lit.” His mouth moved. “Anything.”

Caleb stood still.

He had slept under them on his first night in the garden, among the white roses, and read them off a line to a stone in his sister’s pence, and lain four nights in the lilies while their gold held a white flower half-folded in his blood. Twelve crystals of the deep trench, kept back from the Alorian ships three months running before ever there was a vineyard or a dead woman or a ring in her fist, and hung on silver chains in the throat of a mountain for no reason anybody had told him, turning and turning in their light, keeping him alive.

They had been the half-weight ships.

He did not say so. His throat had closed on it.

“I asked her for it. The woman in the cellar. She refused me as courteously as I had asked. She did not raise her voice. She was holding a lamp.” His right hand had left the oak and gone up to his throat while he spoke, and its fingers were feeling there along the plain steel chain of his key, and beside it, for something finer that was not there. “And then the vines.”

The paper on the table between them had begun to brown.

It browned from the corner nearest Andrew’s other hand, the words going one by one, *I renounce* and *the Sovereign* and *the ring*, curling and darkening as the light came up out of him into the white of his eyes. Andrew did not see it. He was looking at the light.

Caleb drew the paper away across the table, out from under the heat, and laid it flat under his palm until it stopped smoking.

“I would have been the king who woke it,” said Andrew. “Do you understand? Nobody would ever again have been able to hold out a hand to the Solar House and say *give*. Not the Coast. Not anybody.” The white was going down in his eyes, slowly. “You lit the Throne Hall at a year old. You crawled across it and the columns brightened, and nobody in that hall could say afterwards who had done it. I knew. I have known it every day since.” He looked at his own hand in the light. “I have spent my whole life borrowing what you were born with, an hour at a time, out of a twist of paper.”

He reached across the scorched oak and laid the back of one cold finger against the gold at Caleb’s wrist, lightly, as a man come in out of a hard frost lays his hand on the glass of a window with a fire behind it, only to feel that it is there. Then he took it away, and looked at the finger, and closed his hand over it.

Caleb did not answer.

Something in his blood had turned over at the word *eldest*, and lay now under his breastbone, awake. It had stirred in him once before, under twelve stones. It had stood up in a glasshouse when he was eight and had nothing left to spend, and lent him its shoulder, and he had spent himself anyway, to the root, on a boy who had told him what he was. He did not know what it was. He knew that it had not come to him for taking.

He kept that to himself as well. It was not a thing he could give his brother, and he had given his brother a great many things.

“And the Underglow,” he said instead. “You sell it to them.”

“They would buy it of somebody. Better of the Treasury than of the harbour.”

“They sit in the doorways with their eyes open.”

“I know what they do. I keep a column for it.”

“And when they do not get up?”

“The same column.”

He said it as he would have said the price of lamp oil, and it stayed in the room among the burnt prints, as the smell of scorched paper stays after the window has been opened.

Andrew sat back, and when he spoke again it was in the voice he used from the dais, as if the last of it had not been said.

“The Ashward put a knife a finger’s breadth from your heart on the Fault road. Walker told me. They had my first clause in writing above all the rest, and they broke it in a gorge in the dark with blades my own sister made. When I am King, the Ashward will learn what that costs.” He folded his hands on the oak. “And when the Eclipse comes, your Sovereign will stand before his Nine with nothing on his hand, while his ring is knocked down in Durnhollow to the highest bidder. I shall not need to lift a finger to him. I need only bring you home and let you say what is true.”

Caleb’s hand had gone to the crescent. He felt the weight under the black silk, cold, the size of a crown piece, and kept his face as his mother kept hers on the dais.

“You keep your hand on your heart.”

“It aches.”

“It will mend. Everything mends in the light.” Andrew nodded at the paper under Caleb’s palm. “Learn it. You have four days.”

Caleb folded the paper in four and put it into the right-hand pocket of his coat, the one Vesna had cut for the violets. At the door he turned and looked back at his brother sitting at the long table in his grey beside a blade of white light, with the gold going dull on his face and his hands folded over two dozen burnt prints of themselves.

“Andrew. Your hands are cold.”

“It is nothing.”

“In the Citadel. At noon.”

He went out. Paul came off the wall and fell in behind him, two paces back, and they went down through the white rungs of the stair, and above them, through the door that had not quite closed, Caleb saw his brother lift his two hands off the oak and hold them up into the light and turn them over, as a man looks at a pair of gloves he has been handed in the street and is not certain are his.

Matilda came for him at the third bell of the night.

He heard boots in the corridor, two pairs and then a third, in step, and a voice outside his door that he had last heard across a gorge in the Fault. It said a good deal more now, in the clipped flat Alorian of the Guard’s orders of the day, to somebody who answered *Paladin* at every pause. Then the door opened on a tall figure in a white cloak, and shut behind her.

“Do not light anything,” said Matilda. “I can see you perfectly well. You are shining.”

She came down the dark room as straight as she had come up the passage of a sheep cave with her sword drawn, and stopped by the bed and looked at him, from his boots to his hair, as she looked at a line of soldiers. Then she bent and took his face between her two hands, which smelled of leather and steel, and held it a moment longer, and let it go.

“Mother came to the Guard’s tower at the first bell of the day. In her white. With her hair down. She has not set foot outside the palace since the spring. She stood in the door of my office and told me where you were, and went back down the stair before I had stood up.” She held out something white over her arm. “Put that on. Over that.”

It was a cloak of the Radiant Guard, white wool, hooded, with the sunburst worked at the throat in the plain yellow thread of the Guard’s seamstresses. She did not ask him to take off the black. She watched him put the white over it and draw the hood up, and then reached out and pulled the hood a finger further forward over his face, as she had once pulled his cap down over his ears on the Citadel walls when the wind came off the spires.

“The Crown Prince borrowed four of my Guard for your door. I have come to inspect them. I have found them in poor order and sent them to bed, and put two of my own in their place.” She turned for the door. “Twenty is a patrol. Two is an inspection.”

In the corridor two of the Guard stood at the door in their white with their eyes on the opposite wall, as soldiers look when they have been told what they are not seeing. Paul was on his feet beside them, with the blanket at his boots.

“Walker,” said Matilda.

“Paladin.”

“You have not seen us.”

Paul looked at Caleb. Caleb, under the hood, looked at the wall above Paul’s head, as the Guard were looking at theirs.

“No, Paladin. I’ve not seen anything for a year.”

Caleb stepped over the blanket without looking down at it, and went after his sister.

\* \* \*

She took him down by the lamp-men’s stair, which ran through the back of the palace in the thickness of the wall from the roof to the cellars, so that the men who trimmed the grid could come and go unseen, and out by a door so low that she had to stoop under it. Then they were in the city.

It was the dead of the Citadel’s night, and it was noon.

He had been put to bed behind the lead at the first bell every night of his life, as every child in the Citadel was, and never once seen what the city did with itself while it slept. It did nothing. It lay under the white sky with every shutter shut, terrace below terrace going down the hill in the glare, white walls and white steps and white roofs and not a soul on any of them, and the spires blazing over all of it like candles left burning in a nursery after the children have been carried up to bed. Shadows lay at the foot of every wall as short and black as spilled ink. On a balcony a white nightcap had been left out on a line, and hung there, and did not stir, because there was no wind.

He looked up at the spires once. Every one of them was a heart. He found that he could not look at them now without wanting to lay two fingers somewhere and count, and he looked down at his boots instead.

They went down. The terraces went past them, the palace white and then the merchants’ cream and then the stone of the lower town, and the light grew thinner as they went, as it had grown thinner every night for a thousand years with nobody to watch it. And then it failed.

He felt it fail in his nape and his palms, as one feels a fire go out in the next room, and the light that came up the street to meet them was yellow.

The Underglow lived under second-yield stones, cloudy lamps in iron cages at the corners of the lanes that gave a half-noon the colour of weak broth, and nobody had ever put shutters on the Underglow, because nobody had ever thought it would need a night. The thud and the sigh of the refineries came up through the cobbles into his boots, and the dust lay gold on the steps and the sills and the washing hung across the lanes, glittering like pollen in the yellow light, sweet on the tongue. The lamps knew him. Each cloudy stone woke a shade as he went under its cage and leaned its light down after him, as a sick child’s face turns on the pillow after a candle carried past the door, and sank back when he had gone. Matilda walked through it with her hood up and her hand on her sword hilt, and did not look to left or right.

Caleb looked.

They were in the doorways.

They sat on the tannery steps and the boarding-house steps, leaning on the doorposts with their faces lifted to nothing, a carter and two lads and a woman with a basket on her knee. There was gold at their nostrils, round the edges in a fine gilt line, as gilt lies on the edge of a page, and their eyes were open, wide, and looked at nothing at all. The lids had forgotten how to come down. The eyes had gone dry and red at the rims, and the dust had settled on the wet of them and stayed there, gold, and nobody had brushed it off.

He had walked down a lane like this at nineteen, in a hooded cloak, and a girl on a step had watched him the whole length of it without once turning her head.

Nobody watched him now.

He went past them one after another, and not one of those open eyes came round after him. They looked where they had been looking, at the yellow lamps or the wall opposite, wet and bright and blank. He saw it as he would have seen it in a patient, in the first moment at a bedside, the moment he had been taught to trust before any other. He knew these eyes. They were the windows of a house where the family has gone away, and the curtains have been left up and the furniture stands under its white sheets in the dark, and nobody will ever stand at them again to see who is coming up the road.

He stopped in the middle of the lane, in the yellow light and the gold of the dust.

He thought of a lycan guildmaster at the gala with a ship’s chain on his chest and gold at his nostrils, whose open eyes had rested on the glass chair for four hours together. He thought of someone in the Caruso harbour quarter whom the quarter would not name, who knew a great deal about what passed in a great many doorways, and of a door at the bottom of this city that his sister never locked, and somebody who had stood in the Underglow and watched it.

Somebody had been looking out of them.

“Caleb,” called Matilda, a little way ahead.

“They were windows.”

She came back to him. She did not ask what he meant. She stood beside him in the yellow light and looked where he was looking, at the carter on the tannery step with his eyes on the wall, and her hand stayed on her sword.

At the corner of the lane, above the foundry, a man had set up a barrow with a brazier glowing under a tin hood, and a smell came off it of hot mutton and pepper that cut through the sweet of the dust as a bell cuts through talk. He was a squat man in an apron, selling pies to the refinery men coming off the shift, and when he saw two hooded figures in the white of the Guard he looked at the gold that came and went at the edge of the shorter one’s hood, and touched his cap.

“Mutton, Highness? Best in Aloria.”

“I am told,” said Caleb, “that it is not difficult.”

The pie man grinned at him with a gap where two teeth had been, and wrapped a pie in a square of brown paper and put it into his hands, hot enough to hurt, and would not hear of a coin. “Not from you, Highness. My mam’d have my ears off.” Then his eyes went past Caleb to the carter on the step, and the grin went away.

“They’ve been like that eight nights,” he murmured, to Caleb and not to the Paladin, who had gone on a pace to look down at the foundry door. “Word come up with the Coast’s last courier, Highness, to them as needed it. There was a woman on the Coast used to look out of them. Out of all of them, top to bottom. Nan Corrow, they called her. A lord in purple broke her neck in her own chair, eight nights back.” He turned a pie on the brazier with his fingers, as if it might be listening. “That same night every one of ’em on every step in the Underglow shut their eyes in the one breath, top of the hill to the bottom, and put their heads down like a temple at the blessing, and slept where they sat a day and a night. Some woke up crying and couldn’t say what for. They still sit. They still take the sunshine. Only there’s nobody in ’em now but themselves.”

A handcart came down the lane on iron wheels that the dust had muffled, pushed by two refinery men with cloths tied over their mouths and noses. It stopped at a doorway a little above the foundry, where a man in a refinery apron sat with his chin on his breast. One of them bent and laid two fingers under his jaw, as Caleb would have, and waited, and shook his head at the other, and they took him up between them by the arms and the knees, easily, as men lift a thing they have lifted a great many times, and laid him on the cart. There were others on it already, under a sheet of sacking gone gold with the dust.

Caleb was beside the cart before he knew that he had moved, and the two men stood back from it with their caps in their hands.

The man was cold. He had been cold since before the bell. His eyes were open and gilded at the lids, with the yellow lamplight lying on them, and when Caleb put out his hand the gold came up in it and lit the dead face for a breath, warm, as if somebody had opened a door on it from a lit room, and went down again. He closed the eyes with his thumb. The dust came away from the lashes and lay on his skin, and glittered there.

“Treasury pays the cart, Highness,” said the elder of the two men through his cloth. “Tuppence a head. It’s all wrote down.”

On a step across the lane from the foundry a young woman sat with her back against a doorpost and her hands in her lap. She was perhaps five-and-twenty. There was gold at her nostrils, and her eyes were open on the foundry door and on the great north window above it that never went dark, as they must have been on every rest-day for years.

Caleb went across and crouched on the cobbles in front of her, in the gold of the dust, and took her wrist.

Her heart went under his fingers quick and uneven and her own, as hearts go in sleepers who wake on a cold step and do not know the street. After a long while her eyes came round, slowly, as a heavy door comes round on its hinge, and found his face under the hood.

“There used to be somebody.” Her voice was thick and puzzled and polite. “Behind.”

“I know.”

“Now there’s only me.” She looked at him a moment longer. Then her eyes went back to the window, and stayed there.

He laid her hand back in her lap, and the pie beside it in its brown paper, still warm, and stood up, and went down the three steps to his sister’s door, which was not locked, because it never was.

The foundry was as he remembered it, a high brick room full of glass, with the iron gallery going round under the roof and the great north window letting the white light down through a forest of alembics and retorts, so that it broke on the floor in colours. It smelled as it always had, of brimstone and burnt sugar and spirits. On a shelf over the long bench a row of flasks was humming, each on its own note.

Just inside the door, where nobody could come in without going round it, there was a soldier’s cot with a grey blanket made square, and a sword laid on the blanket in its scabbard.

“You wrote stand closer,” said Matilda, behind him. “I could not stand closer than her floor without standing on her.”

Cecily was at the far end of the bench, under the window, with her back to them and her sleeves rolled to the elbow and her hair coming down. She did not hear the door. Nobody had ever known her hear a door while she worked. Caleb stood in the broken colours on the floor and looked at the curve of her back over the bench, and the burns on her forearms, more of them than there had been in the spring, and the long brown lock fallen across her cheek, and found that he could not trust his voice to say her name.

So he said something else.

“I have come,” he said, rather loudly, to the window, “about my bath.”

Cecily’s tongs rang against the glass.

She turned round and saw him in the white cloak with the hood fallen back and the gold coming up in his face, and her hands went to her mouth, both of them, tongs and all. Then she came down the whole length of the foundry between the benches, past the alembics and the coils of copper and the stool with her shawl on it, through every colour on the floor, and did not stop at the edge of anything. She ran into him and put her burned arms round his neck and held on, and she smelled of sulphur and sugar, and she was shaking from head to foot.

“Tilda told me,” she said into his collar. “Tilda came home and said you were alive and that you never had your bath, and would not tell me one other thing, not one, and I have been so angry with her for a fortnight that I put salt in her tea.”

“I noticed,” said Matilda.

Cecily let him go far enough to look at him, with her hands still on his shoulders. And under the white of the Guard’s cloak, which had come open as she held him, she saw the black.

He watched her see the high collar and the closed crescent over his heart, and understand that it was not a prisoner’s coat, and not know what kind of coat it was.

“You are wearing their black.”

“Yes.”

“Andrew said you were held against your will.” Her hands tightened on his shoulders. “He said that if I made them, they would bring you home.”

Behind her on the bench, laid out in rows on black velvet as she laid out everything, there were blades.

There were twenty or more, finished and half-finished, slivers of Aether like icicles with a gold thread frozen in each, set in hilts of black cold-iron, and their warmth came across the foundry onto his face like the warmth of a hearth across a room, and something in his blood leaned towards them and he would not let it. On the nearest hilt, at the end of every long stroke of the runes, there was a little upward curl like the tail of a fern.

“Those are for the King’s Guard,” said Cecily. “For the Eclipse. In case the Coast should try to — he said, in case.” She looked at his face. “Caleb. What did mine do?”

He took her hands off his shoulders and held them, the burned forearms and the fingers that were not quite steady, as he had held a great many hands at a great many bedsides.

“They found me. All twelve, on the Fault road, in a gorge with walls a mile high. They were drawn all together, and made noon in the dark, and pinned every shadow in that gorge to the rock. There was a boy on the box of my carriage. Mirek. He is sixteen, and says *prächtig* about everything, which is Holdish for splendid. They threw him against a wheel, and his leg broke on it.” He felt her hands go cold. “He is mending. He walks with a crutch, and has been fed a great deal of pie on it.”

“And you.”

“They had orders not to touch a hair of my head. One of them had a knife at my heart before he remembered them.” He did not let go of her hands. “And one of them broke his orders, and came at me with the fifth.”

“The fifth sang,” said Cecily. It was barely a sound. “I set it again.”

“It still sang. I heard it in the dark before I saw it. He came at me with it, and the Sovereign of the Coast put himself in front of it. It went into his side under the ribs, and broke there.” He stopped.

“Tell me.”

“It did not bleed. It shone. The mouth of it was lit from inside, and through his skin I could see the rest of it lying slantwise under his ribs, burning, and fine gold lines running out from it towards his heart, as a crack runs out from a flaw in a flask that has been set too hot on the flame. They were longer every hour. It was coming undone in his blood.” He felt the gold come up in his own hands round hers, warm, and let it. “I took it out of him the next day, in a sheep cave, in the dark, thread by thread. It took most of a day and nearly the whole of me. He lived.”

Cecily stood with her hands in his, in the gold.

Along the shelf above the bench every flask that had been humming went down at once, flat, and stopped, as the talk at a long table stops when a glass goes over.

She took her hands out of his, gently, and went back up the foundry to the bench, and stood over the blades on their velvet, and put out one finger and touched a hilt where a stroke of the runes turned up into its curl.

“I put those on so that they would look glad. I put them on Tilda’s sword when I was thirteen. I have put them on everything I have ever cut.” She drew her finger back. “The armourer said a rune does not need to look glad. I said that was a great pity for the rune.”

Then she took up the edge of the black velvet and folded it over them, all of them, the finished and the half-finished, and smoothed it flat, and stood with both hands laid on it as if it might get up.

“He shall not have these.”

“Lock the door,” said Matilda.

“I have never locked it.”

“Then I shall go on sleeping across it,” said Matilda, “and you will go on stepping over me in the night to get to your still, and treading on my hand.”

Cecily looked round at her and made a sound, wet and astonished, that Caleb had not heard her make since she was eight on the edge of a gold bath, and put the back of her wrist against her mouth.

\* \* \*

Eleanor came in a quarter of a bell later by the door at the back, the one that went up to the Treasury, with a despatch case under her arm and a lamp in her hand that she did not need. Every lamp in the foundry brightened as she came. The flasks on the shelf, which had begun again to hum under their breath, wavered and found their notes a shade higher, as if they had all stood up straight.

She saw him, and stopped dead between two benches with the lamp held up, and looked at him without a word. Then she sat down quite suddenly on the end of Matilda’s cot, which nobody was permitted to sit on.

“You are thinner,” said Eleanor.

“I have been unwell.”

“So I am told by nobody. The Coast has sent me not one line since I sailed. I gather from the silence that there is something I shall dislike very much.”

“Later.”

She looked at the black under the white, and his hands, and his face, and did the sum, as she always did, and put the answer away to be audited at her leisure.

“Later,” she agreed.

She opened the despatch case on her knee. On top lay a single sheet of Citadel white with two seals on it, side by side, the sun and the lily.

“Mother sent me this at the second bell of the day, by her own chamberlain, with no note. It is the household purse’s copy of the warrants the King will seal on the night of his crowning, as soon as the sun is on his head. The first relieves the Arch-Chancellor of her office, with the Throne’s thanks. The second relieves the High Paladin of hers. The third requires the Grand Alchemist to deliver forty blades to the King’s Guard by the morning of the Eclipse.” She closed the case on it. “Mother has kept the household purse for forty years. She has never once sent a copy of anything to anybody.”

“She came to my rooms last night.”

“She has come to your rooms every night for eleven weeks. Last night, I imagine, was the first time she found anybody there to talk to.”

He did not answer that.

“So I have four days left of being the Arch-Chancellor of Aloria, and Tilda has four of being the High Paladin, and Cecily has four to make forty knives, and Andrew has four to make you learn something by heart.” She looked at the fold of paper showing at his pocket. “Is that it?”

“It is very well drawn.”

“He always did draw well.” She held out her hand, and he gave it to her, and she read it in the light of her own lamp, from the bottom up and then from the top, and gave it back. “He has given the *Lantern* a seat at his feet.”

“He told me so.”

“Their correspondent asked for it herself. She has come to the Chancellery twice since, on no business that she would name, and sat in my outer office for an hour each time in a coat that changed colour whenever she crossed her knees, with a copper coin on a ribbon that sings whenever my clerks tell her a falsehood about where I am.” Eleanor’s mouth went thin. “It has sung a good deal this week.”

Oriane de Chardon, who could not lie.

“Andrew wants every word of his crowning written down,” said Caleb, “by somebody who cannot lie.”

“Yes,” said Eleanor. “So do I.”

She said nothing else. She sat with the case on her knees and her gloved hands laid flat on it, and the ink on her second finger showed through the white kid, where it always came through.

Caleb turned to Matilda.

She stood by the door with her back to the bricks, where she could see all of them and the door as well, with her plait over her shoulder and the scratch on her cheekbone healed to a thin pale line.

“Tilda. Paul’s mother.”

Her face did not change. It never did, until it did all at once.

“At Loch Aird,” he said. “Under the steward. Since the spring. His mother, and his sisters, Ishbel and Morag.” He had heard the names once, in a garden, with the ash falling, and kept them. “Andrew sends him word every week that her cough is much improved.”

“I asked him after his mother,” said Matilda slowly, “on a ledge in the Fault. He said, *So I’m told*.”

“Yes.”

She was quiet. Along the shelf the flasks hummed, each on its own note.

“Loch Aird is four hours by the Guard’s fast yacht, with the wind. I was posted there at nineteen. The steward keeps a bad table and a worse gate.” She took her shoulders off the bricks. “Does Walker know you have told me?”

“No. And he is not to.”

She looked at him as she looked down a line, from one end to the other.

“You have forgiven him.”

“No. That is a separate matter.”

Matilda went to the cot, and Eleanor got up off it without being asked, and Matilda took up her sword, with *Again* cut on its guard and Cecily’s glad little curls at the end of every stroke, and buckled it on.

“I shall be back by the eve. Ceci. You will put something heavy against that door.” She stopped by Eleanor and nodded at the bench where Cecily stood with her hands on the black velvet. “Stand closer,” she said.

Then she put her hand on Caleb’s shoulder, over the white and the black, and looked at him a moment, and went out by the low door into the yellow light, and they heard her boots go up the steps past the young woman who sat with her eyes on the window, and away.

\* \* \*

Cecily went to the end of the bench and came back with a flask in her two hands, of heavy old glass, stoppered, with its label in an eight-year-old’s capitals, faded to brown. *FOR CALEB’S BATH. SUNLIGHT.* The liquid in it was gold as clover honey, and it took the white light from the north window and turned it over and over, pleased to be looked at.

“Tilda said you never had your bath.” She held it out. “Take it. It is the only thing I have made this year that does what I said it would.”

He took it. It was warm in his hands all through, as a loaf is warm, or a cat.

“Will you be at the crowning?” asked Cecily.

“I am expected to make a speech.”

His sister looked at him, with her hair coming down and the burns on her arms and the colours of the north window lying across her shoulders.

“Then I shall come,” said Cecily, “and put up my hair.”

\* \* \*

Paul had gone to sleep on the threshold when he came back, with the blanket over his shoulders and his head against the jamb.

Caleb stood over him in the white light of the corridor, with the Guard’s cloak over his arm and a flask of sunlight in the pocket with the violets, and looked down at the big brown hands lying open on the blanket, with the ash that no Citadel soap could shift still in the knuckles.

He could have said it. *Matilda has gone to Loch Aird.* It was six words. It would have gone into that sleeping face like noon into a shuttered room when somebody opens it, and he wanted to say it so badly that his hand had gone down towards the broad shoulder before he knew.

He took the hand back.

There was a sliver of crystal in the breast of that livery, and a twin to it in a drawer in the east tower. And he had not forgiven him. He stood in the corridor and did not know which of the two reasons he was keeping silent for, and was afraid that he did know, and stepped over the blanket without waking him, and shut the door.

From his window, before the first bell of the day, he watched a white yacht lift off the Guard’s field below the walls, fast and quiet, with no colours flying, and turn north over the shuttered city, and go up into the white until he could not tell it from the sky.

\* \* \*

The speech came up every morning after that on a silver salver with his breakfast, in the square careful hand, with the Crown Prince’s love. It was always the same speech. Andrew had always believed that a thing learned by heart was learned by writing it out again.

Caleb kept them. He turned each one over, and wrote on the back.

*The noon of the Citadel is white*, he wrote on the first. *I have looked at it a whole day to find you a colour for it, and there is none. It is the white of a page before anybody has written on it. Nobody here has looked at it either. They are all born in it, and they go to bed with their faces to the wall.*

*There is a cat on a step in the lower town that sleeps on its back in the full light with all four feet in the air*, he wrote on the second. *I think it is the only creature in the Citadel that has understood the noon. I have asked to be introduced.*

On the third, he wrote about his sisters, and crossed most of it out, because it was not his to write down. He left one line. *Cecily is going to put up her hair.*

On the nights that followed, his mother came at the third bell without a lamp, and sat at the foot of his bed in her white, upright, with her hands folded, and they did not speak of Old Meridian again. One night she asked him what Vesperian silk was like to wear, and he told her at length, and she listened as she had listened to his accounts of the Vales when he was six, asking now and then what colour a thing had been. Another night she brought a plate with a slice of cake on it, the lemon kind, and set it on the coverlet beside his hand, and said nothing.

He ate it. He did not forgive her. He found that the two things could sit at the same table, as Matilda and the Sovereign had once sat on the fleeces of a sheep cave, and neither was obliged to like it.

On the fourth night, at a hundred and ninety, the other heart quickened.

It quickened hard and fast, a long way south at the far end of the thread, as it had quickened at the sill of a garden with the frost going up white off him, and it did not steady. He lay in the dark with his hand flat on the crescent and felt it go on, high and hard, as a heart goes in a man who stands still in front of something and has made up his mind about it. He did not know what.

Then the cold came. It came up the thread from the far end, as cold comes along an iron railing from the end that stands in the snow, until it reached his breastbone and lay there, and his breath went up white off his lips into the dark of a Citadel bedchamber, where no breath had smoked in a thousand years. Eight times it came, and went, and came again, and each time the far heart went on, high and hard, and never once faltered.

The thread ached the whole length of the Marches like a plucked string that will not stop, and there was nothing he could do about any of it, behind the lead, except what he had promised.

So he counted. He counted for both of them, out loud, low, in the dark, slow and even, as a boy had once counted in a glasshouse with mud to his knees, and somewhere past two hundred and sixty the far heart came down, slowly, as a man lets out a breath he has held a long time, and fell into step with his count again.

On the fifth night, at three hundred, he took the speech that had come up that morning, the last of them, and wrote on the back of it in pencil, by the gold of his own hand, because he had promised.

*It is the eve of the eve. I have been here five days and I still cannot find a colour in it. I think it is only waiting for somebody to come and look at it who has never seen it before.*

He folded it in four and put it away with the others, beside the violets and the sunlight, and lay down in the black coat with his hand on his heart.
