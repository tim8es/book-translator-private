## Chapter 6: Chapter Five - The Heir

*Consigned at the Citadel wharves, for carriage by air to the Eclipse Marches. Twelve crates, machine parts (refinery), sealed at the consignor’s request and not to be opened in transit. Weight declared. Weight found somewhat greater. No remark entered.*

— From a bill of lading of the Lowmere house of Van Houten & Daughters

*The Citadel. Twenty-seven years ago.*

Andrew was twelve years old, and he had spent three days practising how to kneel.

He had knelt before thrones since he could stand, so the kneeling itself was nothing. The Harp Vales had taught him the rest. For two years among the temple scholars of the western valleys he had learned law and lineage and the long, cold arithmetic of rule, and learned to wake before the bells and to speak only when the answer was certain. He had slept in a stone cell with the window open on the snow, where the novices’ harps came up from the cloister at dusk as thin and clear as meltwater, and eaten in silence at the end of a long refectory table where no one ever sat within three places of him, because he was the heir and the scholars knew what was owed. He had left the Citadel a boy who fidgeted in his collar. He meant to come back as a son his father could look at and be sure of.

So he had practised on the road home. In the carriage, with the blinds drawn against the dust and the noon coming in round their edges in thin gold seams, and his travelling grey buttoned to the throat, he had knelt on the boards between the seats until his knee ached through the wool. He had rehearsed how far to lower his head and how long to hold it there, and which words to say, in which order and in what voice, and he had said them over to the empty seat across from him until his own voice sounded like a stranger’s, and then longer still, until it sounded like a king’s.

The Throne Hall was built to make kneeling feel like a privilege. Light fell through its high glass in columns so bright they seemed solid, and the white marble floor had been polished until it gave back the ceiling, so that a man walking the length of it walked upon a second hall of light, upside down beneath his feet. The air smelled of beeswax and white lilies and warm stone. Two hundred courtiers lined its length in silks the colours of morning. At the far end, on the dais beneath the golden sunburst, sat his father and mother.

Andrew walked the length of the hall without looking at anyone. He had been taught that the heir walks as if the floor were laid for him, and he did. He passed through column after column of light, and each one lay warm across his shoulders for a step and then let him go. At the foot of the dais he went down on one knee. The marble was cold through the grey wool, the only cold thing in all that brightness. He set his right hand over his heart, felt it knocking there against his palm, and bowed his head to the depth he had practised.

“Majesties. Your son returns from the Vales, and asks leave to serve.”

His voice held. He was proud of that.

“Rise, Andrew.” King George’s voice was warm, though his face kept its public stillness. “The Vales have sent us back a young man.”

Andrew stayed down. There was a second form, and he meant to finish it. “I am grateful for the —”

The west doors opened.

He heard the court turn before he saw why. There was a ripple of silk, and a hush with a smile in it, and the dust in the columns of light went whirling up in the draught from the doors like midges over a summer pond, and then his sisters came up the hall together, because someone had decided that the heir’s homecoming should be a family occasion and no one had asked him.

Matilda came first. She was nine and already walked like a soldier, chin up and shoulders square, and in her arms, held against her hip with her whole attention, was a baby.

Eleanor followed, seven and immaculate, hands folded, surveying the court the way she surveyed a sum she had already solved. And behind Eleanor, a long way behind, came Cecily, who was four and had not yet learned to go anywhere at a pace set by other people. She toddled up the marble in slippers too big for her, slapping softly at every step, and stopped once to squat down and pat a patch of light on the floor, as one pats a hearthstone to see whether it is still warm.

Andrew knew about the baby. A year ago a letter had come in his mother’s hand. *A son. Caleb.* It had been read aloud in the refectory at the Vales, and the scholars had bowed to him, because a second son made the succession secure, and Andrew had understood that this was why it mattered. Afterwards he had folded the letter into his book of statutes, where it served very well to mark his place.

The baby was round and fair-skinned and wide awake, with a head of soft brown hair that curled at the ends, and the Solar shimmer lay on his skin as faintly as the gold dust on a moth’s wing. He was looking past the King and the Queen and the two hundred courtiers, straight at Andrew, and he had been looking since the doors opened.

Matilda and Eleanor stopped three paces from him, the proper distance from the heir. Matilda began her curtsey. The baby chose that moment to object. He twisted in her arms, shoved at her collar with both fists, and let out a sound of such concentrated indignation that someone in the front rank laughed aloud before they could stop themselves.

“Caleb,” Matilda said through her teeth. “Be still.”

Caleb declined. He arched his back and kicked, and Matilda, who at nine could already hold a practice sword level for a quarter of an hour, could not hold him. She crouched and set him down on the marble before he could fall onto it, and straightened with her face scarlet.

The hall waited to see what the baby would do.

He crawled to Andrew.

He went with great purpose and no skill whatsoever, one knee skidding on the polished stone, one palm slapping down ahead of it, his gown rucked up around his middle. Every slap of his hand rang in the hush. He crawled into a column of light and came out of it gold to the shoulders, and crawled into the shadow beyond and dimmed, and into the next column and blazed again, with his eyes on Andrew the whole way. It took him some time. Nobody moved to stop him. Later, Andrew would understand that nobody had known whether they were allowed to.

Andrew stayed where he was, on one knee with his hand over his heart, because no one had released him from the second form. Under his palm his heart had begun to go hard and fast. Two years of lessons turned their pages at the back of his mind one after another, and none of them applied.

Caleb arrived at his knee. He grabbed it with both hands, and Andrew felt them through the wool, hot and damp and astonishingly strong. He hauled himself upright, wobbled, and peered into Andrew’s face from a hand’s breadth away. His eyes were brown. Andrew had expected blue, their father’s eyes and his own, and instead the child had their mother’s eyes, dark and warm and unafraid.

Then Caleb reached up and patted his hair.

He patted with the flat, ungentle delight of someone who had recently discovered hair and approved of it. The heir’s circlet slid sideways over Andrew’s ear, and Caleb patted that too. Then he moved on to Andrew’s face with both hands, cheeks and nose and chin, making sure that everything was present and warm and his.

In two years at the Vales Andrew had been touched by the tailor who measured him for his travelling grey, apologising at every pin, and by the physician who laid two cool fingers on his wrist at each new year and wrote down what he found. When he left the Citadel his mother had kissed his brow before the court, as lightly as a seal is pressed into wax. He could not remember anyone touching him like this at all. All his life he had been bowed to and admired from three paces off. Nobody had ever crossed the floor to him, or wanted him badly enough to crawl.

Heat rose behind his eyes before he could stop it, the blood waking, and for one instant the light of the hall flared white across his sight. The marble threw the glare back up at him. A murmur ran through the front ranks. The heir had lost his composure before the entire court. His masters at the Vales would have been appalled.

Caleb laughed. He had seen the white flare in his brother’s eyes and decided it was a game. He flung both arms around Andrew’s neck, pressed his face against the side of Andrew’s head, and held on with the perfect confidence of a creature who had never once been dropped.

He smelled of milk and sunlight and the lavender water the nursery maids used. His breath came hot and damp against Andrew’s ear, and one fist had closed in the hair at the nape of Andrew’s neck and would not let go. He was so warm.

Slowly, because he did not know how, Andrew took his hand from his heart and laid it on the baby’s back. Then the other. Under his palms he could feel the narrow ribs, and the breath going in and out of them, and a heartbeat quick as a bird’s, knocking against his own through two layers of cloth. The child fitted there. He waited to be told what to do, and no one told him, and he knew anyway.

He stood up with his brother in his arms.

It ruined the second form. He let it. On the dais his mother had her hand pressed to her mouth, and his father, who never smiled in the Throne Hall, was smiling, and all down the hall the columns of light had brightened a shade, as they did on feast days, though no one afterwards could say who had done it.

“Well,” said the King. “It seems he has decided.”

Caleb had found the circlet again and was trying to take it off, so Andrew bent his head and let him. The gold came away in two plump fists and was examined, and approved, and solemnly offered back, and dropped.

It rang on the marble, a high clear note that went up into the vaulting and hung there a while before it faded, and then it lay where it had fallen, gold on white in a column of sun, and nobody picked it up.

Only then did Andrew notice Cecily.

She had arrived at last and stood at the edge of the column of light, a pace behind her sisters, staring up at him. He was the brother she could remember seeing perhaps three times in her life, tall and strange in his travelling grey, and he was holding the baby, and she was looking at the baby in his arms with her whole face. Her hand lifted a little, towards his sleeve. Then it went back to her mouth, and she stayed where she was.

Andrew kept his arms around Caleb.

He held his brother against his shoulder in the white light of the Throne Hall, with the warm weight of him settling heavier as the baby began, of all things, to fall asleep, and the glow of him soaking into the grey wool like noon into a garden wall, and made a promise, silently, as only a boy of twelve can make one and mean it with his whole heart.

He would never let anything hurt him.

*The Citadel. The night of the gallery.*

Night in the Citadel was a matter of shutters.

The great crystals on the spires burned with noon at every hour, all year round, and so the city slept by the bells and by heavy curtains, and the swifts under the palace eaves, who had never once seen a dusk, went to sleep whenever they happened to feel like it, and the shutters of the palace were lined with lead so that the royal family might have darkness when it wished for it. Andrew seldom wished for it.

He kept his study in the east tower with the shutters open a hand’s width, so that a single blade of white light lay across the floor, and he sat at the long table beside it with a lamp he did not need and the Treasury’s quarterly returns, which he read the way other people read novels. The room smelled of ink and warm dust and the lamp’s faint oil. Beyond the shutters the city lay hushed under its unending noon, a whole people asleep with their faces turned from the light.

The drawer began to glow a little after the second bell of the night.

It came first as a thin white line along the seam, and then as a pale point at the keyhole, like an eye opening. He finished the column he was reading before he unlocked it, since the news would keep and his dignity might not. Then he took the key from the plain steel chain at his throat and lifted out the crystal, a sliver of clear quartz no longer than his finger, and held it in his palm while it warmed.

It was the twin of a stone the Radiant Guard carried in the field. Its brother had gone to the Obsidian Coast in the lining of a soldier’s boot.

For a month it had spoken every evening in the same low and careful voice. Tonight the care had gone out of it. The steadiness remained, because Paul Walker had been trained by Matilda, and nothing Matilda trained ever lost its seat entirely. But under it there was a scrape in the breath between sentences, and the crystal grew hot in his palm as it spoke, and the voice came out of it thin and close, so near his ear that he could hear the man swallow.

A fight in the yard, told flatly and without emotion, like an injured soldier reporting for duty. The prince walking into a circle of lycans and ordering them to stand back, and the lycans heeding the order. The prince on his knees in the blood, and the healing.

Andrew closed his eyes at that. He knew what Caleb’s healing looked like. He had watched him at fourteen close a stable boy’s split skull in the Harp Vales, with the whole of the temple looking on and the light pouring off him like water off a weir, and had thought, with a pride so fierce it hurt, that there was nothing in the world like his brother.

Then the gallery, where the light would not stop, and the Sovereign.

*He fed on His Highness, Highness.* Paul’s voice caught on the title and recovered. *From the throat. I saw it. His Highness lives.*

The crystal dimmed and went dark in his hand.

Andrew sat for some time with it closed in his fist, while the quartz cooled slowly against his palm until it was only a stone again, and the blade of light from the shutters crept a finger’s width along the boards, and the lamp burned on beside him, steady and useless, as lamps in the Citadel always were. Somewhere below in the courtyard a sentry’s boots rang on the stones and stopped, and rang again.

He had always known what vampires were. Every child in Aloria was raised on the nursery tales of the cold folk under the ash, who could not cross a sunlit sill and who left frost on the cradle rail of any baby that would not go to sleep. But each evening he had listened to reports of books and tea and harpsichords, and of a cook who baked scones, and had told himself that a hostage was property, and the Coast kept its property with the same cold care it kept its ledgers, and no Conti would risk the treaty for one meal.

Nine days ago the voice had told him about the garden. Twelve deep-trench crystals, it had said, hanging in a spiral in the belly of the mountain, with white roses under them, and lemon trees. The stones his refineries were starving for, the stones the Treasury was screaming about in the returns under his hand, hung in a vampire’s cellar so that his brother could lie under them. Andrew had sat where he was sitting now with his palm pressed flat to the wood and his head bowed over it like a man at prayer. Caleb would not die of the Blight. Someone had seen to that. That night he had slept until the bells.

Now he understood what the garden was for. The Sovereign of Vesperia had kept his brother fed and in the light for the same reason a farmer keeps the best of his flock in the warm field. And tonight he had come to collect.

The paper under his hand had begun to brown.

Andrew looked down. Where his fingers lay on the quarterly returns, the edges of the page were curling and darkening, and a thin thread of smoke rose from the ink. The columns of figures were going out one by one under his fingertips, sums he had checked twice that evening, blackening and lifting away. The smell reached him last, hot and bitter, like toast left too long at a nursery fire.

He had not felt his eyes change. He felt them now, the heat behind them and the white glare they threw across the table and up the panelled wall, and he took his hand away and counted his breaths as the Vales had taught him, until the light went out of them and the smoke thinned and stopped.

His hand had gone to his throat. His fingers had passed over the steel chain and were feeling beside it for a finer one that was gone.

He made them stop.

He had worn Caleb’s signet for five weeks in the spring, on a fine gold chain under his collar, because it would not go over the knuckle of any finger he had. He had grown used to its weight against his breastbone, warm from his own skin, knocking softly there when he rode.

He had asked for it at supper in Caleb’s rooms, with the shutters thrown wide as Caleb always had them and the noon lying gold across the plates, with some idle story about a wine merchant in the Marches who gave better terms to a prince, and Caleb had pulled it off and dropped it into his palm, laughing, because Caleb had never in his life refused him anything. The Radiant Guard wrote down every prince who passed the border, and the heir would have been remarked on. With Caleb’s signet he went through as Prince Caleb, who went everywhere, and bought too much wine, and was suspected of nothing.

And then there had been the woman in the cellars.

The dark between the casks, and the smell of wine and old oak and wet stone, cool after the dust of the Marches road. A lamp held up in a slender white hand, its flame standing straight and unwavering in the still cellar air, and a face above it he had not expected, smooth and pale as any vampire’s, and old-eyed, and calm. Behind her stood a low door of black iron, and behind that lay the relic he had crossed the Marches for, the greatest dragon heart ever found, a crystal the Sanctuary had kept for a thousand years and never once shown to an elf. She heard what he wanted and refused him courteously, as one refuses a guest who has asked at dinner for something unsuitable.

Then the fire in the vines.

The light went out of him and up through the earth, and the old vines took it with a roar, and the glare came pouring down the cellar stair, white as the noon he had brought with him. Casks split in the heat with a sound like ribs breaking. The wine ran out across the flags and hissed where it met the light, and the cellar filled with a hot red smoke that smelled of burning fruit.

She put herself between him and the iron door and stayed there, though the lamp had fallen from her hand and the smoke was in her hair, and looked at him the whole while with the same calm, old eyes, as a governess looks at a child who has broken a vase in temper and will be sorry for it later. As she fell her hand caught at the chain at his throat and closed on it, a finger’s breadth from his skin, with a strength he would not have believed in someone so slight and so nearly dead. Her fingers never touched him. They closed on the gold and held, and when he wrenched himself away the chain broke and the ring stayed in her fist.

He searched for it among the dead until the Vesperian riders came, going among them in the smoke, stooping over one and then another to look at their hands, until his eyes streamed and his coat was black with soot. He laid his palm on the iron door and pushed, and pushed again, and it lay cold and shut against him. He left with neither. Nine days later the Coast’s courier stood in the Throne Hall, on the white marble his brother had once crawled across, with Caleb’s signet on a black cushion.

He went to his father that night. The King stood at the window of his study with his back to the room, a dark shape cut out of the glare, and heard him out without turning.

“Caleb was never at the vineyard, Majesty. I was. I wore his signet across the border, and I lost it in the cellars, and the woman who took it from me is dead.”

It was as much of the truth as a king needed. Andrew left out the heart, and the fire.

For a long time the King said nothing. Then he said, “The Crown Prince cannot be given to the Coast.”

“No, Majesty.”

“The ring in the dead woman’s hand has already named a hostage.”

Andrew stood with his hands clasped behind his back and felt his nails go into his palms. The words were ready behind his teeth, and they were simple, and they would have cost him everything. *Then send me.*

He heard himself speak instead, from what seemed a long way off, in the clear and certain voice he had once practised for three days on the road home. “Hostages under the Accord are kept, Majesty, not harmed. It need only be for a season.”

His father did not turn round. The noon stood white in the window behind him and laid his shadow the length of the study, to Andrew’s feet.

Afterwards Andrew walked back through the palace, through long white galleries where the noon stood in every window like a guard who would not be relieved, past the door of his brother’s rooms, and kept his eyes ahead. He would have Caleb home before the Blight could kill him. He had given himself six weeks.

It had been four, and he was no nearer.

He put the crystal back in the drawer and locked it, and put the key back on its chain. Then he sat at the table, in the blade of white light, with his hands folded on the scorched page so that they would be still, and did not sleep, while the bells went on ringing the hours of a night the sky above the Citadel had never once seen fit to provide.

The Grand Alchemist kept her laboratory at the bottom of the Citadel, where the refineries began, and where the white noon of the spires, having poured itself down so many terraces, at last ran thin and failed.

Andrew went down to it on foot, three days later, with two of his own guard at a distance and his hood up, because he liked to see the lower city with his own eyes and did not like the lower city to see him. The Underglow was the part of the Citadel that did not appear in the portraits. The spire-crystals were too high to light it, so it lived under second-yield lamps, cloudy stones that gave a yellowish, sickly half-noon, and the refinery chimneys breathed a fine glittering dust over everything that settled on the cobbles and the washing lines and the faces of the children. It got into the mouth and lay sweet and gritty on the tongue.

The air down here was close and warm and tasted of hot metal, and from every side came the slow, enormous breathing of the refineries, a thud and a sigh, a thud and a sigh, that a man stopped hearing after an hour and felt in his teeth for a week. The people who lived there called it the Glow. The people who lived higher up did not call it anything, because they never came down.

At the corner of a street of tanneries, where the reek of lime and wet hides lay over everything like a blanket, a boy of perhaps fifteen was sitting in a doorway with his head tipped back against the frame and his eyes wide open, staring up at nothing, smiling. There was a faint gold shimmer at the edges of his nostrils. His skin had a lustre that no child of the Underglow came by naturally, the lustre of someone who had borrowed an hour of noon and would pay for it tonight.

In the Underglow they called it sunshine. The physicians called it Aether-Dust. Andrew called it revenue, and kept a column for it in a Durnhollow ledger that grew a little every week. He looked at the boy without breaking stride, and judged the quality of the shimmer, and thought that the new refining was better than the old.

Cecily’s laboratory had been a foundry once. She had kept the high brick walls and the iron gallery that ran round them, and filled everything in between with glass. There were alembics taller than a man and retorts no bigger than a thimble, coils of copper pipe that climbed the walls like ivy, and long benches crowded with jars and crucibles and loose papers weighted down with whatever had come to hand.

Somewhere a still dripped, as steady as an old priestess at her beads. The place smelled of sulphur and scorched sugar and the cold sharp breath of spirits, and the light from the great north window came down through a forest of glass and broke into colours on the floor. On a shelf above one bench a row of little flasks had been left to cool, and every one of them was humming faintly on a different note.

His sister was at the far end, bent over the bench beneath the window, with a patch of that broken colour lying across her back like a shawl someone had thrown over her while she worked.

He stood a moment and watched her. She did not hear him come in. She never heard anything when she was working. He had been told that she once worked straight through the evacuation of the entire eastern refinery without lifting her head, and emerged afterwards to ask why it had gone so quiet.

She had rolled her sleeves to the elbow, and there were burns on both forearms, old and new, a dozen neat shiny patches in the fine gold of her skin. Her hair had been put up at some point in the past day or so and had been giving up the attempt ever since. A long brown lock of it had fallen across her cheek, and she had not noticed, or had noticed and could not spare the hand to move it. At her elbow stood a tray with a cup of tea gone cold and skinned over and a heel of bread with one bite out of it, and beside the bench a stool, pushed back, with her shawl dropped across it where she had last sat down.

She had not slept. He could see it in the set of her shoulders, which had climbed up towards her ears and stayed there, and in the way she held the tongs, with the exaggerated precision of someone who knows her hands have stopped being quite reliable. Once, while he watched, the tongs trembled, and she stopped and breathed, and went on.

“Cecily,” he said.

She started, and the tongs rang against the glass, and she set them down slowly and turned round. When she saw who it was, hope went across her face and was put away again. “Your Highness.”

“Andrew will do, down here, I think. There is no one to be scandalised.” He came down the length of the bench, and stopped a pace short of her, which was where one stopped. “You look dreadful.”

“Thank you.” She pushed the lock of hair out of her face with the back of her wrist. “I have been busy.”

“So I see.”

On the bench behind her, laid out in a row on black velvet like knives at a surgeon’s elbow, were the blades.

There were four of them finished, and the parts of a fifth. The longest was no longer than his forearm. Each was a single sliver of refined Aether, clear as ice and faintly gold at the heart, set into a hilt of dull black cold-iron that had been cut all over with runes. Even at rest on the velvet they gave off a light that made him want to narrow his eyes, and a warmth he could feel on his face from where he stood, like a hearth felt across a room. The blood in him knew them. It stirred and leaned towards them, like a dog that hears its name.

He had seen the drawings, but he had never seen one finished, and he stood looking at them for longer than he meant to, with their warmth on his face and the blood in him still leaning.

“The iron is dwarven,” Cecily said, beside him. Her voice had lost the careful thinness it had when she talked to him. “Cold-iron, from the Jötun North. It is the only thing that will hold the runes without carrying the light into your hand. The Aether is deep-trench, or what little of it I could get, and refined eleven times. The runes hold the refining. If they did not, the whole blade would try to become noon at once, and there would be nothing left of it, or of whoever was holding it.” She hesitated. “It is still not stable. Not entirely. The fifth one sang when I set it, and I had to start again.”

“But it works.”

“It works.” She picked up the shortest of the finished blades by the hilt and held it up to the north window, and the light went through it and came out the other side changed, brighter and harder and whiter, until the whole bench seemed to be standing in a column of summer. The flasks on their shelf went up half a note together, and the glass alembics rang faintly in sympathy, and Andrew felt the heat of it on his eyelids. “It cuts the way dawnbending cuts, Andrew. It burns what dawnbending burns. Anyone could use it. You would not need a drop of the blood.”

He knew what dawnbending burned. So did she. The light of the Solar blood, turned outward as a weapon, was death to vampires, and she had made it into something any hand could hold. He watched her decide, as she had been deciding every day for weeks, not to say it aloud.

She put the blade down, and the column of summer went out of the air above the bench as if a door had shut on it, and she closed her shaking hand into a fist against her skirt until it stopped.

“You said,” she began, and stopped, and began again. “You said that this would bring him home.”

“I did.”

“I have done it. Nearly. I need more of the deep-trench stone, and there is none to be had, because the Coast is withholding it. Eleanor says the Treasury has not had a full shipment in three months.” She looked up at him. “Where is he, Andrew?”

“Somewhere he can be reached. With these.”

“That is not an answer.”

“No,” Andrew agreed. “It is not.”

She looked away first, as she always had, and the broken colours from the window slid off her shoulders as she turned, and she folded her burned arms across herself and held on to her own elbows. “Father says he is on pilgrimage.”

“Father says so, yes.”

“He has never been on a pilgrimage in his life. He went to the temple at Glastonmere once, when he was sixteen, and asked the high priestess if the holy spring could be made a little warmer.” Her mouth moved, not quite a smile. “Matilda has put every banner she can command into the Marches. Eleanor has frozen the Coast’s money in the counting-houses. Neither of them will tell me why, and neither of them will tell Father anything, and I am down here making knives. Where is he?”

She was thirty-one years old and the most gifted alchemist the Dominion had ever produced, and she was asking him the way she had asked him for sweets when she was small, as if she half expected to be refused.

“I know that he is alive. I know that he is being kept against his will. I know that Father has forbidden anyone to speak of it, because if it were known, the court would demand that he act, and he will not. And I know that if Matilda learned what I know, she would ride across the border tomorrow with every soldier she has, and the people who hold him would kill him before she was halfway to the gate.”

Cecily had gone white to the lips, and on the shelf behind her the humming flasks wavered and fell flat, every one, like a nursery that has heard voices raised downstairs.

“So I am not going to tell you where he is,” Andrew said gently, “because you would tell Matilda, as you should, and she would do what she must, and I would lose him. I am going to bring him home myself. Quietly, and soon, and in a way no one can be blamed for. And for that I need these.”

“They are forbidden.” It was barely a whisper. “Every treaty since the Accord —”

“The Accord was written by people who had never had a brother taken.”

She had no answer to that. He had known she would have none.

He let the silence sit until the still had dripped five times and the flasks on their shelf had settled back to their own notes. Then he let his eyes wander to the end of the bench, where among the alembics stood a stoppered flask of heavy old glass with a label in a round, careful, childish hand. *FOR CALEB’S BATH. SUNLIGHT.* The liquid inside was the colour of honey and caught the light from the window like a live thing. He had seen it the moment he came in, and kept it in reserve, as one keeps the last coin in a purse.

“You still make it.”

Cecily’s face softened. She reached out without seeming to know she did it and touched the label with one fingertip, along the crooked line of the letters, where the ink had faded to brown. “He asked for it at his birthday. In the spring. He said he had not had a proper bath since he was nine, and would I make him some, for old times’ sake, and he would swim in sunlight and be young again.” She pressed the back of her hand hard against her mouth. “He is twenty-eight, Andrew.”

“I know.”

He remembered the bath. He had been sixteen and newly admitted to their father’s council, and he had come to say goodnight and found the nursery bathroom glowing gold from wall to wall, the steam itself lit up and turning in the air like pollen, the tiles running with light. And Caleb, five years old and naked as an egg, standing up in the middle of it with his arms spread wide and the gold streaming off his elbows, shrieking with joy. *Andrew! Andrew, look, I am swimming in the sun!* Cecily, eight, sat on the edge of the bath with her knees drawn up and her face alight, watching him the way she had watched him every day of her life, because he was the only audience she had ever needed. Andrew had lifted him out and wrapped him in a towel and carried him to bed dripping, with the wet warm weight of him squirming against his chest and the high voice going on and on about the sun, and the gold had come off on his coat and never washed out.

He let her see him remember it. It was the only moment in the conversation when he did not have to choose what his face was doing.

“Twelve,” he said, when he trusted his voice. “I need twelve, Cecily. Within the month. Can you do it?”

She looked at the flask, and at the blades on their velvet, and lastly at him, and he watched the hope take hold of her face the way the light took hold of the glass. Her shoulders came down from her ears. She stood up straighter than she had since he came in, and for an instant she was four years old again at the edge of a column of light, with her hand lifting towards his sleeve. “I will find the stone,” she promised. “Somewhere. I will find it.”

“Thank you. And, Cecily. Not a word of this to anyone. Not to Matilda. Not to Eleanor. They are doing what they can, in their own ways, and they are doing it very loudly, and every time they make a noise the people who hold him grow more careful. We are the only two who can be quiet.”

“Yes. Yes. I understand.”

She had already turned back to the bench and was reaching for the tongs, and her hands, he saw, were steady.

He left her bent over the bench again under the great north window with her hair coming down, setting the fifth blade. Behind him, on its shelf, the row of flasks went on humming to themselves, each on its own note, like a choir that had never been taught to sing together.

\* \* \*

The *Lantern of Lutèce* reached the Citadel on the eleventh day after his visit to the Underglow, in the usual way, which was to say in the pockets of every courier, laundress and junior secretary who had passed through the Court of Lys that week, and by noon there was not a room in the palace without a copy in it, the kitchens included, where the pastry cooks had pinned the engraving of the Sovereign above the bread ovens and were taking turns to sigh at it.

His mother had banned it twice. Andrew found her with one in her lap.

She was in her morning room, alone, with the shutters closed and a single lamp, which was how he knew before he saw the paper that something was wrong. Queen Philippa loved the light, and sat when she could in the brightest window in the palace with her face tipped up to the spire light like a flower. She sat in the dark now, in a white gown with the broadsheet spread across her knees, and went on looking at it when he came in. The room was close and still. The lilies in the vase on the mantel had dropped their pollen in a drift the colour of rust across the marble, and no one had been let in to sweep it.

“Mother.”

“They have drawn him very badly,” she said. “They always do. His jaw is far better than that.”

He crossed the room and took the paper from her, gently, and she let him. Her fingers were cold where they brushed his.

His brother’s face smiled up at him from last season’s portrait, beside an engraving of a very dark and handsome vampire drawn by someone who had never set eyes on him, and between the two of them, in crimson ink an inch high, ran the words *THE PILGRIM PRINCE: BETROTHED?*

He read it standing. It did not take long. The fae wrote well, and briefly, and could not lie, and had made a high art of arranging true things in the most damaging possible order. A Caruso heiress refused before the Council. The Sovereign already promised. *To him.* The Alorian prince rendered, the correspondent wrote, *speechless for perhaps the first time in his well-documented life.* And at the bottom, set out with the relish the fae reserved for other people’s customs, the matter of the ring.

He had known about the ring since the first night. *The Sovereign put a ring on His Highness’s hand, Highness. Iron. It will not come off.* Andrew had taken it for what Paul had taken it for, and what Caleb had surely taken it for, a collar. Since the gallery Paul’s reports had gone short and plain, the hours and the meals and the visitors and little else, and Paul had not been in the great hall. For six weeks Andrew had turned that ring over in his mind every evening like a stone kept in a pocket, black iron on his brother’s warm hand, and all that while every lord and clerk and lycan on the Obsidian Coast had been looking at the same hand and seeing a promise.

The edge of the paper was smoking.

“Andrew,” said his mother.

He looked down. His thumb had gone through the crimson ink. The whole corner of the broadsheet was brown and curling, and *BETROTHED?* was blackening letter by letter under his hand, and the white glare off his eyes lay across his mother’s folded hands and the white silk of her gown like frost. A flake of ash broke from the corner and drifted down onto her knee, and she left it there.

He put the paper down on the table beside her. He did it slowly, and the white glare went out of his eyes and left the room darker than it had been before he came in.

“Forgive me. I did not sleep.”

His mother looked up at him then. Caleb had her eyes. Since the spring Andrew had not been able to look into her face without seeing his brother look back at him out of it.

“He kissed him,” the Queen whispered. “Before all those people. And my son could not think of a single thing to say.” Her voice cracked on it, a little, and she mended the crack at once, as she had been trained to do from the cradle. The royal smile came up onto her face out of long habit, there in the dark where no one but her son could see it, like a lamp left burning in the window of a house where everyone has gone to bed. “Caleb has always had something to say.”

“I know.”

“I chose the wine myself,” she confessed. “Did you know that? I told George I would not have him dragged. I would not have him wake to guards at the door and struggle, and be carried out in front of the servants. So I sent my own chamberlain to pour it, and the chamberlain told me afterwards that Caleb smiled at him over the glass.” Her hands tightened on each other in her lap until the rings bit. “I thought that was a mercy.”

Andrew went down on one knee beside her chair.

He had not meant to. Then he was there on the cold boards with his head below hers, as he had knelt at the foot of the dais at twelve with a speech learned by heart. His hand lifted towards her folded ones, and stopped in the air between them. *It was I who wore the ring. It was I who was at the vineyard.* He had said it once already, in his father’s study, and been told what to do with it.

He put his hand back on his knee. Then he rose, and bent and kissed her forehead, and left her in the dark with the ruined paper, and the lilies on the mantel letting fall their rust onto the marble, grain by grain.

In his own rooms a letter was waiting on the salver, delivered by the dwarven courier who came up to the palace every tenth day with the Treasury’s bills of exchange. It was written on thick grey paper that smelled faintly of forge smoke and cold stone, in a hand like print and sealed with the seven-bar mark of the Seventh Chamber of the vault of Dornhöhle, which the court called Durnhollow and where Andrew kept a great deal more money than the court knew about. Beneath it some tactful secretary had left a clean copy of the *Lantern*. He folded that into his coat without reading it again.

*Master Ulrich Brandt, of the Seventh Chamber, presents his compliments to His Royal Highness the Crown Prince of Aloria, and begs to inform him that a client of the Chamber, being at present the holder of certain paper of the Obsidian Coast, has asked that a conversation be arranged with a principal of the Solar crown upon matters of mutual interest. The Chamber offers its station at the Kessel Pass for the purpose, on any night of His Highness’s choosing, under the full guarantee of the Holds. The Chamber takes no position in the matter. The Chamber never does.*

Andrew read it twice. Then he rang for his secretary.

“Have a yacht readied for the Marches. If anyone asks, the Crown Prince has gone to inspect the Radiant Guard’s positions along the border.”

The Radiant Guard was Matilda’s business, and no one would think it strange that the heir took an interest in it.

He burned the letter himself, in the grate, with a match. He watched the grey paper catch at one corner and the flame climb it, slow and ordinary and yellow, the fire any man might make. His hands had been quite steady for some hours now.

\* \* \*

The station at the Kessel Pass was cut into the side of a mountain above the snowline, where the dwarven vault couriers changed their mules on the long road from the Holds to the Coast.

From the air it was nothing. A ledge of dark rock in a wilderness of dark rock, a single square door, a stone chimney with a thin thread of smoke going up from it into the mist. The yacht came down through cloud so cold and wet that it beaded on the rails and froze there, and set down on the landing shelf with a scrape of runners on old ice. The air outside was thin and bit at the back of the throat. Somewhere in a stable cut into the rock a mule shifted and blew, and the smell of it came out on the wind, warm dung and wet hide and hay, the only warm thing on the mountain.

Only when Andrew had walked through that door, between two dwarven guards who looked at his face and then at their captain’s ledger and let him pass without a word, did he understand how far back into the mountain it went, and that it was a vault.

The corridors were plated from floor to vaulted ceiling in black cold-iron, riveted and runed and gleaming dully in the light of oil lamps, and the moment Andrew stepped onto the iron floor he felt the blood go quiet in him, as a hive goes quiet under smoke. He had carried the light in him since before he could walk. It had woken when he woke and burned when he burned, a heat at the back of his eyes and a hum along the bones of his hands, so constant that he had long ago stopped hearing it. Now it lay down in him and did not get up.

He walked on through the iron with his footsteps ringing, deafened in some sense he had no word for. A dozen paces later, and much more unsettling, he felt what was almost rest. Cold-iron held a rune and let nothing else pass through it, and the runes on these walls had been cut by the best masters of the Holds for a single purpose. Here nothing he touched would scorch. No elf would bend a ray of light inside these walls tonight, and no vampire would call a shadow. That was the guarantee of the Holds, and in five hundred years no one had found a way round it.

He was shown down the longest corridor of all, where the lamps hung so far apart that he walked from one island of yellow light to the next across a sea of black iron, to a room at its end.

It was a counting room, as plain as a cell, with a long table of dark oak and a ledger on it, and ink, and three pens laid side by side with their nibs aligned, and iron walls all round, and an iron lamp hanging from an iron chain and throwing a circle of light that stopped well short of the corners. The room smelled of lamp oil and cold metal and, faintly, of the linseed oil someone had rubbed into the oak. At the head of the table a dwarf of middle years with a beard braided into three flat plaits and a pair of spectacles on a ribbon rose and bowed as low as the Seventh Chamber’s tariff of courtesies permitted for a crown prince, and not a hair lower.

“Your Highness is punctual,” said Master Brandt. “The Chamber is grateful. The other party is also punctual. The Chamber is grateful for that too.”

The other party sat along the far side of the table, at the edge of the lamplight, where the cold-iron walls gave back no gleam at all.

Andrew had imagined sitting down at a table with a vampire often, lately, and in some detail. The reality was quieter than any of it, and a good deal more polite.

Don Salvatore Caruso looked no older than a man of thirty. His face was smooth and fine-boned and pale as a church candle, and his hair was black and thick and swept back from a high brow, and there was not a line on him anywhere. He wore garnet silk, and a great many rings, and an expression of mild and courteous interest, the look of a connoisseur before a painting he has been told is very fine and has not yet made up his mind about. Only the eyes gave him away. They were dark and still, and they had seen most things happen before and been patient through all of them. The cold came off him across the table in a slow draught, like the air from an opened cellar, and Andrew’s skin rose under his sleeves to meet it.

Beside him sat a young woman in black, with a thorn on a chain at her throat, and a fainter cold coming off her than off her grandfather, like the breath of a well rather than a cellar.

“Your Highness,” said Don Salvatore. His voice was the dry, unhurried voice of someone very old, coming out of a face that was not. “How good of you to come so far. I trust the Marches were kind to you.”

“The Marches were very kind,” Andrew answered, and sat. “They were empty. That is the kindest thing about them.”

Don Salvatore inclined his head. His long white hands lay at rest on the oak, ringed to the knuckle, and Andrew’s lay folded on his side of it, and neither pair moved. “You will forgive me if I do not offer you my hand. It is almost the only point on which our courts have ever agreed.”

“Then let us not spoil it.”

The corner of the young woman’s mouth moved.

“My granddaughter,” said Don Salvatore.

“Donna Valentina.” Andrew inclined his head to her by the degree owed to the heiress of a great house who would now never be the consort of a Sovereign. He saw her mark it down against him, and liked her rather better for it. “I have been reading about you.”

“Everyone has.” Her voice was low and amused. “I do hope you enjoyed it, Your Highness. I did not.”

“Let us begin.” Master Brandt dipped a pen.

\* \* \*

Don Salvatore began, as Andrew had expected, by not beginning.

He spoke first of the weather in the Marches, which was worse than it used to be, and of the price of deep-trench crystal, which was higher than it used to be, and of the unhappy state of the Accord in general, which was not what it used to be either. He spoke of these things beautifully and at length, with the ease of someone who had been speaking beautifully at length for several centuries and saw no reason to stop, and Andrew let him, and sat back in his chair and listened for what was not being said. The lamp hissed on its chain, and whenever Don Salvatore lifted a hand its light woke the garnet silk at his wrist to the deep red of blood-wine poured into a lamplit glass. Master Brandt’s pen hung over the page and did not descend.

Then Don Salvatore asked, in the same breath, “And how does His Highness your brother find his pilgrimage?”

“My brother,” said Andrew, “is on pilgrimage.”

“So I read.” Don Salvatore turned one of his rings a quarter turn on his finger. “He has found a most unusual shrine. He was looking very well.”

“I am glad to hear it.”

The dark eyes rested on him, patient and incurious. “He wore white silk, stiff with gold. The colour of mourning, in Aloria, I believe. I have wondered what he meant by it.”

The crystal had told him nothing of the silk. Andrew thought of his brother walking out alone into a vampire’s court in white, and of his mother in her white gown in the shuttered dark, and for a moment the two pictures lay one over the other and would not come apart. He kept all of it off his face.

“I imagine,” he said, “that he meant to be looked at.”

“He was.” That was Valentina. “By everyone.” She tilted her head at him, as her grandfather did, but her eyes were not incurious at all. “I was rather sorry, afterwards, that I had been told to dislike him.”

“And did you?”

“Not in the least. That was the worst of it.”

Don Salvatore laid one long hand over his granddaughter’s, briefly, and she fell silent.

“We will not waste each other’s time, Highness. You have read the *Lantern*, I think, with some attention.” The dark eyes moved, briefly, to Andrew’s hands, folded on the table. “The Sovereign has refused the oldest house on the Coast before the whole Council, in favour of the Alorian prince he holds under the Accord. With your brother as his consort, he will have the crystal trade in one hand and your father’s conscience in the other, and he will never need a Caruso dowry again.”

“And you would like him not to have them.”

“I should like,” said Don Salvatore, “the Coast to be ruled by a house that remembers what it owes to its own.”

“He is also,” Andrew pointed out, “by every account I have ever read, impossible to unseat.”

Don Salvatore smiled. It was a beautiful smile, and there was nothing whatever behind it. “No house on the Coast can stand against a Conti in the shadows, Highness. But there are things in the world that are not shadows.” He touched each of his rings in turn, as patiently as a harper tuning strings. “And your house, I think, has recently learned how to make them.”

Andrew let the silence lengthen until the lamp had swung once out and back on its chain, and the shadows of the three pens had swung with it across the open ledger and back again.

So the Ashward knew. Cecily’s laboratory had been set among the refineries so that its chimneys would look like all the other chimneys and its deliveries like all the other deliveries. The deep-trench stone had come in by the same wharves as the Treasury’s own. And still someone had stood in the Underglow and watched her door. He put that away, to be dealt with later.

“Solar arms are forbidden under the Accord.”

“A great many things are forbidden, Highness,” said Don Salvatore gently. “It did not stop your brother from lying down in a garden full of our withheld sunlight, I understand.”

Andrew went cold.

His heart missed and then came down hard, and for one instant he was back in the east tower with the crystal hot in his fist and Paul’s voice catching on the title. In the Citadel the table would already have been smoking under his hands. Here the oak lay cool and untouched beneath them. Then he understood that Don Salvatore was fishing, or repeating the gossip of kitchens. Don Salvatore knew nothing of the gallery, or of the throat. If he had known, he would already have taken it to the Council and asked for the Sovereign’s crown under the Accord, and there would have been no need for a vault at the Kessel Pass or a Crown Prince of Aloria at the end of an iron table.

He closed his hand on that knowledge. It was the one card at this table that no one else knew existed.

“What are you offering?” he asked.

\* \* \*

It took them two hours.

Master Brandt wrote it all down in his square, printed Holdish hand, reading each clause back in a voice without any expression and waiting with his pen lifted until each of them had agreed aloud. He looked at nothing but the ledger, which seemed to be the only honest face in the room. The pen scratched, and the lamp oil burned down by the width of a finger, and once, far off in the mountain, a mule bell rang and stopped, and none of them turned their heads.

Twelve crates, of the Grand Alchemist’s design, to be delivered by the new moon. Carried by air from the Citadel wharves by a Lowmere house of the Chamber’s choosing, and declared as machinery for a refinery. Received at the Kessel Pass, and passed to the Ashward by the Chamber’s own couriers, so that no Alorian hand and no Caruso hand would touch them in between.

In return, when Andrew was King, Aloria would recognise the Ashward as the Coast’s Sovereign house. Today it held one seat among nine on a Council that answered to a Conti. But when the Coast had need of a new Sovereign, and the shadows had leaned, Aloria would recognise the Ashward first, and loudest, while the other realms were still clearing their throats. In its turn, the Coast would restore the crystal trade at the old weights and on the old terms, carried in Lowmere ships through Thalassan waters with the tallies open to any merchant who cared to look. And the matter of the Sanctuary would be closed.

“Closed how?” asked Valentina.

“The Coast will find,” answered Andrew, “upon a fuller inquiry, that my brother was never at the vineyard.”

Don Salvatore raised his fine black brows. “Your brother’s betrothed already says so, Highness.”

“Yes. And no one believes him.”

For a moment Don Salvatore looked at him with something that might almost have been approval. Then he inclined his head, and Master Brandt wrote it down.

It was a long time before either of them came to the last clause. Andrew had known from the beginning that it would be the last. He had carried it across the Marches in his chest like a coal, and here, with no light to spend itself in, it could only lie against his breastbone where the ring had hung that spring, and burn hotter with every clause.

“There is one more condition.”

Don Salvatore waited, with his ringed white hands quite still on the dark oak.

“My brother is not to be harmed.” Andrew said it quietly, and evenly. “Not by your people, or your weapons, or anyone you pay. Not his person, not his health, not a hair of his head. Whatever the Ashward does with what I send it, it will do it somewhere my brother is not, and when it is over, he will be delivered to my people alive and whole and unafraid. That is not a term. That is the price of every other term. Write it first, Master Brandt, if you please, above all the rest.”

Master Brandt looked up for the first time in two hours. Then he turned back three pages, and dipped his pen, and wrote it at the top.

“Accidents,” murmured Don Salvatore, “do happen, Highness. In war. Even to princes.”

“Then there will be no war,” said Andrew, “only an accident, and it will happen to one person. You are very old, Don Salvatore, and very patient, and I am sure you can manage that.” He waited until the dwarf’s pen had caught him up. “If my brother is so much as singed, I will know it. And the Ashward will learn what else the Grand Alchemist can make.”

The cold-iron walls held everything, and no shadow stirred in the corners. The heat pressed at the backs of his eyes and found the iron waiting for it, so that his eyes stayed dark and his voice level, and only his folded hands had whitened at the knuckles. The dwarf’s pen scratched on in the silence.

At last Don Salvatore lifted one ringed hand an inch from the oak and laid it down again. “Write it, Master Brandt.”

Master Brandt wrote it. And while the ink was drying, Don Salvatore remarked to the lamp that the Sovereign would no doubt wish to have his betrothal sealed under the bond-law of the Holds, as the old custom required, and that he understood Master Brandt’s city would shortly have the pleasure of receiving the happy couple. The road from the Coast to Durnhollow was a long one, he added, and ran through some very lonely country.

“The Chamber does not discuss the business of its other clients.” Master Brandt did not look up.

Andrew did not look up either. But he heard it, every word, and kept it.

\* \* \*

When it was done, and signed, and sanded, and the Chamber’s seven-bar seal pressed into three separate pools of grey wax at the foot of three separate copies, Don Salvatore rose and bowed and took his leave with the same unhurried courtesy with which he had come, and went out into the iron corridor. The cold went out with him, and the lamp flame stood up a little straighter in its glass.

Valentina stayed.

She stood at the end of the table, pulling on her gloves one finger at a time, and looked at Andrew until Master Brandt, who had been a banker a very long time, gathered up his ledger and his pens and discovered urgent business in the next room.

“You have not asked me about the kiss, Your Highness.” She settled the last finger. “Everyone asks. My maid asked, which I thought very forward of her. You have sat across that table for two hours with that terrible newspaper folded in your coat, and you have not asked once.”

He had forgotten it was there. Now he could feel it, stiff against his ribs, the fold of it pressing through his shirt. He kept his hand from it.

“I read the account.”

“The account is very poor.” She smoothed her gloves. “It was quite brief. The Sovereign kissed him the way one seals a letter. Your brother did not kiss him back. He stood there in his white silk as if someone had walked up and struck him with a flower.” She watched him, and seemed to decide. “He did not step away, either.”

Andrew looked at her.

Inside the iron there was nowhere for the heat to go. There was only the lamp, and the cold, and his own jaw tightening until his teeth ached, and the picture she had given him, which he could not now put down. His brother, who had never stood still for anyone in his life, who had wriggled out of Matilda’s arms at a year old and crossed a whole hall rather than be held where he did not choose, standing still in a vampire’s hands.

“You may go, Donna Valentina.”

She came a step nearer, and then another, until only the corner of the table lay between them, and lowered her voice. A faint scent came with her, bitter and green, like a crushed stem. “My grandfather thinks that you want the Sovereign dead for your crown. He has seen a great many princes.” Her dark eyes rested on his face. “I think you would burn the whole of Aloria to the ground to get your brother back, and that you would not stop to count the dead.”

The lamp swung on its chain.

“Then you understand me,” said Andrew, “rather better than your grandfather does.”

“I thought I might.” She drew her cloak about her shoulders. “I hope your brother is grateful.”

She went out. The iron door swung shut behind her with a soft, heavy sound, like a ledger closing, and Andrew was alone.

He sat on for a while in the iron room with his hands flat on the cool oak, and nothing in the world burned.

\* \* \*

He did not go back to the Citadel that night. He had the yacht take him up above the mist, over the high white spine of the Marches, and there he sent the crew below and stood alone on the forward deck in the dark with his coat open to the wind.

The Marches had an ordinary night. He had forgotten that. Far from the spire-crystals there was only a sky so black and so crowded with stars that it seemed to hang a hand’s breadth above the rigging, and away to the south, very low on the horizon, a smudge of darker darkness where the ash clouds of the Coast began. The wind came off the snowfields smelling of nothing at all, and it went through his open coat and through his shirt, and he let it.

Overhead the stars burned white and cold and far off, a great pale river of them running from one edge of the sky to the other. Caleb would have talked the whole night through about them, and asked the name of every one, and not waited for the answers.

Away from the iron, the light came back into him. He felt it rise behind his eyes, slow and hot, and for once he let it. His eyes burned white in the dark, and the glare of them lay across the brass of the rail and the frost on the boards, the only light for a hundred miles. There was no one on the deck to see, and no paper to scorch.

He took the crystal from his breast pocket and held it in his palm until it warmed.

The voice at the other end answered almost at once. It was very low, with the muffled closeness of words spoken into cupped hands in a corridor, by a man with his back against a door. “Highness.”

“The Sovereign will take my brother to Durnhollow,” Andrew said, “to seal the betrothal under the bond-law. Before the new moon, or soon after. I want to know the day they leave, and the road they mean to take, and how many go with them. As soon as you know it yourself.”

There was a silence at the other end. Through it, faint and close, Andrew could hear the man breathing, and beyond the breathing the hush of some great stone house asleep.

“Highness,” Paul protested, at last. “His Highness saved my life.”

“I know. You told me.” Andrew looked out at the smudge of darkness on the southern horizon. “He would. He has never been able to bear seeing anyone hurt, not even a soldier sent to watch him. It is the best thing about him, and it is the reason I need you there.”

The silence stretched. The crystal lay warm in his palm, and the wind sang thinly in the rigging, and Andrew waited, and kept the waiting out of his voice.

“Your mother is well,” Andrew said, into it. He said it gently, in the voice one uses to a frightened horse. “I had a letter from the steward at Loch Aird. Her cough is much improved. Your sisters send their love.”

At the other end the breathing stopped, and then began again, more carefully. After a long time, the voice answered, “Yes, Highness.”

The crystal went dark.

Andrew put it away. He stood at the rail with his hands on the cold brass and his face turned south, towards the Coast and the smudge of ash and the fortress in the mountain where his brother was sleeping now, in a garden of stolen light, with a vampire’s ring on his hand.

He thought of the circlet ringing on the white marble, and a warm weight against his shoulder, and two fat hands on his face, and eyes that had looked at him from a hand’s breadth away and seen no distance at all. He had been the heir to a whole kingdom all his life, and he had been chosen only once, by a baby who did not know what an heir was.

He had made his brother a promise once, in the white light of the Throne Hall, and meant it with his whole heart. He meant it still.

He would bring him home. And then he would never let anything hurt him again, whatever it cost, and whoever had to pay.
