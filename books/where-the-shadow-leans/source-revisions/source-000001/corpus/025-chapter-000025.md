## Chapter 24: Epilogue - The Garden

The Coast kept no seasons, and saw no reason to begin keeping them for an Alorian, so it was by the Citadel’s calendar and his sisters’ letters that Caleb knew it was autumn when Paul Walker came back.

He was on the water-stair when it happened, at the Coast’s evening, with a book open on his knee that he had stopped reading some while before. The twelve crystals hung above him on their chains, and the water went chattering down the black stone beside him, and the lemon trees had come on again in their urns with a new crop, green and hard and full of opinions. Every white rose and golden lily in the garden was looking up at the doors.

They had done it since he came home from the Marches. They had turned from the crystals all together on the night his finished ring first woke the runes on the doors, every rose and every lily in one movement, and since then, whenever Arian came down and sat on the sill, they turned to the sill and stayed turned. Tomas had given up putting them right. He deadheaded round them, as a housemaid dusts round a grandfather asleep in his chair.

Arian was on the sill now, as he was at that bell every evening, with his shoulders to the iron frame and his boots out in the corridor, and the garden’s light coming out along the iron beside him as far as his knee. The notes he made in the margins of the Council’s papers went down in silver ink and came up grey with frost before they were dry. Above him the shadow that had gone up the vent after a sling still lay where it had stopped, reaching, singed brown at the end. He had never told it to come down.

Arian lifted his head, as he lifted it for a heart on the other side of a wall, and Caleb laid his book face down on the stone, which Eleanor held to be a hanging matter, and watched the doors.

For a long while nobody came into them. Then somebody did, and stopped in the dark of the corridor a pace behind the sill and two paces from the Sovereign, with a grey blanket rolled under one arm and a bundle in the other hand, and stood there looking at the floor, as he had stood at Caleb’s back for fourteen years.

Caleb went up.

He climbed between the lemon trees and the terrace walls with their roses hanging over, and the flowers that had been looking at the sill turned after him as he climbed, and back to the sill, and after him again, as the crowd along the lists at a tourney follows the lance from end to end and cannot make up its mind which knight it came to see. The light kept pace with him across the mezzanine as far as the sill, and no further.

Arian had not got up from the sill.

“The sergeant carried my bag up from the harbour, Highness,” said Paul, to the floor. “I asked him to let me carry it. He wouldn’t.”

Caleb looked at him. The Highland March had put colour into his face and woodsmoke into his coat, which was a plain brown thing with no livery anywhere on it, and he had shaved for the Coast, which Caleb had never known him do for anybody, and had cut himself doing it, under the jaw.

“How is your mother?”

“Well, Highness.” Paul’s mouth moved. “She’s well. Truly. She says that’s how it’s to be said now, so I’ll hear the difference.” He shifted the blanket under his arm. “She sent me.”

“Did she?”

“She said I’d sat under her roof all summer looking at the door, and I was to go and look at the right one.”

On the sill Arian had laid his papers aside and sat quite still, with his head on one side, listening to something nobody else in the corridor could hear.

Paul lifted his head.

It came up a little at a time, as a man straightens from a load he has carried so long that he has forgotten the weight of it, and he looked up from the stone of the corridor at Caleb, and kept looking.

“Highness.” His hand tightened on the blanket. “Is it yet?”

The last time, under a black sun with the yachts lifting round them, Caleb had given him two words and kept back the rest.

This was the rest of it.

“Yes,” said Caleb.

Paul did not move. He stood in the corridor with the blanket under his arm and his eyes on Caleb’s face, and his throat moved, twice, and nothing came up it.

“I’d not ask you to forgive me, Highness,” he said at last.

“No. You have never asked me for anything in fourteen years but a door. It was the whole trouble with you.” Caleb put out his hand across the sill and laid it flat on Paul’s side, under the ribs, on the plain brown cloth. The last time his hand had lain there it had been in the blood to the wrist, on the black stone of the lower yard. It was warm under his palm now, and whole. “I forgave you on the Guard’s field, while your mother was talking into her hands. I have been waiting a season for you to come and be told so. You took your time.”

“I’d wood to split, Highness.”

“So I see. Did you shave with the axe?”

Paul put his hand to his jaw, and looked at the blood on his fingers, and laughed, rustily, as a gate opens that nobody has used all summer. Caleb had not heard the sound since before the spring.

The frost went out across the iron from the sill, a finger’s breadth towards the corridor, and stopped.

Paul turned to the sill and stood straighter, as the Guard stands when an officer it does not know has come in.

Arian stayed where he was. He looked up at Paul from the iron with the frost on his lashes, as he had looked at him down a black gallery with a prince’s blood gold on his mouth. Caleb knew what that look had done to a house of the Coast in a single night, and so, he thought, did Paul. This time Paul did not look at the floor.

“For eleven weeks your heart kept another man’s time,” said Arian.

“Yes, Sovereign.”

“It does not, now.”

Paul’s hand had gone to his chest, flat, as if to see for himself.

“No, Sovereign.”

“Then keep the door.” Arian took up his papers again.

Paul looked at Caleb.

“Which door, Highness?”

Caleb thought of the doors there were. There was the door of the Sovereign’s study, which was never shut now, and the door at the far end of the study, which was shut a good deal.

“This one.” He laid his hand on the iron. “It is the only door on the mountain I can open, and so it is the only one I am entitled to give away.”

Paul looked at the great doors with the runes of the Holds cut over every inch of them, and at the round hollow in the iron where a ring went.

He set down his bundle. He unrolled the grey blanket on the corridor floor, on the other side of the doorway from the Sovereign, where the dark was, and sat down on it against the iron with his knees up, as he had sat on the night of the gallery.

There was nothing in his boot.

Down the corridor somebody was coming with a tray. Vesna came round the turn of the wall under the violet lamps with bread and broth under a cloth, and stopped, and looked down at the man on the blanket for some time.

“Walker. You’ll want feeding.”

He went to the outer temples at the end of that week, on pilgrimage, since the whole Dominion had been told in the spring that he was on one, and it seemed to him high time that somebody went.

Eleanor lent him a yacht. She entered it in the Treasury’s books under *Pilgrimages, sundry*, and sent him a copy of the entry so that he should know it had been done properly. Paul came with him and sat on the step of the cabin door all the way north, because he said that a door was a door.

The outer temples stood on the last ridge of the Solar lands, where the noon of the spires gave out. Caleb felt it go. Over a long bare upland of heather and grey stone the white began to wear thin beneath the yacht, as the last of a wave sinks into a long beach, and the sky came through it blue, and there was the sun. It was a real one, gold and low in the west over the Marches, and every stone on the ridge had a shadow lying out behind it. On the crest, white and low and very old, with a bell in an arch on its roof and wild thyme grown up between its steps, stood the temple.

His mother met him at the gate.

She wore white still, but it was the plain undyed white of the temple, with no gold thread in it anywhere, and her hair was braided down her back as he had never seen it by daylight, and there was no chamberlain behind her. She stood in the gate with her hands folded before her and watched him come up through the thyme with Paul two paces behind him, as she had watched him across a Throne Hall.

“You came.”

“I had your letters. I read them twice.”

“You did not answer them.”

“No.”

“I did not ask you to.”

She had written every week, on the temple’s plain paper, and there had been nothing in any of the letters that asked for anything. She wrote of the bell, and the thyme, and the pilgrims who came up the ridge with their shoes in their hands, and once of a vineyard in the Marches, where her elder son lay under the new rows, and from which the Consigliere of the Coast had written her a single line in silver to say that the cuttings had taken there, all eleven rows. And at the end of every letter, under her name, on a line by itself, she had written what colour the sun had been when it went down.

“Your father is on the terrace,” said his mother. “He goes out at this hour.”

She took him through the temple, through a court of white stone with a cistern in it and a fig tree leaning over the cistern to look at itself, and out at the far side onto a long terrace that faced west over the Marches. At the door she stopped, and let him go on alone.

His father sat on a stone bench at the end of the terrace, in temple linen, with his hands on his knees and his face to the sun.

He had sat with his back to Caleb all his life. He had kept it turned to him at the windows of his study, and in the Throne Hall, and in the middle of Caleb’s own floor on the night before the wine, with the noon coming in round him like water round a post, and said half a sentence over his shoulder, and gone away.

He heard Caleb’s step on the stone. He turned round.

“Caleb,” said his father.

“Majesty.”

His father looked at him, at the black coat of the Coast and the crescent over its heart and the gold coming faintly at its seams, and at the iron ring on his left hand with its setting filled at last, and did not look away.

“Will you sit?”

It was a question. Caleb sat.

The bench was warm from the day. He sat at the far end of it from his father, and after a while his mother came out from the door and sat down between them, and the three of them sat in a row on a stone bench in the open with a hand’s breadth of warm stone between each of them and the next, which nobody crossed, and looked at the Marches. There was room on the bench for one more, at his father’s end. Nobody sat in it.

The sun went down.

It went slowly, as suns go in the Marches, as if there were a great deal of country to bid goodnight and it meant to leave none of it out. The long slopes below the ridge went from gold to copper, and the shadows of the stones ran out eastward over the heather until they reached the temple wall and began to climb it, and down in the valleys the first lamps of the hill towns came out one at a time, and the sky over the far hills turned a colour Caleb had no word for.

“Apricot,” said his mother, to the sky. “Last night it was nearer the inside of a shell.”

“You keep notes.”

“I had never sat and watched one go down until this summer. Nor had your father. We had lived the whole of our lives in the noon.” She folded her hands in her lap. “I was told once of somebody who took notes on the sun for a man who had never seen it. I thought I might take them for somebody who never saw it end.”

Caleb said nothing. Beyond her his father sat with his face to the west and the last of the light on it, and the light did not make it gold, because nothing had made it gold since the morning he walked off the throne.

When the sun was gone, and the sky over the Marches had gone from apricot to violet and the first star had come out low in the south, his father spoke, to the dark.

“Will you come again?”

Caleb thought about it honestly. He thought of a chair turned round to face a door, and of a door he had left open behind him on purpose, with the noon lying through it across a study floor.

“Let me have a season.”

His father was quiet a long while. Caleb watched him hear his own words come back to him across a bench, in somebody else’s voice.

“That is fair,” said his father.

“We shall see in the spring.” Caleb stood.

His mother walked him back through the court with the fig tree, and at the gate she stopped, and did not put out her hand.

“Tell me what colour it is. Where you are. If you should think of it.”

“There is no sun where I am, Mother.”

“I know.” She looked at the black coat. “Tell me anyway.”

He went down through the thyme to the yacht with Paul behind him. At the foot of the steps he looked back, which he had not been able to do in the Citadel.

His mother stood in the gate in her white. And beyond her, through the temple and the court and out at the far door, in the dusk, his father had turned all the way round on his bench, with his back to the last of the sky, to watch him go.

He had been down to the cellar and the lower yard and the lamp room, and in all that while never once to the kitchens of Castel Nerezza. It was Mirek who took him in the end, on two good legs, taking every stair two at a time to prove that he could, with the book of recipes from the Sovereign’s study under Caleb’s arm.

The kitchens lay under the great hall, cut into the warm heart of the mountain, and they were the only rooms in that cold fortress that were too hot. The bread ovens had never been lit in a thousand years, because they had never needed to be. The red river ran close behind the rock, and its heat came up through their floors, dull and steady, and the loaves came out brown and smelling of warm crust. Copper hung on every wall, and Wildmarch herbs hung from the vaulting by the hundred with their heads down, like bats in a barn, and the whole place smelled of yeast and woodsmoke and rosemary and, faintly, of blood-wine warming on the back of the range.

Vesna was at the long table with flour to the elbow and the beetroot cosy on the pot at her side, and when she saw what was under his arm she put down her rolling pin.

“His Highness is in my kitchen.”

“His Highness has a question.” He laid the book among the flour and opened it at the only page it would open at. *Scones, after the manner of the Citadel kitchens.* There was the floury thumbprint in the margin, and beside it the silver word that had been written, and struck out, and written over again. He put his finger under it. “What is this word?”

Vesna did not need to look long.

“*Teplo*.”

“Which means?”

“Warm.” She wiped her hands on her apron. “He wrote it so I’d not mistake the oven. He spelled it like an Alorian, with too many letters on the end, and I told him so, and he struck it out and wrote it again. At that table. With his face like Mirek’s over his letters.”

“I never made a face,” Mirek protested, from the door.

“You’re making one now.” She did not look round. “A man who’s never once in his life been warm, writing it down twice in my book so it’d be right.”

Caleb looked at the word. He had wondered at it since a night in the Sovereign’s study with the primer in his hand, and had chosen a scone over the answer.

“When?”

Vesna looked at him across the book and the flour, and he saw her weigh it, as she weighed flour in her palm without the scale, and find it come out right.

“First week of the winter.”

The heat of the ovens came up through the floor.

“There was snow on the hill,” said Vesna. “We’d a house up the Wildmarch, the last on the street, with Tomas’s roses up the front of it, the only roses in the town. He came up the hill road at night on his feet, alone, with his boots full of snow, and knocked on my door like a tinker.” She sniffed. “I’d never seen a Conti in my life. Nobody on that hill had. I opened the door with a pan in my hand.”

“What did he want?”

“He asked.” She looked at him. “That’s what I told you on your first morning, and it was true, and you never asked me what. He said he was building a garden under the mountain and filling it with suns, and there wasn’t a soul in his house could set foot in it, nor ever would. He wanted a cook who could carry a tray into the light, and a man who could keep roses under twelve suns without his hair catching, and a boy to run up the stairs when the cook’s knees went.”

Mirek made a noise at the door.

“He said there’d be somebody coming in the summer,” Vesna went on. “Somebody who’d been in the light his whole life, and would be cold here, and frightened, and rude to everybody.” She folded her arms. “He said he was to be fed.”

Caleb sat down on the bench.

“In the summer.”

“After the Summit. He’d it all thought out. You were to come in the summer, wed, and stay, and I’d have had six months at the scones.” She looked down at the book. “Then there was the vineyard, and you came in the spring, asleep, three months early, and I’d nothing to go by but his writing.”

“And the scones —”

“I made them every week from the snow on, from his writing. Stones, every week. Tomas ate them and said nothing, because he’s a good man.” She did not look at the door. “Mirek fed his to the dogs in the yard.”

“The dogs liked them,” said Mirek.

“The dogs were sick on the stair,” said Vesna. “And on your first morning I made them the same, because it was his writing and I’d been asked. And you sat on that chaise in your lace and ate every one, with butter, and told me they were very fine.”

“They were dreadful.”

“They were.” Something moved in her face. “Then I made them properly.”

Caleb looked at the book, at the thumbprint and the silver word beside it, written by a man in a cold study who could not feel his own fire, in the first week of a winter with no hostage in it and no debt, for a garden that nobody had asked him to build. He had thought, at a camp table in the Marches with a dwarf’s thumb on his ring, that he had come to the bottom of that column.

“Did he tell you to call me —”

“He did not.” Vesna took up her rolling pin. “That one’s mine.”

He sat at the long table and found that he could not for a moment say anything at all, and Vesna let him not say it, and went back to her pastry, because she had raised sons and knew when to look at the pastry.

“He couldn’t go in,” she told it, after a while. “So he sent for somebody who could.”

Caleb got up.

“Where’s His Highness going?”

“To the garden.” At the door he stopped, because it was owed. “Thank you, Vesna.”

“Don’t you start counting at me,” Vesna warned, and went on rolling.

Arian was on the sill.

On the far side of the doorway Paul sat on his grey blanket with his back to the other leaf of the iron, and between them the light of the garden came out through the open doors and lay across the sill in its long gold line, and neither of them was in it. Caleb came down the corridor out of the violet dark and thought that they looked like two stone dogs on either side of a gate, which he did not say, since one of them was a Sovereign and the other had only lately stopped looking at the floor.

Arian looked up.

“Vesna has told me about the scones.”

Arian’s face did nothing at all.

“And the snow, and the hill road, and your spelling.”

“She told me it was not hers to say.”

“She has changed her mind. It is the fashion on this mountain.” He looked down at the light lying on the iron short of Arian’s knee, as it had lain every night for half a year. “You built a garden that you could not go into, and hung it with suns that you could not stand under, and then you went up a hill in the snow with your boots full of it and knocked on a door to find somebody who could carry a tray down the stairs that you would never walk.”

“Yes,” said Arian.

“Every soul in this house has been down those stairs but you. Vesna and Tomas and Mirek. The seamstresses, with my coat. Paul. My sister, to count the stones.” He stopped. “Do you want to come in?”

Arian looked at him from the sill for a long time. Far down in the dark of his eyes a light went by, a long way off, as the deck lamps of the night ferry go by at the foot of the Coast, seen from the high road.

“Yes.”

Caleb stepped past him over the sill into the gold, and turned, and knelt on the iron of the mezzanine, facing him across the line where the light stopped.

“Give me your hand.”

Arian gave him the right, with the three rings on it and the Sovereign’s heavy gold on the third finger. Caleb took it by the wrist. He turned his own left hand palm up, ring and all, and laid it on the dark side of the line, and set Arian’s fingers across his palm, so that his own hand was beneath. It was the other way about from a day in the soil of a glasshouse, when there had been a boy’s hand under his.

Then he asked.

He had learned on the Coast to ask his light for less than it wished to give. He had asked a hall’s noon to lean aside and his sister’s sunlight to stay. He had never asked anything so old as what hung over his garden, nor so large, and he did not ask it in words. He asked it as a man asks a horse he has raised from a foal, with the whole of himself and no whip, and under the light, at the bottom of him, the old thing that had stood up on a hill in the Marches stood up again, and spread, and the garden heard.

The crystals turned.

All twelve of them, on their silver chains, down the long spiral of the vent, turned together, slowly, as they had turned once and chimed when he said a thing at this door that he had not known he was going to say, until every one of them had its face to the sill. And in the heart of each, the light moved.

It had stood still in them since they were cut from the trench, as the light of every deep-trench stone stands still, and burns. Now, in all twelve at once, it began to go round. It went round as the light went round in the stone on Caleb’s finger, slowly, lighting its stone to the edges each time it came. And the light that came down out of them over the roses and the long fall of the water-stair was gold still, but it was the gold of a bath run in a nursery. It was the colour that came up in his own skin when he kept it low for somebody he did not wish to burn.

He drew Arian’s hand over the line.

He drew it slowly, with his own beneath it, as one draws a child’s hand towards a hearth to teach it what warm is. The light came over the backs of Arian’s fingers, the same fingers that had reddened and blistered on that same iron half a year before, and lay on them.

The frost on them lit. It caught the gold and held it, and did not smoke.

The skin under it stayed white.

Arian looked down at his hand, and went on looking, so long that the light went round twice in every stone above them, and Caleb did not hurry him. He had waited for the end of a great many sentences that year, and found that he did not mind waiting for this one.

“What have you done?”

“Asked.” Caleb did not let go. “They are hearts. Cecily says so, and Andrew said so, and was right, and I am told the nursery has said so for a thousand years and nobody listened to it. They woke once on a hill and saw where the wings went. I asked them to do what I do.”

Arian waited.

“Keep it low, so that it warms and does not burn. I learned it for you, in a room you had never lit. It seemed a pity not to teach it to the furniture.”

Arian’s fingers moved on his palm. They turned over, slowly, and lay palm up in the gold, open, with the light lying in the cup of them, as a man holds out his hand from a doorway to see whether it has begun to rain.

“It is warm.”

“Yes.”

Under Caleb’s fingers, at the wrist, the heart that kept his time came hard against the frost, as a man knocks who has stood a long while at a door in the snow.

“It has never once been anything but bright,” murmured Arian. He was looking at the light in his hand.

Caleb stood up. He stood on the mezzanine in the gold with the garden going down behind him, terrace under terrace, to the water at the bottom, and kept hold of the hand.

“Come in.”

Arian looked up at him from the sill.

“May I?”

“It is your garden,” said Caleb.

“It is yours.”

“Then I am asking you into mine.”

Arian stood.

He had sat on that sill every night since the first, shoulders to the iron and boots in the corridor, when the doors were shut against him and when they stood open, and he had burned his fingers on it on the twenty-first day and said nothing of it, and kept it a whole night with the light stopped at his knee while a prince lay in the lilies below with a flower opening in his blood, and come back to it starving with silver in both hands, and set his boot on its gold line when a sling went up past it through a fall of ash, and drawn the boot back smoking because he had been asked to, and sat on it a whole season since, every evening, with his papers on his knee and his shadow burned up the wall above him, a hand’s breadth from everything he had ever made for anybody, and never once put out his hand for any of it.

He stepped over it.

The light came up over his boots and his knees and his coat and his face, and the frost lit on him from his hair to his heels, as it had lit on a hill under the wings, and did not smoke. He stood in the light of twelve suns, inside the garden he had made, and nothing whatever happened to him.

Every flower there turned to him, all at once, every white rose on every terrace and every lily in the long bed by the water-stair, and the lemon trees leaned in their urns.

And behind him, on the iron, his shadow lay down.

It had never lain there. It came out from his heels long and black across the iron, the first shadow of the Umbral houses the garden had ever been asked to hold, and the garden held it. Above the door, the shadow that had reached up the vent all summer came down. It came down the black stone as a cat comes down off a high shelf when the one it was waiting for has come home, and lay down at his heels and went into the other, scorched end and all, and was his.

Caleb felt it set, through the hand he held, as one knows in a quiet house that a clock has stopped, by the hush where the ticking had been. On the iron the edges of the shadow, which had never once in his sight lain quiet, went still and clean, as a pond sets in the first hard frost, from the rim inward. The Coast said that a vampire’s years stopped where his shadow set, and that the deepest set last. Caleb looked at the face beside him in the gold, which would be that face now by this light and every other for as long as the Coast had dark to lend, and found that he had no objection to raise.

Somewhere on the terraces below there was the sound of shears put down.

Tomas stood among the roses on the second terrace, broad as a door, with black earth to his elbows and his cap in his hand, and he looked up at the Sovereign of the Coast in his garden. Then he turned to the nearest bush and cut a rose from it, the best on the terrace, and came up the iron stairs with it, and laid it on the iron at the top of them, a stride short of the Sovereign’s boots, and went back down a stair, without a word.

Arian stooped and took it up.

He held it a moment in both hands, as he had once held out a child’s cloak. Then he bent his head to it, and Caleb watched him breathe in.

“It smells of the glasshouse.”

“You built it so.”

Arian breathed it in again, and Caleb thought that he had made a whole garden smell of a glasshouse twenty years gone, and had never once been near enough to know whether he had got it right.

In the doorway, Paul had got up off his blanket, and stood with his hand flat on the iron, holding open a door that did not need holding, with the light on his face.

They went down.

Arian went down the iron stairs of the garden with the rose in one hand and Caleb’s hand in the other, a step at a time, and turned his face to everything as he passed it, the lemons and the starflower and the silverleaf and the water going down its stair, as if he meant to learn every one of them by name and would not be hurried about it. At the bottom he stopped by the pool under the water-stair, where Caleb had knelt four mornings running to look for two marks under his jaw, and the black stone under the water gave him back a man standing in gold. Then he stood among the little golden lilies of the Citadel’s lower terraces, which nobody at home had ever thought worth a second look, and looked up.

He looked up the length of the garden, past the terraces and the roses all bent down towards him, past the twelve stones going round on their chains in their slow gold, to the iron doors at the top, standing open on the violet dark of the corridor, with a lycan in a brown coat in them no bigger than a thumb at that distance, keeping the door.

He had only ever seen it from there.

“Stay in the light,” said Caleb.

Arian looked at him.

He had been asked. He stayed.

He lay down on the warm black earth between the rose beds, where a prince of the Solar House had lain down on the first night he came into that place, under a sun that was not a sun, and Caleb lay down beside him with his head on the cold shoulder, and the light went round above them. It went round in the twelve all that night, and in the morning, and it never stood still in them again. And some time about the second turn, the Sovereign of the Obsidian Coast, who did not sleep a great deal, was asleep in the light, with a white rose on his breast and the frost on his lashes lit gold, and his shadow lying out from him across the lilies, and every flower in the garden leaning towards it.

Their first journey was to Nauplion.

Enzo made the arrangements. The boat had been the Ashward’s, a broad harbour boat with a red sail and a cabin under her deck lined with lead for the wine trade, and she was the Coast’s now, with the rest of what the Ashward had held. Nobody had painted the black thorn off her prow. When he was asked why, Enzo said that she had never done anything wrong. They went out under the ash by night, with the violet lamps of the harbour dwindling behind them, and down through the Thalassan straits, and by day the Sovereign of the Coast sat in the leaded cabin with the hatch shut, and the prince of the Solar House sat on the hatch in the sun with a notebook and read him the sea through the boards.

“*Blue*,” he read, on the first morning. “*Not the blue of the Hall of Petitions, nor of the Chancellery. It is the blue of a jay’s wing feather, if the jay had come into a great deal of money and been told to spend it.*”

The hatch said nothing for so long that Caleb had begun to compose a defence of the jay.

“Write it down,” said the hatch.

They came into Nauplion on the third evening, with the sun going down red behind the hill, and it was barely gone before the hatch came up and Arian came up out of it and stood at the rail in the dusk with the frost coming out on his collar in the warm Thalassan air.

The town went up its hill from the water white, house over house, and every door in it was painted blue, because the merfolk say that blue is the colour of the deep, and a blue door is a door the sea will not come knocking at. The harbour lay under it as round as a cup. All along the quays the braziers were lit, and at each a woman with her sleeves rolled stood over a copper pan as wide as a cartwheel, and the smell came out across the water to meet the boat, hot oil and salt and lemon, and Caleb, who had once drawn this harbour on a tablecloth in mustard, stood at the rail and breathed it in like a man come home to a place he had never been.

Then the light went out of the sky, and the merfolk came in.

Caleb saw them first as light. The water at the harbour mouth lit from underneath, pale and green and cold, and the light came in under the boats in long ribbons, as smoke comes in under a door, and wherever it went the hulls of the fishing boats stood out black on it. Then the heads came up.

They came up all over the harbour at once, sleek and dark, with the water running off them silver, and swam in to the quays and drew themselves up onto the lowest steps with their tails still in the water. Their hair hung to their waists like weed at low tide. Their faces were grave and fine and very old, like the faces on coins that have lain a long while in a well, and below the waist they were barred blue and green and black, as a mackerel is, and shone. Each of them had brought up a basket of woven weed, and in each basket, heaped and silver and still moving, were the little fish.

They sold them to the women at the pans, a basket for a honeycake, since there is no honey in the sea, and the women tipped the fish into the oil by the handful, and they went in hissing and came out gold. And the merfolk did not swim away. They stayed on the steps with their chins on their arms and watched every handful go in, with an expression of the deepest horror and delight, as children at a fair will watch a man swallow fire, and say it is dreadful, and come back the next night to see him do it again.

Caleb ate three.

He ate them as the quays ate them, out of a twist of paper, hot, heads and all, with salt on his fingers and a cut of lemon squeezed over the top, standing at the edge of the lowest dry step with the green light of the harbour coming up under his boots. They were the best thing he had ever put in his mouth. He said so, to the nearest of the merfolk, who was young and had a shell threaded in her hair, and she looked at him with the expression of a duchess who has been told that somebody at her table has eaten the centrepiece.

“It was a *fish*.”

“It was a magnificent fish.”

She said something over her shoulder in the tongue of the deep, and the lowest step laughed, a sound like a pebble beach when the wave goes back. Then she looked past him, up the steps, to where Arian stood well back in the light of the braziers with frost on his shoulders and no paper twist, since there was nothing on any copper pan in Nauplion that was any use to him.

She looked at him a long while, as the merfolk look at the moon.

Then she lifted her hand to him from the step, wet and open, as the quays lift a hand to a boat going out, and said a word, and slid back into the green water and was gone.

The harbour-master told Caleb afterwards, when he had bought him a cup of something resinous and dreadful, that the word meant *cousin*. He said the merfolk kept it for drowned sailors and the moon, and that he had never once heard it said to anybody standing up.

\* \* \*

They sailed for home at the turn of the night.

It was a fine night, by any measure that Caleb could afterwards bring himself to apply. The stars were out over the straits from rim to rim, and a wind came off the land behind them, warm and steady and smelling of thyme, and the red sail filled with it, and the boat put her shoulder down into the swell of the straits and lifted, and put it down again, and lifted.

Caleb was sick a little after the first bell.

He was sick over the rail, thoroughly and without the least dignity, while the merfolk, who had come out after the boat past the harbour light with their green glow going along under the water, rose all round the hull to watch.

A hand came down on his brow.

It was cold. It was as cold as a pewter plate left out on a sill in the frost, and it lay across his burning forehead flat and still and did not move, and it was beyond any argument the finest thing the Coast had yet done for him. No tutor of the Harp Vales had ever thought to mention that it was what the Umbral houses were for.

“Thank you,” said Caleb, to the sea.

“That is two hundred and nine.”

“You are inventing it.”

“I keep the book.” The hand did not move.

Caleb hung over the rail with the cold palm on his brow and the green light moving under the boat, and was sick again, and came up from it, and found that he had breath enough left to be indignant with.

“I wish it to be recorded,” he protested, “that when I told you in a carriage, on the first night, that I wished to be sick on the boat home, it was a figure of speech.”

“I wrote it down.”

Caleb turned his head under the hand.

“You wrote it down.”

Arian said nothing more. He was looking out over the straits, at the stars and the dark land going away behind them, with the wind of Thalassa in his hair, and Caleb lay over the rail under his hand and counted it off for himself. There had been the stars, which he had asked for last and wanted most, and the Holds, and no giant yet, since the Holds were firm that a bridge was not a giant, and no canals, which Maartje Bosboom said were a matter of time, and the merfolk, and the little fried fish.

The boat went down into the swell and came up again.

“And being sick on the boat home,” Caleb added faintly.

“Yes.”

“What is next?”

“Lutèce. In the spring.”

Caleb considered the spring, and the three days in which nobody in Lutèce went to bed, and the lanterns on its bridges, which the old tales said would bend after a passing king, and which might very well bend after a Sovereign of the Coast if they knew what was good for them. He considered the swell of the Thalassan straits, which had not stopped.

“Is there a boat?”

“No.”

“Then I accept,” said Caleb, and was sick again, and the Sovereign of the Obsidian Coast held his head, and the merfolk of Nauplion, who had followed them all the way out to the edge of the deep water to see whether the shining one would do it a third time, went home very well satisfied.
