## Chapter 4: Chapter Three - The Price of Light

*Deep-trench Aether for the Citadel: eight consigned, four shipped, four withheld by order of the Sovereign. This is the third month. The Council has been told it is a dispute over price, and has so far been kind enough to believe it.*

— From the private ledger of Enzo Conti, Consigliere to House Conti

\* \* \*

Vesna came for him in the morning.

He woke among the roses with earth on his cuffs, to a knocking on the great iron doors that would have done credit to a battering ram, and to a voice through the ironwork demanding to know whether His Highness intended to lie about in the dirt all day like a turnip. He climbed the iron stairs in his ruined lace and pressed the ring into the hollow, and the doors opened to Vesna, carrying a laden tray and wearing the expression of a woman who had been kept waiting by better princes than he.

She came out onto the mezzanine and stopped.

The light fell over her, gold on her brown arms and her greying hair, and she stood a while with the tray in her hands and looked down at the terraces, at the white roses and the lemon trees and the long bed of golden lilies. Then she looked at him, at the colour come back into his face and the earth on his cuffs, and her own face softened in a way he had never seen it soften before. It was the look of a woman who has sat up all night beside a fever and watched it break at dawn.

Whatever she felt or thought, she kept to herself. She only set the tray down on the top step of the iron stairs, which was apparently where he would be taking his breakfast from now on, told him to eat before the eggs went cold, and paused on her way out to pluck a rose leaf from his hair, brisk and fond at once, the way a mother tidies a son.

The eggs were excellent. So, to his great surprise, were the scones.

\* \* \*

He went to the garden every day after that, and little by little the garden restored him to his usual splendour.

The gold came first, returning to his skin in the course of a single afternoon, faint and then less faint, until he could hold his hand up to the crystals and see the old familiar shimmer running along the backs of his fingers. The cold went out of his hands. He slept eight hours instead of fourteen, and woke from them rested, and on the third morning he caught sight of himself in the black glass of the cavern wall and saw that the light had come back into his eyes. He had not known until then that it had gone.

He took to spending whole days among the terraces. He walked the paths until he knew every turn of them, and read on the warm stone beside the water-stair, and discovered that the lemon trees were real and the lemons sour enough to make his eyes stream. In the daytime he left the great doors standing open behind him, so that Vesna need not batter them down, and he closed them only when he went up to bed. He found a gardener’s trowel left in one of the beds, which suggested there was a gardener, though he never saw one. Whoever tended the roses must have come while he was deep in a book.

Twice, from the lowest terrace, he looked up and saw a figure standing in the doorway high above, darker than the dark of the corridor behind it, at the place where the light ended. Both times, by the time he had shaded his eyes against the crystals, the doorway was empty.

\* \* \*

With the light came his wits, and with his wits came the old, restless itch to know things.

He had spent his whole life at court, which was to say he had spent it learning that the people who truly knew what went on in a palace were never the people on the thrones. They were the people who carried the trays. So on his fifth day in the garden, when the tea came up to the east wing in the hands of the young lycan footman who had once begged him not to tell the Sovereign about a dropped tray, Caleb asked him to sit down.

The boy looked at the chair as if it might bite him.

“Your Royal Highness Prince Caleb of Aloria is very kind,” he told a point somewhere above Caleb’s left shoulder, “but I’d better not.”

“Highness will do,” said Caleb. “And you had better. It is only a chair.”

The boy sat on the very edge of it, like a sparrow on the rim of a fountain, ready to be off at the first splash.

His name was Mirek. He was sixteen, all elbows and enormous ears, with a lycan’s thick dark hair that no comb had yet defeated, and he was, it emerged after some coaxing, Vesna’s youngest son. The whole family had come into the Sovereign’s household some months ago. Mirek could not say why, and plainly thought it someone else’s business to wonder. Lycans had guarded the Contis for generations, he explained, but a lycan family taken into the Sovereign’s own household was unheard of, and the old families on the Council had talked of nothing else for weeks.

“What else do the old families talk of?” Caleb asked, and poured the boy a cup of his own tea.

Mirek looked at the cup. Then for an instant his eyes went to the black iron on Caleb’s hand, and he turned scarlet to the tips of his ears and looked hastily at Caleb instead, and the last of his reserve gave way.

The treasury was in a bad way, the boy confided, and Consigliere Enzo had not slept in a month. The clerks were laying wagers on how long the Sovereign could hold the Council of Nine, which was to sit in three days. The Carusos had been seen in the lower city buying friends, and in Durnhollow, the clerks whispered, buying up the notes of the Conti debt. The Carusos, Caleb gathered, were the one house on the Council old enough to remember ruling Vesperia before the Contis, and bitter enough never to have forgiven it. They could not unseat the Sovereign, Mirek said, with the flat certainty of a boy repeating what every kitchen knew. No house on the Coast could stand against him, and the shadows in any room he entered leaned his way. So the Carusos meant to marry him instead. The shadows were how Vesperia chose its Sovereigns, the boy explained, when Caleb looked blank. When a Sovereign died, the heads of the great houses stood together in the great hall, and the shadows chose among them. For a thousand years the shadows had chosen the Contis. And the kitchens of Castel Nerezza, as the fortress was called, always knew what passed when the Council was sitting, because the blood-wine had to be decanted in the cellar below the chamber and carried up hot, and the old vent that ran up from the cellar through the rock carried every word spoken in the chamber down to whoever stood at the bottom of it.

“Every word?”

Mirek went pale, realising what he had said. “Highness —”

“I would never dream of eavesdropping,” promised Caleb, with perfect sincerity, and refilled the boy’s cup. “I only wondered. Do tell me more about the cellar.”

\* \* \*

The cellar was cold, and it smelled of old oak and iron and the dark, faintly coppery sweetness of blood-wine sleeping in its casks, and at the back of it, behind the last row of casks, a narrow crack in the rock ran up into the dark. Mirek left him there with a candle and an expression of profound unease, and Caleb set the candle on a cask and sat down on an upturned bucket in a coat that had cost more than the cellar, and listened.

The voices came down to him thin and hollowed by the stone, but clear.

Chairs scraped and settled. The chamber fell quiet, as a room does when a powerful man sits down in it, and down in the cellar, where no draught could reach, the flame of Caleb’s candle drew itself up tall and thin and bent towards the crack in the rock, as the lanterns on the bridges of Lutèce are said in the old tales to bend after a passing king. Caleb knew without being told that Arian had taken his seat. Then he heard Enzo, dry and exact, reading from what could only be the ledger.

It was a long report, and most of it was numbers, which Enzo read out in the patient, level voice of a tutor who has long since stopped expecting his pupils to listen. Caleb had sat through a thousand such reports at his father’s elbow and learned to listen through the numbers for whatever they had been built to hide. He listened now.

“— and in the matter of Aloria,” Enzo said, “the High Paladin has moved every banner of the Radiant Guard she can command without the King’s seal into the Marches. To protect the crystal roads, her dispatch says.”

A murmur went round the chamber, low and displeased.

“And the Arch-Chancellor,” Enzo went on, “has frozen every Vesperian holding in the counting-houses of the Citadel, pending a full review of irregularities in the crystal trade.”

The murmur rose. Someone struck the table. Someone else muttered *Solar arrogance*, and a third voice said that three short shipments were no reason to freeze a treasury, let alone put soldiers on the roads.

Caleb had stopped breathing.

He knew his eldest sister Matilda, High Paladin of the Radiant Guard, who had never once in her life moved a single soldier to protect a crystal road. She thought the crystal roads were Eleanor’s business, and said so, often and at volume. In all her years as High Paladin she had never gone to the very edge of what she might do without their father’s leave. And he knew Eleanor, his second sister. The Arch-Chancellor of Aloria would no more freeze a foreign treasury over three short shipments than she would wear odd gloves to a coronation. Over a matter of that size she would lodge a formal protest, in triplicate, with appendices.

Matilda was massing the Guard and Eleanor was squeezing the treasury for one reason only, and it had nothing whatever to do with crystals. They were shaking the tree as hard as they could to see what fell out of it, because they did not believe the pilgrimage.

On the long nights in the east wing he had wondered whether his sisters had known, whether Matilda had stood by the window with their father and let it happen, or Eleanor had read the decree with that tight line between her brows and held her tongue. He had not let himself finish the thought. Now he was spared it, because down a crack in the rock of a vampire’s fortress, in the dark, with a candle guttering on a cask of blood-wine, the answer came to him in the dry voice of the Consigliere of Vesperia.

His two eldest sisters had not known. And they were doing everything in their power to find him.

Caleb put his hand over his mouth. He had not wept when the chamberlain handed him the wine, nor on the sky-yacht, nor in three weeks of dark days in the east wing, lying awake and counting the stumbles of his own heart. He had been rather proud of that. Now, on an upturned bucket in a vampire’s cellar, it came all at once, and he could no more have stopped it than stopped the tide. He wept as he had not wept since he was a boy, silently and without the least dignity, his shoulders shaking and his hand pressed hard against his mouth so that no sound of it would carry up through the rock. For three weeks he had believed himself thrown away by everyone who had ever loved him. He had been wrong, and the relief of it hurt.

When it was over, he wiped his face on the lace of his cuff, which would never be the same, and did not care in the least.

\* \* \*

When he could listen again, a new voice had the floor.

It was an old voice, dry as a husk and deliberately unhurried, a voice that had never in its long life been interrupted and expected the same courtesy now. Mirek had described the Carusos to him. Caleb had no difficulty putting a face to it.

“The Consigliere reads us his numbers,” said Don Salvatore Caruso, “as if numbers were the trouble. The Alorians are not at our border over crystals. They are at our border because the Sovereign keeps their prince in the east wing and feeds him scones.”

Someone began to laugh, but thought better of it almost immediately.

“And what a prince,” the old voice went on. “The dead of the Sanctuary are not two months in the ground. Forty-one dead among the vines. And Donna Livia with them, who raised our Sovereign as her own from the night his mother died giving him life. The Consigliere’s own wife, cut down in her vineyard, and the ring of the man who did it found in her hand. Yet the Sovereign lodges him in comfort, and has put upon his hand a ring that no Conti has given in three generations, and asks this Council to bleed its treasury white for the privilege.”

In the cellar, Caleb had gone cold.

*The Consigliere’s wife.* One of the vampires killed had been the Consigliere’s wife.

He saw again the grey-suited vampire in the hall, staring at him with the colour gone out of his face, and the reluctance with which that man had opened the casket, and the way he had turned his face away when the ring went on. He remembered every airy, clever, unforgivable thing he had said in that man’s hearing, about chandlers and matching leads and exceptional vintages, while Enzo Conti stood across the hall with his wife not two months in the ground, and looked at the prince whose ring had been found in her hand.

*His* ring. The one he had dropped into Andrew’s palm at supper, laughing. The letter in the familiar hand came back to him then. He did not open it.

And Arian. *Who raised our Sovereign as her own.*

Caleb had thought that he understood his situation. He had thought himself the price of a debt, paid over without a word of explanation by a family that had not lifted a finger to keep him. He had not once, in all his weeks of sulking in magnificent chambers, asked himself whose blood the debt was for.

In the chamber above, no one had answered Don Salvatore. The silence stretched on.

Then Arian spoke.

“He did not kill her.”

Arian did not raise his voice. He never did, Caleb was beginning to understand. But the words came down through the rock like an anchor settling on the floor of the sea, and every other sound in the chamber stopped, and at the mouth of the crack a fine white rime crept out across the stone, as frost ferns across a nursery window.

“His ring says otherwise,” replied Don Salvatore, unperturbed.

Arian did not answer. At the mouth of the crack the rime spread another finger’s breadth over the stone, and stopped.

There was a pause, the pause of a patient man choosing which of several knives to use.

“As the Sovereign pleases,” said Don Salvatore at last, and Caleb could hear the smile in it. “The Council knows where its remedy lies. My granddaughter arrives within the week, as was discussed, with a dowry the Consigliere will find of the greatest interest, and the Alorians may count their crystals and go home.”

“Nothing was discussed.”

“Then it will be,” warned Don Salvatore pleasantly, “when the treasury is empty.”

Chairs scraped. The Council, it seemed, was over. Caleb heard the unhurried footsteps of the councillors leaving and the lighter footsteps of clerks, and the great doors of the chamber opening and closing, until at last there was nothing coming down the crack in the rock but the hollow sound of an empty room.

He was reaching for his candle when Arian spoke again, pitched low for Enzo alone.

“Is he eating?”

For a long moment nothing came down the rock at all, and the candle flame stood straight and still on its wick.

“Vesna says so, Sovereign.” Enzo’s voice was worn thin.

After that the rock was silent. Caleb held his left hand up to the candle and looked at the black iron on it. *A ring that no Conti has given in three generations.* It looked as it always had, a plain dark band with an empty setting, fit for a favourite hound, and it told him nothing, though it lay warm on his finger in the chill of the cellar, as warm as if someone had only just let go of his hand. He sat on his bucket in the cold with the candle burning down beside him.

\* \* \*

He did not go back to the east wing that night. He went to the garden, because it was the only place in the fortress that was his. The crystals did not know it was night. They blazed on overhead in their long spiral, and the water ran down the black stone talking quietly to itself, and the white roses breathed their scent out into the warm air as if no one had ever told them about the dark. He sat on the warm stone of the water-stair with his knees drawn up to his chin, and thought about his sisters.

He thought of Matilda first, because Matilda always came first. She was the eldest of them and the sternest, and the whole of the Radiant Guard stood straighter when she walked down a line. She had taught him the sword from the age of seven and had never once let him win. She had put him in the mud of the practice yard more times than either of them could count, and said *Again*, and would say nothing else until he rose. But when he was twelve and had broken his wrist on her guard, she had sat up with him the whole night through, and when she thought he was asleep she had laid her hand on his hair and left it there until dawn. He was the only creature living who could make the High Paladin laugh. He pictured her now in the saddle at the head of every banner she had dared to raise, in the cold of the Marches, with her jaw set the way it set when she had decided something and the world had better arrange itself accordingly.

Then Eleanor, who frightened grown ministers into stammering and had never been known to raise her voice. Eleanor was all polish and ice. She ran the treasury of a kingdom as other people ran a bath, and regarded most of the world as a sum that had come out slightly wrong. Yet she had spoiled him shamelessly from the day he was born. She had smoothed away his fines and his scandals, seated him beside the most amusing ambassador at every state dinner, and kept a tin of candied violets in her desk at the Chancellery that she swore were for visiting dignitaries, though no dignitary had ever been offered one. He could see her at that desk with ink on her second finger, freezing the treasury of a foreign power with one stroke of her pen and then going back, quite calmly, to her appendices.

And then he thought of Cecily. She was the youngest of his sisters and only three years older than he was, and from the day he could first toddle after her she had been his best friend in the whole world. When he was small and could not sleep, she had made him a draught that turned his bathwater gold, because he had said he wished to swim in sunlight. She would surely have shut herself in her laboratory the moment news of his pilgrimage came out, because she would not have believed it for a moment. Cecily had always known him best. Whatever she was making now, with her sleeves rolled up and her hair spilling out of its careless bun, she would not have slept since the day he was taken.

His sisters were looking for him.

And across the great hall, on the night he came, while he stood making jokes, a man whose wife had died at the Sanctuary had held out a casket and said nothing.

Caleb sat under the blazing crystals until the lamps in the corridor beyond the open doors had burned low, with his sisters’ love in one hand and the Consigliere’s grief in the other, and could put neither of them down.

\* \* \*

The next afternoon, Mirek came running into the garden.

Caleb heard him from the third terrace, a clatter of boots on the mezzanine and a cracked young voice shouting *Highness* down into the light, and he was up the iron stairs before he had quite decided to go. The boy nearly fell down them to meet him. His face was grey and his ears were flat against his head.

“Highness — it’s Paul — in the lower yard — they’ve —”

Caleb did not wait to hear what they had done.

He ran. He had not run since the Citadel, and the light he had stored in the garden carried him down through the black galleries faster than he would have believed, with Mirek pelting behind him and the violet lamps flicking past overhead. He heard the lower yard before he reached it, a low, rising noise like a pack that has run something to ground, and under it a sound that he did not at first recognise as a man trying to breathe.

The lower yard was a pit of black stone open to the sky, where the Coast’s violet twilight came down thin and cold and lay over everything alike, and it was ringed with the lycans of the Sovereign’s guard. There were a dozen of them at least, in Conti black, and two vampire sergeants leaning on the rail above with the bored interest of men at a dull play. In the middle of the circle stood the grey-flecked sentinel from the hall, breathing hard, with his claws out and wet to the knuckle.

Paul lay at his feet.

He lay on his side on the stone, as Caleb had never in his life seen him lie, and his livery was torn open across the ribs, and a dark pool was spreading beneath him. One of the lycans in the circle was laughing. Another said something in the growling tongue of the Wildmarch that Caleb did not know, but he knew the word *Aloria* in it, and he knew the tone. He had been hearing that tone since he was old enough to understand what it meant when people spoke of the Solar House behind their hands.

*The Alorians’ dog*, the laughing one said, in the common speech this time, for Caleb’s benefit. *Guarding what his masters threw away.*

Caleb walked into the circle.

He went through the gap between two lycans who towered over him, and neither of them thought to stop him until he was past. He crossed the stone to Paul and stood between him and the sentinel, and looked up at the sentinel, who was half a head taller than even the Sovereign, and let him see the face of a prince of the Solar House.

“I think,” Caleb said, “that will be quite enough for one afternoon, unless there is some part of this gentleman you have not yet had the pleasure of tearing open, in which case I am afraid you shall have to go through me to reach it, and I should warn you that I bruise exquisitely and will complain about it for weeks.”

The sentinel stared at him. So did the circle of lycans.

Caleb waited for someone to laugh. No one did. Their eyes had gone past his face to his left hand, which he had raised without thinking to hold the sentinel back, and to the black iron ring on his finger with the closed crescent of House Conti cut into its face.

The sentinel’s claws slid back into his fingers. He stepped away. One by one, without a word, the lycans of the circle fell back with him, and the two vampire sergeants straightened and drew back from the rail.

Caleb did not stop to wonder at it. He was already on his knees.

\* \* \*

It was bad. He had known it would be bad from the sound of Paul’s breathing, and it was worse. The claws had gone in under the ribs and dragged, and at least three of the ribs had broken under them, and one of the broken ends had gone somewhere it should never have gone. Every breath Paul took came with a wet, bubbling catch in it that Caleb had heard only twice before in his life, both times in the physicians’ halls of the Harp Vales, and both times the patient had died.

“Paul. Paul, look at me. Can you hear me?”

Paul’s eyes found him. They were lycan eyes, tawny and gold, and they were full of something Caleb did not have time to read.

“Highness.” It came out bubbling. “Don’t. Not — for me. You can’t —”

“I can, and I shall, and you may lodge your complaint with me afterwards in writing.” Caleb was already pulling back his cuffs. “Do be quiet now, there’s a good fellow. I am counting, and you know how cross I become when I lose my place.”

He laid both hands flat on Paul’s torn side, in the blood, and found the pulse beneath them, and counted.

The Star-Grace came when he called it. It came up out of him in a great golden rush, fuller and brighter than it had been since the Citadel, the whole of a week in the garden stored in his blood and waiting. He felt his veins light beneath his skin and his eyes begin to burn amber, and the dark stone yard went gold around him. Somewhere at the edge of it he heard a vampire sergeant hiss and stumble back from the glare.

He did not look up. He was inside the wound.

This was the part the temple physicians could never teach, only drill into a child until the child could do it without thinking. The healer went in with the light and felt the damage as the body felt it, every torn fibre and splintered edge, and then persuaded the body, gently and firmly, of what it had been before. He found the rib that had gone astray and coaxed it home, and knitted the ragged edges of the lung together, and felt the bubbling catch go out of Paul’s breath. He closed the long, dragging tears in the flesh from the inside out, layer by layer, as he had been taught, until his own hands lay on whole skin under the torn livery, and Paul’s heart beat stronger against his palms.

It would cost. He had known it would. In the Citadel, a healing this great would have cost him an afternoon’s rest. Here, under a sky that had never seen the sun, it cost him the whole of his week in the garden, and then it went on costing, and he let it, because Paul’s heart was not yet quite steady and he would not stop until it was.

When at last it was, he took his hands away.

Paul lay on the black stone and breathed. He breathed clean and deep, and put one hand to his own side where the wound had been, and felt the whole skin there, and his face broke open.

Caleb had seen Paul Walker bloodied, and exhausted, and once, memorably, drunk at a Highland wedding. He had never seen him weep. He wept now, lying on the stone of a vampire’s yard with his hand pressed to the place where a prince had given him back his life, and there was grief in his face as well as relief.

“There,” Caleb murmured, and his voice came out strange and far away. “You see, I told you I could. And now you owe me a very long conversation, Paul, and I warn you I intend to collect every word of it.”

He stood up, and that was when he learned what it had cost.

\* \* \*

The light did not stop.

He had taken his hands away, and the healing was finished, and the Star-Grace should have sunk back into him like a tide going out. Instead it went on rising. He felt it in his veins, running hot and bright and ungoverned, pouring up out of places in him he had not known it could reach. He looked down at his hands and saw the gold beneath the skin still blazing, and saw that it had begun to flicker, like a candle flame in a draught.

He knew what this was. It had been in the primer, on the page after the one about the Blight, in the same serene and scholarly hand. *A healer who spends beyond his store where the sun cannot refill it may find the Grace will not be recalled. Having nothing left to spend, it spends the healer.*

He had read it on his nineteenth day in the east wing, and closed the book, and put it from his mind, because he had not expected to live long enough for it to matter.

“Highness?” called Mirek, from very far away.

“I am perfectly well,” said Caleb, and turned towards the arch that led back into the fortress, because the garden was that way and some unreasoning part of him still believed the garden would mend everything. It would not, and he knew it. The garden could fill him with light, but it could do nothing for light that would not stop pouring out of him, and still his legs carried him on, and he let them.

He made it through the arch and into the black gallery beyond, and a little way along it. Then the gallery tilted, and the lamps swung, and he put out a hand to the wall and missed it, and the floor came up to meet him with great courtesy and no hurry at all.

He lay on the cold volcanic glass and burned.

The fire was inside him. It was heat more than pain, a terrible, climbing heat, as if every lamp in the Citadel had been lit at once inside his ribs. His heart had begun to gallop. Then it began to stumble, the way it had stumbled in the east wing at the end of each dark day, only far worse, missing and catching and missing again. The gold along his veins flared and guttered and flared. He could see it lighting the glass walls around him, throwing wild shadows up the gallery, and some distant, orderly part of his mind that would always be a temple student observed that this was very likely how it ended, and that the primer had not mentioned how bright it would be.

The shadows in the gallery moved.

They bent against his light, all of them together, leaning down the gallery towards something that was coming, like grass before a wind, and the air went cold, and Caleb turned his head on the glass and saw Arian.

Nothing living had a right to move so fast. One moment he was a darkness at the far end of the gallery and the next he was on his knees on the glass, and the cold of him rolled over Caleb like a wave over a burning ship. His face was white as bone. And his eyes had a rim of crimson burning around the black.

“Look at me.”

“I am afraid,” said Caleb, with an enormous effort to maintain his dignity, “that I am rather occupied at present, Sovereign, and shall have to ask you to call again at some more convenient hour.”

Arian did not seem to hear him. He had taken Caleb’s face in both hands, and he was looking into it, at the amber blaze of the eyes and the gold running wild under the skin, and whatever he saw there took the last of the colour from his own.

Then his gaze dropped to Caleb’s throat, and Caleb, despite the fever clouding his mind, immediately understood what the vampire intended.

“No,” he found himself crying out, “no, don’t you *dare* —”

When he tried to push himself up, Arian caught both his wrists in one hand and pressed them to the glass above his head without roughness and without the least effort, as though Caleb weighed nothing at all. His other hand slid beneath Caleb’s head and tipped it back and to the side, baring the throat. Caleb twisted against the hold, but could not shift it by so much as a hair’s breadth.

Even through the fever, he saw it all at once, or thought he did. So this was what the garden had been for. Every white rose and every blazing crystal and every scone that a lycan cook had been taught to bake had been husbandry, and he had flourished under it all week like a prize lamb in a paddock, and had even begun, heaven help him, to wonder what kind of man did such things.

He looked up into that bone-white face with its rim of crimson, and he was terribly afraid.

Arian tilted his head and bit down.

\* \* \*

There were two points of cold, like splinters of ice, and a sharp, bright pain that made him cry out. His whole body arched against the hold and was held. Then the cold began to spread.

It spread out from his throat through his veins, slowly, like snow falling on a fire. Everywhere it went, the terrible climbing heat went quiet under it. He felt the wild gold in his veins falter and sink, felt it drawn down and away and out of him, the way the iron spires on the forge-halls of the Deep Holds draw the storm out of the sky and let it run harmless into the mountain. His heart missed, and caught, and missed. Then, under the cold, it caught again and held.

He felt Arian drink.

He felt it as a pulling, deep and rhythmic, drawing the light out of him along with the blood, and at first there was nothing in it but fear and the old, black, childhood horror of every story the temple tutors had ever told. This was what they did. This was what they *were*. He was pinned on the floor of a vampire’s fortress with a vampire at his throat, and every Alorian nightmare was true.

And the fire was going out of him. His body, the traitor, knew it before he did, and went slack with relief in the arms of the creature drinking from it. The shame of that was hotter than the fever had ever been. Something wet slid from the corner of his eye and into his hair, and he could not lift a hand to stop it.

Then something else came through the bite, from the other side.

It came the way a sound comes through a wall, muffled and enormous, and all at once it was simply in him, and seemed to have been there always. It was a loneliness so old and so vast that he could not find the edges of it. It was like a great dark house with every room shut up and the shutters closed, and someone walking its corridors for years upon years, patiently opening one door after another, and finding every room empty, and going on to the next.

It went through him so deep that for a moment he forgot to be afraid, and for that moment, pinned and bleeding and humiliated beyond words, some foolish, incorrigible part of him wanted only to find whoever walked that dark house, and take him by the hand, and tell him that he might stop looking now, and rest.

And then he felt the hands.

The hand that pinned his wrists had not tightened. The hand under his head was cradling it off the glass, and it was shaking. It shook the way Caleb’s own hands had shaken on the sky-yacht, finely and ceaselessly, the tremor of someone holding himself together by main force. Against his skin, so low it was scarcely a sound, Arian was saying something in Vesperian. It was a single word, over and over, and it had the sound of a door being barred against a storm. Then Caleb felt him shudder, once, hard, and heard him make a sound, low and cut off, that was very like pain.

The light. Arian was drinking the light, and the light was burning him.

Before Caleb could make sense of that, it was over.

Arian lifted his head. It was the slowest movement Caleb had ever seen him make, and the hardest, as if the throat itself held him and he were tearing free of it by inches. His jaw was clenched. The crimson around his eyes blazed, and then, as Caleb watched, it narrowed and dimmed and went out, until the eyes were only black again.

There was blood at the corner of his mouth, and it was glowing. It was faint but unmistakable, a soft gold light like a lamp turned low, shining on the lips of the Sovereign of Vesperia in the dark.

He let go of Caleb’s wrists.

He touched two fingers to the place on Caleb’s throat where he had bitten, and Caleb felt a coolness there, a fine, dark tingling, like shadow poured into a wound. When the fingers lifted, the stinging had gone. But Arian’s thumb stayed, resting lightly on the side of Caleb’s throat, over the pulse, and did not move.

Caleb lay on the glass and breathed.

The heat was gone. The gold under his skin had sunk to a faint, steady shimmer, the ordinary light of an elf who had worked too hard and would need to rest. His heart was beating slow and even under Arian’s thumb. He was alive. He was, as far as he could tell, going to go on being alive, and he was so tired he could scarcely think, and every part of him that could still feel anything smarted with humiliation.

He could feel the tear drying at his temple, and Arian was looking at it. For one unguarded moment the Sovereign of Vesperia looked like a man who had been struck. Then the moment closed over, and Caleb could not afterwards have sworn that he had seen it.

“Well,” Caleb said, when he could. His voice came out in a thread. “If this was the price of the garden, Sovereign, you might have sent me the bill like any civilised creditor, instead of collecting it on the floor of a corridor in front of the servants.”

“You were burning.”

“I was managing.”

“You were dying.”

It was said without heat, as flatly as Arian said everything. But the thumb on Caleb’s pulse pressed a little harder, as if to be sure of it.

Caleb turned his head away, towards the glass wall, so that he would not have to look at that face. “You have not apologised,” he said to the wall.

“No.”

“Do you mean to?”

“No.”

Neither of them spoke. Then Arian said, in the same level voice, “I would do it again.”

Caleb believed him. That was the worst of it. He shut his eyes.

\* \* \*

He heard running footsteps, and a voice he knew.

“Sovereign —” Enzo stopped dead. Then Caleb heard the Consigliere of Vesperia draw a long, slow breath.

“Arian.” His voice had gone strange. “That is *elven* blood.”

Arian said nothing.

“It should have killed you.”

“It did not.”

The silence went on long enough for Caleb to count his own heart through it, steady now under the cold thumb.

“The Accord forbids it. If the Council learns —”

“I know what the Accord says, Consigliere.”

Caleb opened his eyes. Above him, Arian had stayed where he was. He was still kneeling on the glass with his thumb resting on Caleb’s throat, but he was looking past Enzo, back down the gallery, towards the arch that led to the lower yard.

Paul stood in it. He was on his feet, one hand braced against the stone, his torn livery hanging from him, and he was staring at the two of them with a face like chalk.

Arian looked at him for a long moment. Paul looked back, and then he looked at the floor.

Then Arian gathered Caleb up off the glass as easily as a folded cloak, and rose.

Caleb had neither the strength to protest nor, to his lasting shame, the will. He lay almost bonelessly against the cold silk of Arian’s coat with his head on Arian’s shoulder and watched the violet lamps go by overhead, while the shadows of the galleries drew back against the walls to let them pass, like a household standing aside on a stair, and was carried back up through the black galleries of Castel Nerezza like a child being taken up to bed. Arian’s arms were steady and firm as they held him. But under Caleb’s cheek, faint and slow and utterly impossible, something was beating in the Sovereign’s chest.

He was too exhausted to make sense of it. He only lay still against it, and counted the beats.

The great doors of the garden stood open, as Caleb had left them, and the golden light lay rolled out across the threshold. Arian stopped at the edge of it.

“Put me down, if you please,” protested Caleb. “I shall walk into my own garden on my own two feet, since I have been carried quite far enough for one day.”

For a moment he thought he would be refused. Then Arian bent, and set him on his feet at the very edge of the light, and held him until he was sure of his balance. His hand stayed at the small of Caleb’s back for a heartbeat longer than it needed to. Then it was gone.

Caleb walked into the light.

He managed the mezzanine, and the first flight of the iron stairs, and a few steps out onto the first terrace among the golden lilies, before his knees gave way and he sat down in the warm earth, rather suddenly, with all the dignity he had left. The light poured over him. He could feel it sinking into the hollow places where the fire had been, filling them slowly, the way warmth comes back into cold hands.

He looked back up at the doors.

Arian stood on the threshold where the light ended, as he always did. He did not come in. He stood there with the shadow of the corridor at his back and the gold of the crystals lying across the iron at his feet, and the only light on him was the faint glow of Caleb’s blood, still shining at the corner of his mouth.

He watched until Caleb lay back among the lilies. Then he drew the great doors closed, and the garden was full of light, and Caleb was alone in it, with two cool, healed marks on his throat and no idea at all what any of it meant.

\* \* \*

Outside the doors, in the dark, Paul Walker sat down with his back against the iron.

The Sovereign had gone. The Consigliere had gone, and the lycan boy, and the corridor was empty. Under the great doors the garden’s light came through in a narrow gold line and lay along the black stone beside his boot, the only light in all that dark. There was no one to see him take the sliver of crystal from the lining of his boot, where he had kept it since the Citadel. It was a paired stone, such as the Radiant Guard carried in the field, and it would carry a voice to its twin and nowhere else. Its twin lay in a locked drawer in the Citadel, in the rooms of the Crown Prince.

He held it for a long time without speaking into it. His side, where the prince’s hands had been, was still warm.

He saw the prince on his knees in the lower yard with both hands in the blood, telling him to be quiet because he was counting. Then he thought of his mother and his sisters in the Highland March, and of what he had been told would happen to them if the drawer in the Citadel ever stayed silent.

So he told the crystal everything, in a low, steady voice, as a soldier makes his report. He spoke of the fight and the healing and the gallery, and last of all he told it that the Sovereign of Vesperia had fed on His Highness Prince Caleb, and that His Highness lived.

The crystal glowed once, faintly, and went dark. Paul put it back in his boot. Then he sat on, with his back against the doors of the garden, keeping guard over the prince he had just betrayed to the Crown Prince, and did not sleep.
