## Chapter 18: Chapter Seventeen - Taken

*Speak into thy two hands, child, and close them over, for the wind is a carrier and will set thy words down at any door it likes. What is held close goes up to the moon. The moon keeps all she is given, and tells it to no one, and never once asks why.*

— From *The Hearth Prayers of the Highland March*, as they are taught at Loch Aird

\* \* \*

The frost on the canopy kept its gold until a little before the second bell.

Caleb lay on his back under the grey curtains and watched it go. The stars he had made in the night paled one grain at a time, each giving up its borrowed gold as the coals in a grate give up their red when there is nobody left in the room to stir them, until the canopy hung over him white and fine and faintly blue again, like the underside of a cloud on the high passes before snow. One grain at the very centre held out longer than the rest. He found that he was willing it on, and let it go, and it went.

The chair by the window was not empty.

It had the look of a chair put into a room for the room’s sake, to keep the chest company, and never asked to hold anybody. Arian sat in it in the black coat he had thrown round himself to answer the door, with his bare feet on the stone and his forearms on his knees, watching Caleb as he had watched the garden from the sill.

“How long have you been sitting there?” asked Caleb.

“Since my uncle went.”

“You might have come back to bed.”

“You were asleep.” Arian’s head tilted, a little. “I wished to see what you did in mine.”

He had seen Caleb asleep on a chaise, and on the seat of a carriage, and on the fleeces in a sheep cave, and in the lilies with his hands in the water. Caleb had never supposed that anybody was keeping a list.

“And what do I do?”

“You take all of the coverlet.”

Caleb looked down. He had it to his chin in both fists and a good yard of it besides wound about his knees, and on the far side of the bed the black sheet lay bare under the frost with the dent of a head still in the pillow. He let go of it with as much dignity as a man can let go of a coverlet, which is not a great deal.

“In Aloria,” he protested, “coverlets are made to be shared. They are as thin as a page, because nobody is ever cold. Yours is the thickness of a rampart. I was only defending it.”

The corner of Arian’s mouth moved. Nothing else in his face did, and Caleb, who had made a long study of that corner since a night under a canopy of frost, added it to his collection and was pleased with himself for the rest of the morning.

“I owe you a dawn,” he promised.

“There was no dawn.”

“There was. I made it myself, which I grant is not quite in the spirit of the thing.” He folded his hands on the coverlet, as he had once folded them on a little book at a long table in a shuttered waystation, and spoke to the canopy in his reading voice. “*The sun rose this morning a little before the second bell, in the canopy of the Sovereign’s bed, where nobody had thought to look for it. It came up gold, and then white at the edges, like the rind of a lemon held up to a lamp. It did not rise so much as stay where it had been put, which I am told is unusual in a sun. The Sovereign did not see it. The Sovereign was looking at something else.*”

There was a silence from the chair.

“What colour was the last?”

“Gold. It held out longest. I was rather proud of it.”

“Write it down.”

“I have no pencil. I have, as far as I can make out, no clothes. I came up your stairs in a shirt, and the shirt has gone somewhere in the night, and I do not feel equal to asking you where.”

Arian rose and took a coat from the chest and laid it over the foot of the bed. It was dove grey.

“Your uncle’s,” said Caleb.

“He has asked for it back.”

Caleb put it on, there being nothing else. It came down past his hands and smelled faintly of old paper and of the cold, and he sat up in the Sovereign’s bed in the Consigliere’s coat with the sleeves turned back twice, and felt like a boy who has got into his grandfather’s study in the night and is about to be found there.

\* \* \*

Arian dressed at the window, turned from the bed, quickly, as a man dresses who has done it in the dark every day of his life with nobody to watch, and Caleb watched him. The black silk went on over shoulders where a faint gold still lay in the hollows from the night, as pollen lies in the throat of a lily after the bee has gone about its business. The jet buttons went up to the throat. Then the rings, two of gold, onto the right hand, and his thumb went to the bare third finger beside them, and stopped.

Caleb’s own hand had gone to his chest. It found the Consigliere’s lapel and nothing under it, and he took it down again.

“What will they ask you?”

“Whether I did it.” Arian fastened his cuff. “The lord of the inland vineyards is to be brought up from the cellar to ask. My uncle says it will be instructive.”

“For whom?”

“He did not say.”

“I am coming with you,” insisted Caleb. “They are going to sit in a row above a wine cellar and discuss my throat. I think I have some claim to be present.”

“They did not ask for the prince.”

“They never ask. They send cards. Do you want me there?”

Arian turned from the window. His eyes were as black as the stone that ought to have been on his hand and gave back as little, but Caleb had been leaning over them for eleven weeks, and he saw the yes at the bottom of them a moment before it came.

“Yes.”

Caleb waited.

“Do not come.”

Caleb had stood up once already before the Nine with his hands shaking, and Arian had said afterwards, *You should not have stood up*, and meant it, as he meant this, with the yes lying under it as the garden lay under the mountain. Caleb held the two side by side, the yes and the correct thing, and found that he could not take one without the other.

“Then I shall be in the garden,” he answered, which was nearly true.

\* \* \*

Two blows came at the outer door of the study, and then a third, lower down, with the side of a boot.

“She will have heard where I slept,” sighed Caleb.

“Before you did.”

Vesna came the length of the study with something long over her arm in a linen sheet, as a woman carries a wedding gown across a yard in the rain, and stopped in the doorway of the bedchamber. She looked at the plain black bed and the frost on the canopy, and at the Sovereign of the Obsidian Coast buttoned to the throat by his window, and last of all at Caleb in the Consigliere’s coat.

Her mouth went into a line. It was not the line it made for a burnt scone.

“His Highness,” she said, “came up those stairs in his shirt.”

“His Highness,” said Caleb, “had very little say in the matter.”

“And he’ll not go down them in the Consigliere’s coat. The Consigliere has had that coat since before my mother was born, and he’ll know if there’s a crease in it.” She shook the sheet off what she carried. “The girls sat up two nights with this. I told them two days, and then they heard His Highness was awake and they’d not wait for anything. So it’s a day early, and if the hem’s wrong I’ll hear about it at every meal till the Eclipse.”

It was black, and cut as the other had been, close and high at the throat in the way of the Coast, with a row of jet buttons to the waist, and over the left breast the closed crescent of House Conti stood up from the silk in black thread so thick it might have been carved on a door. But the linen room had learned something from the last one. Every seam had been let out a hair’s breadth and bound inside with grey, so that whatever came up in him might come through as it pleased, in its own lines, and not scorch on its way out. And on the right side, low down, there was a pocket.

“For the violets,” said Vesna, before he could ask. “His Highness keeps things.”

She held it up by the shoulders, and Arian, who had seen a great deal more of Caleb than that in the night, turned to the window all the same, and Caleb loved him for it, and Vesna put him into the coat as she might have put a boy of six into his feast day best.

It was heavier on the left.

It was the weight of a crown piece, a little more, lying over his heart between the silk and its lining, and in the middle of the black silk’s cold, which had not yet made up its mind about him, it was colder. His hand went up of itself and lay flat on the crescent, and under the thick black thread he felt the stitches that held it.

They were not his. His own were close and square, a physician’s stitches, learned on orange peel. These were smaller and closer, as even as the stitches round the hem of a naming day gown, and he would have known whose they were in the dark.

Vesna settled his collar, as she had settled it on the night the carriages left for Durnhollow, with her warm rough fingers at his jaw. Then her thumb went once along the edge of the crescent, and came away, and her face did nothing at all.

She said nothing about it whatever, which from Vesna was an oration.

“There,” she said, to the collar.

In the plain room the shadows had crept from the corners, as they did each morning for their master, and this morning they had gone to the wrong man. They lay along the stone in a row with their points to the breast of his coat, quite still, like a household gathered at a window to watch a carriage come up the drive.

“They like it,” murmured Arian.

“They’ve more sense than some I could name.” Vesna folded the Consigliere’s coat over her arm, sleeves in, as if it might otherwise make a run for it. “There’s scones on the study table. His Highness will eat them before he does anything else he’s not been asked to do.”

\* \* \*

He ate them standing, by the fire that roared up the chimney for hands that were warm this morning without it. There were three, and they were very good.

The second bell went over, somewhere in the rock.

Arian lifted the cloak from the arm of the gold chair, where it had lain for twelve nights, and stood holding it in both hands. It was so little in his hands. It was a child’s cloak of grey wool stitched with silver, with a hem darned by a woman who had been dead since the spring, and a collar that would not come clean, and the gold sunburst at its neck woke a little as Caleb came near.

“Will you take it down?”

“No. Keep it for me.”

“It is yours.”

“I know. You put it into my hands. You were told to, and you did. Now I am putting it into yours. You are a great deal better at keeping things than I am. You kept that one twenty years and never lost the label.”

“Until when?”

“Until I want it.”

The fire cracked. All down the study the candles stood still in their dishes and leaned neither way, as if nobody had yet told them which way to lean.

“Yes.” Arian laid it over the arm of the gold chair again, with the sunburst turned up.

At the study door he stopped. Beyond it the corridor was violet and cold, and the Sovereign’s stair went down out of it into the dark of the fortress, towards a hall where nine chairs waited over a cellar of wine.

“May I?” he asked.

“You are going to ask every time.”

“Yes.”

“Then yes. Every time. I told you so.” Caleb came to him across the rug. “Go and be honest at them. You are so good at it, and it frightens them half to death.”

It was brief. Arian’s hand came to the small of his back as it had come in the great hall on the night of the betrothal, before the whole of Vesperia, and his mouth was cold and then not, and his heart came down once under the flat of Caleb’s hand, hard and sure, as a seal comes down on warm wax, and the frost on his lashes caught a little of the gold that came up in Caleb to meet it, and kept it. Then he went down the stair, and the dark of the corridor went down the steps after him like the long hem of a court mantle, every shadow in it except the few that stayed on the rug, still pointing at the breast of Caleb’s coat.

\* \* \*

Out on the landing the fortress came back at once, as the sea comes back round a rock when the wave has gone off it. The violet was thin and cold, and the ash went past a high slit of window in a long grey hush, and somewhere far below in the rock a door shut, and the sound came up the stair and went on up past him into the dark as if it had somewhere to be.

Paul was sitting on the Sovereign’s stair, three steps down, against the wall, with his hands lying open on his knees, palms up, as if somebody had lately taken something out of them.

He got up slowly when he heard Caleb on the landing. There was ash on the shoulders of his livery and on his boots, a grey bloom of it, and in the creases of his knuckles, as if he had been out in it a long while.

“You have been out.”

“Up at the crown, Highness. With Tomas.” Paul rubbed his hands on his coat, and the ash stayed in the creases. “The glass wants sweeping every third day, or the garden’d be dark by the week’s end. He goes up the masons’ stair at the first bell. I’ve been giving him a hand. Since the hearthstone.”

Caleb had not known there was a stair to the crown at all, nor that the ash came down on the dome of his garden every hour of every day and was swept off it by a lycan who did not speak, so that the light should come in.

“You have not slept.”

“No, Highness.”

“Nor have I, a great deal. We shall be dull company for each other. I am going down to the cellar. The one under the great hall, with the vent. I am not going to eavesdrop. I am only going to sit on a bucket in a cold room, and if anything happens to come down through the rock while I am sitting there, I cannot be held to account for the acoustics.”

Something went across Paul’s face that might, in a better year, have been a laugh, and he stood aside on the stair to let Caleb go first, as he always had.

\* \* \*

The cellar had no master now.

The old one had gone off the mountain the night of the hearthstone, still in his apron, after ninety-one years at the foot of the vent, and nobody had been put in his place. Caleb took a candle from the niche at the stair head and lit it at the lamp there, and went down. The lamps on the cellar stair had not been trimmed since the potboy went, and stood in their niches drowned in their own oil. The casks lay in their long rows as they had lain, black oak hooped in iron, and the smell of blood-wine sleeping in them came up the stair as it had come up in his third week on the Coast, old oak and iron and that dark coppery sweetness, staler now, as a house smells when nobody has opened its shutters for a week.

On the head of the nearest cask the old master’s chalk still stood in a long tally of fives, and the last five stopped at three, as if somebody had called him away in the middle of a count. Under the tap of the end cask, which nobody had come down to turn tight, a dark drop gathered and fell and gathered again, and the flags beneath it had drunk so many that they had gone black in a ring the size of a plate, and shone.

The bucket was where he had left it.

It stood upturned at the foot of the crack, a little to one side, as he had pushed it with his heel when he rose to go, and nobody had moved it. There was a stool beside it now that had not been there then. Above the stool a ring of new iron had been sunk into the old rock, with a length of chain run through it that lay loose on the flags with the frost still in its links, as a dog’s chain lies across a yard while the dog is out on business. On the cask next to the stool there was a ring of grey tallow where a candle had burned down to nothing, and a scatter of black sand where someone had sanded a card and not troubled to brush it away. In the sand, drawn with one fingertip, idly, as a man draws on a frosted pane while he waits to be called in, there was a single long-throated flower.

Caleb stood and looked down at it.

Then he set his candle down beside the ring of tallow, and sat on the bucket in a black silk coat worth rather more than the cellar, with his hands round his knees. Paul did not sit. He stood among the casks with his back to one of them and his arms folded, and the candle laid his shadow long across the flags.

Above them, through the rock, the Nine were coming in.

\* \* \*

Chairs scraped and settled. The voices came down the crack thinned by the stone, as they had come before, and then they stopped, as a room stops when a man sits down in it whom nobody can afford to interrupt.

The candle on the cask leaned.

The first time, a candle in this cellar had bent towards the crack, as the lanterns on the bridges of Lutèce bend after a passing king. This time it bent its flame over the black sand and the drawn flower and laid it down sideways in the air towards the bucket and the man on it, and held it there. A slow warmth went up the back of Caleb’s neck. The Sovereign of the Obsidian Coast had taken his seat in the hall above and heard a heart come down the cellar stair under his feet that he knew better than his own, and was not in the least surprised.

He covered his eyes with his hand. Among the casks, Paul shifted his weight.

“The Council sits upon the breach of the Accord, Article the Sixth,” said Enzo’s voice, dry and level. “The lord of the inland vineyards will put the questions, being of the Nine until the Nine say otherwise. The Consigliere has found no law against it.” A paper turned. “He has looked.”

“Sovereign,” said a second voice.

Caleb had heard it last at a long table under lowered lamps, above a coat of mulberry velvet. It was an ancient voice in a face that was not, pleasant and unhurried, and it came down through the rock as a draught comes in under a door. His hand closed on his knee.

“Did you drink from the throat of His Highness the Prince of Aloria, held under the Accord?”

“Yes,” said Arian.

“In the black gallery. By force. While he was held.”

“Yes.”

A murmur ran round the chamber and came down the rock as a murmur comes down a chimney, and died.

“And has the Sovereign drunk from him since?”

The candle on the cask stood up straight, tall and thin, as candles stand in a room where somebody has stopped breathing, and among the casks Caleb heard Paul’s breath go in and not come out.

“Yes.”

The chamber went up as a market goes up when a cart overturns in it. Voices went over each other in the rock, and a chair went over, and someone struck the table twice with the flat of a hand.

Then the cold came down.

It came through the rock as frost comes through the wall of a farmhouse in a hard winter, until there is a white fur on the inside of the stone, and in the cellar Caleb’s breath went white in front of his face, and above him the voices stopped, as the rooks in an elm go quiet one by one when the shadow of something with longer wings goes over the field. Into the quiet, unhurried, came the ancient voice.

“When?”

“Last night.”

Caleb’s hand came down from his eyes and went over his mouth instead.

“By force?”

“No.”

“Then by what means, Sovereign?”

“I asked him,” answered Arian. “He said yes.”

There was a silence in the chamber of the kind that falls when nine people are each waiting for one of the others to find something to say. Caleb sat on his bucket with the gold coming up under his skin so fast that the candle beside him went pale as a candle does in a sunny window, and could not have said whether he wished to laugh or to go up through the rock and stand beside him.

“Did he,” said the lord of the inland vineyards at last. It was not a question. “And will the prince say so himself?”

“He has, my lord. Before the Holds, with the iron in the fire.” Arian’s voice stayed where it was, level and cold. “They wrote it down.”

“And what did the Holds write?”

“*Emphatically*,” said Arian.

Somebody in the chamber laughed, once, and stopped as if a hand had been laid over his mouth. In the cellar Caleb bent forward over his knees and laughed without a sound, as he had once wept on the same bucket without one, until his eyes ran and the gold went down his wrists in two bright lines and lit the flags between his boots. Behind him among the casks Paul made a noise that was not a laugh at all.

Caleb did not turn round. He let him have it in the dark without being looked at.

\* \* \*

“The Sovereign is admirably honest,” said Don Salvatore.

His voice came down the rock like warm oil poured on a stone. Caleb had last heard it at the gala, telling Eleanor what was in her own warrant before she had said a word of it, and it was as kind now as it had been then.

“It is the most charming thing about the Contis.” A ring clicked once against the arm of a chair. “I will not trouble the Nine about the breach. I wish only to say, since it touches my house, that the Holds have written to the Ashward, with great civility. A client of the Seventh Chamber went down two nights ago to a place under Dornhöhle which does not exist, and took away from under our mark a thing which was never there.” The voice smiled. “The Ashward will not trouble the Council about a thing which was never there. We only rejoice to hear that His Highness is so much recovered.”

“Yes.”

It was the same yes he had given the Nine three times already, and it was the coldest thing Caleb had ever heard come down through rock. The ring of tallow on the cask went white with frost from the edge inwards, and the black sand in it, and the flower drawn in the sand, and the candle flame shrank down into its cup as if it wished to be somewhere else. The iron hoop of the cask ticked once as the cold took it, like a clock in a house where everybody has gone to bed, and the chain on the flags went white from link to link, as if it had been drawn through a barrel of salt.

Caleb sat still.

He thought of a phial of white glass turned over in Vesna’s fist in the lilies, with a black thorn stamped in the lead of its stopper, and of a man in a wine cask over the Marches with silver going into his palms. And he thought of the Don upstairs in his garnet silk, whose house had poisoned him with one hand and locked the cure in the iron under Dornhöhle with the other, sitting above a cellar with his hands folded, rejoicing.

He had never wished to hurt anybody. He found that he could imagine it now, brightly, and he set the imagining down on the flags beside the bucket and left it there, because another voice was speaking.

It was a woman’s, low and careful and old, and Caleb did not know it.

“Sovereign. In a thousand years no child of the Umbral houses has drunk from an elf and lived to be asked about it. The Sovereign has done it twice. How?”

Nothing came down the rock. The silence in the hall above went on so long that the frost round the tallow ring crept out over the oak of the cask, and Caleb sat and watched it come.

“The Nine ask it,” insisted the woman.

“The Nine may ask,” said Arian. “It is mine.”

Under the floor of the great hall Caleb laid his hand flat on his own chest, over the crescent, and felt his heart go on under it, steady, and knew that it was going on in another place too, beat for beat, in a chest the Nine were sitting round in a row and asking about, and that it would not tell them.

\* \* \*

“Then I will ask the Nine something,” said the lord of the inland vineyards.

A chair creaked, gently, and Caleb knew what he would ask before he asked it. Two decrees of the Sovereign lay upon the table of the Nine. One had closed the harbour quarter, sealed in a cave in the Fault, and the other was to have put the Ashward off the mountain, and the Ashward had not gone. Neither bore a device, because the Sovereign had none to give them, and all Lowmere knew on what terms. Any one of the Nine might contest a decree with no device on it, and ask the shadows to settle it.

“The Sovereign has broken the Accord twice and tells us so.” The ancient voice settled over the chamber like a cloth over a table. “And on the night of the gala the shadows of this hall leaned two ways. I ask that the Nine stand in this hall and ask the shadows.”

Nobody spoke. The candle stood in its cup without a flicker, and down the rows of casks the dark did not move at all.

“Not now,” said Don Salvatore.

He said it kindly. “Not today, cousin. Nobody must ever be able to say that the Nine were hasty with a Conti.” The ring clicked against the chair. “Let it be asked at an hour no one can quarrel with. On the day of the Long Eclipse the Accord is renewed, and the season of the Solar Throne’s inquiry ends. And on that day, as the Holds have been so good as to tell the whole of Lowmere, the Sovereign’s ring is either given back to him or sold. If he stands before the Nine at the hour of the Eclipse with the ring of his house upon his hand, the Nine will have nothing to ask the shadows. If he does not —”

He left it there for the Nine to finish.

“The Holds keep such tidy books. Let the Nine keep theirs as tidy.”

“A living Sovereign,” said Enzo, “cannot be unseated.”

“Nobody here would dream of it, Consigliere. That would be treason, and the Ashward has never been treasonous in its life.” The voice was all gentleness. “We do not ask the shadows who is Sovereign. We ask only whether his word is to be obeyed. A Sovereign may sit in his high seat until the mountain falls into the sea, and nobody need ever obey him again.”

Caleb had been eleven weeks on the Coast, and been poisoned on it once already in his own chair, and he had learned what the Coast did with a man in a high seat whom nobody need obey. It left him the seat. It sent him oranges, and waited, as it had waited once for another Conti, at another Eclipse, and wept at his funeral afterwards, handsomely.

“The steward will call it,” said the lord of the inland vineyards.

The staff struck the floor three times, and the steward of the hall said, in a voice that went into the rock and came out at the bottom heavier than it had gone in, *Let those of the Nine stand who would have the shadows asked, at the hour of the Long Eclipse, in this hall.*

Then Caleb heard the chairs.

He counted them as he had once counted pence on his fingers at a long scarred table with his feet not reaching the floor. One, and that was the mulberry velvet, unhurried. Two, almost with it, as a second bell follows the first. Three, after a breath, and four. A long silence then, so long that he began to let his breath go, and a fifth chair went back on the glass with a short hard scrape, as if whoever was in it had made up his mind only at the last and wished to have it done.

Five. Then nothing, though he waited.

Along the rows of casks the dark had drawn in about the bucket while he counted, as a household draws in round the kitchen hearth when somebody knocks at the door at night and nobody wishes to be the one to answer it, and it lay close round his boots, with its points to the breast of his coat.

He did not hear Don Salvatore’s chair. Don Salvatore had never been seen to stand for anything that somebody else could be made to stand for in his place.

“Five,” said the steward of the hall.

“Then it is asked,” said Arian.

He said it without anger and without haste, in the voice in which he had once told Caleb that the garden was his. In the cellar the candle flame lay down without going out, flat along the top of its wick towards the bucket, as a banner lies out from its pole in a gale, and stayed so, burning, as if it were holding on to something.

“I have never been afraid of my own shadows, my lord.”

“No one has ever supposed so, Sovereign,” murmured Don Salvatore. “That is the beauty of asking them.”

\* \* \*

There was more, and it went past Caleb like water past a stone. He sat on the bucket with his hand flat on the crescent, and under it the ring lay against his heart, cold, the only thing in the cellar that was not moving.

He saw it as plainly as if Eleanor had ruled it out for him on Chancellery cream, the inquiry and the Accord and the ring and the Leaning, every column of it adding up to one dark morning. And the Sovereign would walk up to that day with nothing on the third finger of his right hand, because what ought to be there lay against the heart of the one person in the world who could have told him so on any night since Dornhöhle, and had not.

*Not in the dark*, he had thought, in the bed upstairs with the bare finger against his mouth, *as a thing to be thanked for in bed, like a bottle brought up from the cellar.*

He was in the cellar now.

He could go up the black glass between the standing chairs and put it into the Sovereign’s hand before them all. The Nine would see a Solar prince, held under the Accord, holding out the ring of the Sovereign’s own house, which he had bought. They would say for a hundred years that the Conti seal had been redeemed with Solar crowns and the Conti Sovereign bought along with it, and it would be as true as Oriane de Chardon’s report on the morning of the snowdrift, and do as much harm.

And he thought of Arian’s face, which nobody could read, being told before five standing councillors a thing he had the right to hear first and alone, from Caleb, in a voice nobody else could hear.

Not in the hall, then.

At the door.

He would give it back tonight, at the sill, where Arian had kept every night on the dark side of the light. He would kneel on the stone as he had knelt the night before, and put it into that hand, and close the long white fingers over it one at a time, and say whatever came into his head, which would probably be something foolish. And Arian would thank him, because he could not help himself, and the count would go to eight, and the two of them would go on owing each other for a great many Eclipses yet, and he could not find it in him to mind.

He got up off the bucket.

“Highness,” said Paul, from among the casks. The candle, which had stood up again, laid its light across half his face and left the other half to the cellar. “If you could go home. Tonight. Fairly. If nobody’d be hurt by it. Would you?”

“No.”

“Not for your sisters?”

“My sisters know where I am. Two of them have come to see for themselves.” He picked up the candle. “Paul. I am home.”

Paul looked at him across the cellar for a long moment. Then he looked at the floor, and stood back among the casks for Caleb to go up first.

\* \* \*

The garden doors woke at his ring, rune after square rune going from red to gold, and somewhere inside the iron the old slow wheels turned, and the doors parted. The light came out over him down the corridor, warm and gold, and went into him through the new silk as if the silk were not there. He left the doors standing open behind him. He always did, by day.

Tomas was on the top terrace among the roses with a pair of shears, taking off the spent blooms, and he lifted his grey head as Caleb came out onto the mezzanine and nodded once, as a man nods to a neighbour over a wall. There was ash on his shoulders too, and here and there a white rose had a fleck of it lying in its heart, like a cinder in a cup of milk.

Caleb went down the long gold spiral to the bed of golden lilies by the water-stair. The lilies had straightened since he lay in them, and the water went over its stone lip talking to itself as if nothing had ever happened on its bank but lemons.

He sat on the step where he always sat, and laid his left hand on his heart and his right on the warm stone, and began to rehearse.

He had it by heart by the turn of the third crystal, having tried it twice on the nearest lily, which bore it with great civility and leaned in at the good parts, and he threw it away, because it was a speech, and the man he meant it for had sat through a great many speeches and never deserved to be made one at. By the fifth crystal he had given it up and was only sitting in the light with his hand on the ring, thinking of a long white hand closing over something cold one finger at a time, and he was so happy that he could not keep still, and kept still anyway, because he had been taught.

Paul had come down after him.

Paul stood at the doors, as a rule, with his back to the iron, and came into the garden only when he was sent for. Now he had come all the way down and stopped on the step above Caleb, a little to one side, in the full gold, with his big hands hanging open.

“Sit down, Paul.”

“I’ll stand, Highness.”

“You have stood for fourteen years. You may sit for one afternoon. I shall not report you.” Caleb turned on the step to look up at him. “You owe me a very long conversation. I told you so in the lower yard with my hands in your side. And you have promised me twice since, once in Dornhöhle and once on a ledge with the sun on us, that you would tell me everything when we were home.”

He smiled. He could not help it. It came up out of him with the gold.

“Well. I am home. So.”

Paul was not looking at him. He was looking up, past the terraces and the mezzanine and the open doors, up the whole gold throat of the old vent to the dome, which from the lilies showed only as a pale round of violet a long way above. He was looking at the crown of it.

“Not yet, Highness.”

Something fell on Caleb’s hand.

It was grey and soft, as light as a flake of pastry, and when he lifted his hand to look at it, it smeared, and there was another on his sleeve, and another turning slowly on the water of the stair as it was carried down. He looked up.

It was snowing ash in the garden.

It came down out of the gold in long slow drifts, turning over and over, and as each flake passed a crystal it shone gold at the edges like a moth too near a lamp, and went grey again, and fell on the roses and the lilies. Caleb had lived eleven weeks in the ash of the Coast and never seen it fall in his garden, because his garden was under the glass, and the glass was swept every third day, at the first bell.

At the very top of the vent, where the iron ribs of the dome came together, there was a dark round hole where the crown had been.

Above the hole the ash was lit with a white light, whiter than the crystals and colder, laid over the crown of the mountain like a lid of noon set down on a pot. Where the ash drifted into that light it caught, and came down through the hole as cinders, red at the heart, and went out on the way down past the crystals, and lay on the roses grey. His blood knew the light before he did, and stood up in him, and leaned towards it. Under his hand the ring lay cold and leaned nowhere.

On the top terrace Tomas had straightened, with the shears still open in his hand, and was looking up.

Then the singing began.

\* \* \*

It had no words in it.

That was the first thing Caleb knew about it, and afterwards it was what he remembered longest. It came down the throat of the vent out of the white light, high and clear and without a single word, as a song comes across water at night from a boat that cannot be seen, and his throat ached to hear it. It went into the light and the light changed. It went pearl at the edges, as a candle goes pearl behind a shade of horn, and the crystals hummed on their chains in answer, all twelve, on a note they had never sung before, and the water on the stair stopped talking to itself and only ran.

The fae could not lie. Children learned it in every realm along with their letters. They could not set one false word beside another and say it aloud.

Nobody had ever said that they could not sing.

Three of them rode the song down out of the crown, down the middle of the light as if they were walking down a stair, unhurried, with their hands at their sides and their faces turned up a little to the music. There was no stair. There was nothing under them but the gold and the falling ash. They came down past the first crystal and the second, and their coats were white feathers, white as the gown of a girl at her first ball in Lutèce, every feather edged with gold where the crystals touched it.

Caleb was on his feet. He did not remember standing.

“Paul,” he called. “Paul, go and fetch —”

He turned, and Paul was not there.

The step above him was empty. The terraces went up in their long spiral to the open doors with nobody on them, and Tomas was gone from among the roses, and then the roses were gone. Where the top terrace had been there was a long white floor that held the light upside down beneath it like still water, and the smell had changed. It smelled of lilies, and beeswax, and stone warm from the noon.

It was the Throne Hall.

He had learned to walk on that floor. There were the columns of light falling through the high glass, so bright that a hand might have been laid on them, and the dais, and the great sunburst, and the noon standing in every window. Somebody was laughing at the far end of the hall, and it was Cecily at eight on the edge of a bath, with the steam round her lit gold to the ceiling, and a tin of candied violets stood open on the marble, and somewhere behind him, in the voice he had heard say nothing else for a whole winter in a practice yard, Matilda said *Again.*

He wanted to go home so badly that it bent him.

It came up out of the bottom of him as it had come up on the water-stair with Eleanor’s complaint on his knee in his seventh week on the Coast, as if it had never gone anywhere, and under the song there was a voice with no words in it that said *Yes*, and *Come*, and *Here*.

He put his hand on the ring.

And he laughed. It came out of him in the middle of the Throne Hall without his leave, because he had stood in that hall a thousand times and the noon in its windows had never been this colour. It was the gold of twelve stones in the throat of a mountain. He had lain in it four nights with his hands in the water. He knew it as he knew his own name.

“That is very pretty,” he said aloud, to the singing. “I have been home. I did not care for the company.”

The Throne Hall went thin, as a page goes thin against a window, and through it, faint as the writing on the other side, he saw the terraces again. On the top one Tomas stood among the roses with his shears open in his hand and his face turned up to nothing, smiling, as a man smiles who has come home late and found the lamp lit in his own window. There were tears running down his weathered face into his collar, and he did not seem to know that they were there.

At the foot of the water-stair, among the golden lilies, the first of the fae had come down out of the air and was standing on the earth.

\* \* \*

She was not young. Her face was long and pale and fine-boned under a cap of white feathers, and the eyes in it were tired, as a courier’s are tired who has ridden a long road in bad weather and been paid by the league. Close to, there was a darn at the elbow of her coat, where somebody had mended the feathers with white silk that had gone a little grey.

The other two came down behind her into the lilies, a man and a woman, and did not stop singing. The song came out of them as the note comes out of a glass bell long after it is struck, and the Throne Hall hung round Caleb like the steam over that gold bath, thinner now, and waiting.

“Your Royal Highness,” said the fae in front of him, and curtseyed as deep as the Citadel’s own court would have curtseyed to a prince of the blood, there in the lilies with the ash settling on her feathers. “I am Aude de Vair, of the Court of Lys. We are sent to bring you home.”

The fae could not lie.

He had taken Oriane de Chardon apart once on the black glass of the great hall with nothing but that, and a little patience. He had the patience still. He found it somewhere under the wanting, and stood on it, as a man stands on a stone in a ford.

“Who sent you?” he asked.

“The Crown Prince of Aloria, Highness.”

“Does he know that I do not wish to go?”

“He has been told that you will say so.” Her voice was low and pleasant, with Lys in every vowel. “He has been told why.”

“Why?”

“Because you are enthralled, Highness.”

The song behind her rose a little, as if to cover the word.

“Am I?”

Aude de Vair looked at him for a long moment with the ash on her white cap, and something moved in her tired face that a fae of the Court of Lys had perhaps not been paid for.

“I do not know, Highness. I have not been told how to know it.”

“Then you are taking me on the word of a man who was not here.”

“We are taking you because the Accord is broken in that part, as the Consigliere of this Coast declared before witnesses at the gala, and a hostage so wronged is restored to his house if his house require it. The Crown Prince requires it, and sends for his own.” Her voice stayed low. “That is what I was told to say, and it is true that I was told it.”

“My sister required an inquiry.”

“Your sister is not the Throne, Highness.”

“Is the Sovereign harmed?”

“Not by us.”

That was a fae answer, and he let it go past him, because there was another question standing at the back of his mind, and had been since the ash began to fall, like a man at the back of a room who has been waiting to be noticed.

“Who opened the crown?”

Aude de Vair did not hesitate. She had no means to.

“Your man.”

\* \* \*

He turned round.

Paul was there. He had been there all along, on the step above, a pace to one side, where the Throne Hall had painted him out. He stood with his hands at his sides, and in the right one, held loosely as a man holds a match he has forgotten to strike, was a sliver of clear crystal no longer than a finger, glowing faintly at its heart.

There was ash on his shoulders. There had been ash on his shoulders on the stair.

Caleb knew the crystal. The Radiant Guard carried them in pairs in the field, and a word spoken into one came out of its twin and nowhere else in the world.

He had seen Paul hold his hands like that a hundred times. Under a rune lamp in a passage in Dornhöhle, with his head bowed. By the third carriage in the courtyard, with the ash coming down. On the turn of the water-stair last night, after the cup. *The Highland March prays into its hands*, he had told Arian once, *so that the wind cannot take the words before they reach the moon.*

*There is no wind in this courtyard*, Arian had said.

“There was never any prayer.”

“No, Highness.”

He did not ask who was at the other end of it. He knew a room in the east tower where the shutters were never quite closed, and the man at the long table in it, as he knew the gold of the garden.

“How long?”

“Since the Citadel. Since the night before the wine.” Paul’s voice came out low and level, as a soldier reports, and something under it was coming apart. “I said I’d tell you everything when we were home. We’re not going to be home, Highness. Not you and me. Not after this. So I’ll tell you now. I told him the garden. I told him the gallery, that night, sitting with my back against those doors. I told him the road to Durnhollow and the day you’d go and how many’d ride with you, and the Fault road, and the hour, and that you’d go at dusk.” His hand closed on the crystal. “I told him about the flower, with you lying in the lilies. I sat at the doors and counted your heart for him, Highness, and told him how many it missed in a hundred. Every bell of every night, I told him how you were.”

Caleb stood on the step in the falling ash.

He saw a boy on the wheel of a carriage with his leg broken under him, and a sun-blade breaking in a man’s side. He saw Arian’s head turn in the dark of a sheep cave towards Paul’s heart, and turn again in the lambing pens, and in the courtyard by the third carriage, and towards the wall of the great hall when Valentina Caruso stood up and said *eleven nights*. Every time the head had turned, and stayed turned, and listened.

Arian had known something, perhaps not what. He had listened to that heart go wrong for eleven weeks and let it be, because it was the heart of a man Caleb loved.

“Paul,” said Caleb. “Do you think that I am enthralled?”

“No, Highness.”

“Then why?”

Paul looked at him, and for once since the Citadel his look did not stop at Caleb’s collar. His eyes were tawny and gold, lycan eyes made for the dark, and they were running in the light.

“Because my mother’s cough is much improved.”

Caleb did not understand him. Then he did.

“He has them.”

“Under the steward at Loch Aird. My mother, and Ishbel, and Morag. Since the spring.” The words came out of Paul one at a time, as if each had to be lifted. “He’s never once said what he’d do. Not once in all of it. He only ever tells me she’s well. Every week. *Your mother is well, Walker. Her cough is much improved. Your sisters send their love.* In that voice.” His big hand shook round the crystal. “He knows I know what it means. And he knows I know he’d do it, because he’s never once had to say it.”

Behind Caleb the singing had only waited, as the sea waits at the turn, and he felt it begin again now, rising.

“They nearly killed you, Highness. Three times since the Citadel. In the gallery and in the gorge and in your own chair at your own fire, with a flower. And every time I sat with my back to the door and told him so, and every time he said *Your mother is well.*” He drew a breath that shook all the way down. “I don’t know what I believe any more. I know what I saw in the gallery, and I know what I saw last night on those stairs. I don’t know which is true. But I know where my mother sleeps.”

\* \* \*

The song changed.

It had gone looking for what he wanted through the noon of the Citadel and the gold bath and *Again*, and found him not there, and it came back down into him as water comes down a dry channel and runs to the lowest place in it. The Throne Hall went out like a lamp. There was only the garden again, his own garden, gold, with the ash coming down through it.

And at the top of it, at the open doors, a tall figure in black waited at the edge of the light with his hand on the iron, as on the first day.

He lifted his foot and stepped over the sill.

He came down onto the mezzanine in the full gold of the crystals, and the frost lay quiet on his shoulders, and the white hands did not redden. He came down the iron stairs as a man comes down the stairs of his own house in the evening, one hand trailing the rail, down past the lemon trees and the roses, all the way into the light, and it lay on his face and did him no harm at all, and he was looking at Caleb, and he held out his hand.

*Come*, said the song. *Here.*

Caleb took a step towards him, and another, and the lilies bent round his boots. He had wished this on the first day, and on the night of the lemons, and on the night of the hearthstone with his own voice saying *He is not to come in*, and on every night between, and here it was, coming down the long gold stair of the garden with its hand held out, as if it had always been allowed.

He was almost at the iron stairs when he knew.

It was the frost. He had seen the backs of those fingers blister on a doorframe on his first day in this light, and come back out of it broken open to the red on the night they carried him down to the lilies. He had held one of them across the line of the light and felt it burn in his palms while he mended it. The light had never touched that man without costing him, and he had never once come an inch further than the edge of it.

That was how Caleb knew him. The real one burned.

“No.”

He let the light go.

He let it go all at once. He had spent the last weeks learning to ask it, low and warm and careful, so that it should warm a man and not burn him, and he did not ask it now. He opened the doors of himself and let it out. It went up out of him gold and white together through the new silk and every bound seam of it, through his hands and his throat and his eyes, and met the gold of the twelve stones coming down, and the garden went white.

The seeming tore, as a painted cloth tears in a playhouse when a lamp falls against it, from the middle outward in long burning rents, and the figure on the iron stairs curled at the edges as a pressed leaf curls in a grate, and was not there. The doors stood open at the top of the garden as he had left them, and the corridor beyond was empty and violet, and on the iron sill, where the gold stopped, the long shadow Arian had left there lay pointing down into the garden, towards him.

Behind him one of the fae cried out.

The song broke in the middle of a note, as a glass bell cracks when it is struck too hard, and came back ragged from two throats instead of three, and Aude de Vair said something sharp in the tongue of Lys. He turned. The fae in the lilies stood with their arms thrown up over their faces against the white of him, and the man among them had gone down on one knee, and the light was all round them, and it was his. The gilt edges of the man’s white feathers had caught. The fire ran back along them towards the quills in thin bright threads, as it runs along the edge of a field at the stubble-burning, and the smell of them came across the lilies, scorched and sweetish, and the man made a sound into his hands that Caleb had last heard in an infirmary in the Vales, from a farrier with a kettle down his arm.

He drew it in.

He did it as he had done it in the gorge of the Fault, drawing the light out of the air into his two palms and folding it down on itself, narrower and narrower, into a thread. He would put it into their three pairs of tired, beautiful eyes, as gently as he could, more gently than he had just been, so that they would not see for an hour and could not sing what they could not see, and he would go up his own stairs to his own doors and wait on the sill for the Sovereign, and give him the ring tonight. He lifted his hands.

Paul’s hands closed over his.

They came round Caleb’s two hands from the outside, big and brown and calloused, and held them shut together, cupped, as they had closed every night round a sliver of crystal and a voice. The thread of light was inside them, and it would go through a lycan’s hands as easily as through a lycan’s eyes.

“Do it, Highness.”

He was close enough that Caleb could see the ash on his lashes and the wet under it.

“I’d let you,” whispered Paul. “Go on. I’d let you.”

Caleb looked at the hands round his.

He had put his own two hands into Paul’s torn side once on the black stone of a yard, and knitted a lung back together a breath at a time, and told him to hush while he counted. He knew what the light would do to those hands. He could smell it still, coming across the lilies.

He could not.

The thread went out in his palms between Paul’s as a spark goes out on wet slate, without a sound, and the light that had been roaring in him went down after it all at once, as water goes down a sluice, and left him standing in the garden with his hands held shut in another man’s and nothing in them.

The song came back whole, from three throats, rising. It came into him wherever the light had gone out of him, as mist comes into a valley at evening and fills every hollow of it, and it was warm, and sweet, and it tasted of a glass of Citadel white pressed into his hand by a man with a composed and courteous face.

His knees went.

Paul caught him. He caught him as he had caught him on a stair at fourteen with a turned ankle, and in a sheep cave at the line of the sun, high against his chest with an arm under his knees, and Caleb felt himself lifted, and felt Paul’s heart going under his cheek like a horse that has been ridden at a wall.

“No, Paul,” he breathed. It came from a long way off. “Not this time.”

“This time, Highness.”

\* \* \*

He could not move his hands.

The song had taken his hands and his feet and the light in him and laid them gently somewhere he could not reach, as a nurse lays a child’s toys on a high shelf at bedtime, and it had left him his eyes. He lay in Paul’s arms with the ash settling on his face, and he could see the whole of the garden.

Something was let down out of the crown on a line so fine that only the gold showed it. It was a sling of white silk on a ring of silver, such as Lowmere bargemen use to swing a cask from a quay into a hold, and it came to rest among the lilies at Paul’s feet. Paul stepped into it with Caleb in his arms. Aude de Vair looked up into the white light and lifted her hand, and far above, a windlass began to turn.

They went up.

The lilies went away beneath his boots, and the lemon trees, and Tomas among the roses with his wet face turned up to nothing. The first crystal came down past them, turning slowly on its silver chain, so near that Caleb felt it on the side of his face like a hand. The fae went up beside them on their own lines, singing, with their white feathers lit, and the ash came down past all of them the other way.

And the doors came down towards them out of the wall.

He had only ever seen them from below. The sling went up past them now as a bucket goes up a well past the one lamp somebody has hung halfway, a stone’s throw out from the wall in the middle of the light, and for the space of one slow turn of the windlass the doors were level with his eyes, and open, and the corridor beyond them was not empty.

He was on the sill.

He had come fast, from a hall full of the Nine with five of them standing, because a heart under his ribs that kept another man’s time had gone suddenly as hard and fast as Caleb’s own, and he had not waited for the steward to strike the floor. He stood on the iron at the very edge of the light with one hand on the frame, and the frost on him was boiling. It went up off his shoulders in a long white plume, and off his hair, and off the hand on the frame where the gold lay across the backs of the fingers. Caleb had mended that hand once, a finger at a time. He watched the light have it now. The skin went red, and tight, and shone, and rose all across the backs in white blisters as full and close as the bubbles on a pan of milk about to boil, and split along the knuckles, and ran, and the frost came up out of him over the wet and went to smoke on it as fast as it came. He did not take the hand away.

The song tried. Caleb felt it reach for the figure in the doorway and lay over it a man coming in, unburned, smiling, with his hand out. The figure in the doorway burned straight through it, as a lamp burns through a paper shade, and did not come in.

He had put his foot on the very edge of the iron. Caleb saw the boot come forward onto the line where the gold lay, and the gold take it, and the leather begin to smoke.

Arian looked at Paul.

Behind him the corridor stood up. Every shadow in it came up off the stone at his back at once, as a pack of hounds comes up off the straw at a step in the yard, and leaned out after him over the sill as far as the line of the gold, and no further, and stayed there, long and black and trembling, every point of them turned on the sling and the man in it. Caleb had seen what the dark of the Coast did when its master let it off his hand. He had seen it in the Fault go over a man on his knees with his empty hands held up, and take him all the same, and leave nothing on the rock but frost.

The look on the sill was the one Arian had given once down a black gallery to a man in torn livery with a face like chalk, and Paul, in the sling, with Caleb in his arms and the ash on his lashes, looked back at him for longer than he had ever looked at anything. Then he looked down, as he had looked down in the gallery and on every night since, and there was no floor under him to look at. There was only the garden, a long way down, gold, with the ash falling into it.

“Walker,” said Arian.

He said nothing else to him. Paul’s arms tightened round Caleb, as arms tighten round what they carry when the ground goes out from under them, and he did not look up again.

Then Arian looked at Caleb.

He had the other foot on the sill. He was going to come in. Caleb could see it in the line of him, and he could see the twelve stones between them and the length of air no shadow could cross, and he knew the light would have him long before he reached the middle of it, and that there was nothing in the middle of it for him to reach.

The song had Caleb’s hands, and his feet, and his light. It had not quite got his voice, because he had not given it his voice, and in the Harp Vales he had learned to say a thing plainly and low when it had to be said.

“Caleb,” said Arian.

Every flower in the garden turned its face up. It went up the terraces from the bottom to the top, the lilies first and then the roses on their walls, white head after white head turning up the long gold spiral, as it had turned at the same name on the night Arian went to Durnhollow, and this time they turned up after him, every one of them, into the falling ash.

“Stay in the dark,” said Caleb.

He saw the hand on the frame close on the iron. He saw the boot on the line of the gold come back, slowly, an inch, into the shadow of the sill, with the smoke still going up off the leather, and stay there.

He had been asked. He stayed.

Behind him the shadows of the corridor lay down again on the stone, as hounds lie down when the word is given, with their eyes still on the gate.

The windlass turned, and the doors went down past them, and then they were above the doors and looking down on a lit doorway in the black wall of the vent with a dark figure in it, getting smaller, standing still with his hand on the frame and the smoke going up off him white into the gold.

And the shadow came.

The long shadow on the sill lifted off the iron, as a man asleep in a chair sits up at the sound of his own name, and came out into the light after him. It came up the face of the black wall, long and thin and dark, out of the shadow of the doorway into the full gold of twelve stones, reaching. The light burned it at the tip as a wick burns down, and it went on reaching, after the breast of his coat and what lay under the crescent, further than any shadow ought to have been able to go into that much light, until it could come no further, and stayed there, stretched up the wall of the vent like a hand held up, burning.

Caleb watched it until the crown came down round him, and the white light poured into his eyes, and a great many hands took hold of the sling. And there was the song, on and on, without a word in it, laying him down on the high shelf with everything else.

His heart was going hard. He could not lift a hand to count it.

Somebody else would be counting it.

\* \* \*

He knew the hum. He had woken to it once on a chaise of cool silk with ash in his mouth, the low hum of light-engines under a deck, going on into the bones like a hive in a wall. He lay in it now and could not open his eyes, and did not wish to.

There was singing beside him, low, and it did not stop. Whenever he came up towards the top of it, it rose a little, and he went down again.

Once when he came up the light had changed. It came through his eyelids red and gold and hot, as the noon comes through the lids of a child asleep in a garden, and he knew that they had come out of the ash and that the Coast was behind them.

Once when he came up, a voice was speaking on the far side of a door, low and close, as words are when they are spoken into cupped hands.

“Highness.”

A silence. Then, faint and thin and near, as if a man were speaking at Caleb’s own ear from the far end of the world, another voice answered.

“Is he whole?”

He knew it. He had crawled the length of the Throne Hall towards it on the white marble before he could say a word, and gone to sleep on its shoulder.

“Yes, Highness. Not a hair of his head.”

“And the Coast?”

“The Sovereign came to the doors. He didn’t come in.” A pause. “His Highness told him not to.”

The voice was quiet for a while. Caleb lay in the hum with the song going on beside him and listened to his brother breathe in a locked room a long way north.

“They will say anything, at the last,” said Andrew. “The fae say the enthralled weep to go back, and swear they were happy, and ask for whoever held them by name. They say it is the worst of it. Let him weep, Walker. Let him say what he likes. He will not remember the half of it, and I will remember the rest for him.” The voice gentled. “It is a rescue. Whatever he says when he wakes, and whatever you think you saw. You took him out of the dark. Hold on to that.”

Paul said nothing.

“Bring him to the east landing. Not the Throne Hall. I want no one at the step but me.”

“Highness.” Paul’s voice scraped on it. “My mother.”

“Your mother is well,” said Andrew.

He said it gently, as a groom speaks to a horse that will not be led, and Caleb had never heard him use that voice, and the song rose and took him down again before he could hear whether Paul said anything at all.

He counted. He had no hands to lay on his own wrist, so he listened for it in the bones of his ears, as he had learned in the Vales to count a pulse he could not reach. He had promised three hundred, every night, and he did not know whether it was night. Somewhere a long way south another heart was keeping his time, and he would not let it lose count because he had been carried off asleep a second time.

Somewhere past two hundred he lost his place in the numbers, and went down, and did not come up for a long time.

\* \* \*

He woke to the taste of noon.

It was hot and white and tasted of marble that has stood in the sun since before anyone living was born, and it lay on his tongue where the ash had lain once. The silk of a chaise was cool under his cheek. There was no singing. It had stopped, he did not know when, and the shelf where it had laid his hands had come down to meet him, and he could move his fingers.

He opened his eyes on pale ashwood and polished silver, and the royal sunburst on every panel and fitting, blazing. They had been made to drink the Citadel’s noon and give it back, and the noon was here to drink, pouring in at the porthole as hot and white as metal from a crucible, and they gave it back off every panel and hinge until the cabin swam in gold. It came onto his face and into him through the black silk, and his blood rose to meet it whether he wished it to or not, and the gold came up under his skin in his hands and his throat, and he lay on the chaise and hated it, and could not make it stop.

He put his hand on his heart.

The ring was there, under the crescent, between the silk and the lining, the one cold place on him in all that gold. Nobody had found it. They had carried him up out of the throat of a mountain in a sling of white silk and north over the whole of the Marches, and not one of them had thought to feel along the breast of his coat.

He got up, and went to the door, and opened it.

Paul was standing outside it, as he had stood outside every door Caleb had slept behind since Caleb was fourteen, with his back to the wall and his hands at his sides and the ash still on his shoulders, grey in the white light. He looked up when the door opened, and did not look away. His face had nothing left in it to put away.

On the first morning on the Coast, Caleb had come out of a door much like this one with ash in his mouth and his hands not steady, and found Paul outside it, and smiled at him with the whole of his face.

He did not smile now. He looked at Paul for a long time, in the gold, and Paul let him. Then he went past him, up the companion ladder, towards the light.

The yacht had come down on the east landing, a long white shelf of marble jutting from the flank of the palace below the heir’s tower. There were no heralds on it, and no guard, and no court in the colours of morning. There was only the noon, standing on the marble from one end of the landing to the other, so bright that the marble seemed to have no surface and the edges of everything went soft in it, and above it, as they had burned at every hour since before he was born, the great crystals on the spires stood up against a sky with no evening in it anywhere, and blazed.

At the foot of the yacht’s step, a man in grey was kneeling.

He knelt on one knee on the marble with his right hand over his heart and his head bowed, and when he heard Caleb’s boot on the step he lifted his head, and the light went white across his eyes. Caleb had seen the white come into his brother’s eyes at a council table, and at the quintain, and once, when he was nine, at a dog that bit Cecily. This flared up and stood there, and lit the marble round Andrew’s knee and the hand over his heart, and Caleb understood, with a slowness that frightened him, that it was joy.

“Caleb.”

He rose, and came up the step with his hands out, as he had once crossed a nursery bathroom running with gold to lift a boy out of it, and Caleb stood on the top step in the black coat of the Coast with the crescent of House Conti over his heart, and did not go to meet him.

“You are home.” Andrew’s voice was not quite steady, and Caleb had never heard it so. “It is over. They cannot reach you here. Nothing can.” His hands had stopped a hand’s breadth short of Caleb’s arms, as if he had remembered something, and hung there. “I promised you once. You will not remember. You were a year old. I promised that I would never let anything hurt you, and I let them have you for eleven weeks, and I will be sorry for it every day I live. But it is done now. I have brought you home.”

Caleb looked at the hands, and at the white going down slowly in his brother’s eyes, and at the face, which was thinner than it had been in the spring, and older, with something in it under the joy that he did not yet have a name for and did not wish to. He thought of a broken gold chain laid out on a desk in a tall narrow room, with one link wrenched open.

“Let me by, Andrew. I can walk.”

Andrew’s hands came down.

Caleb went down the step past him onto the marble, on his own two feet, into the noon. It came at him from every side, off the marble and the white walls of the palace and the blazing crowns of the spires, and it filled him to the fingertips, and the gold rose in him so bright that his own hands were hard to look at, and every part of him that was an elf of the Solar House stood up in it and was glad. He walked across the landing in it with his hand flat on the breast of his coat, and was not.

At the parapet he stopped, and turned his face south.

There was the Citadel going down below him in its white terraces, and beyond the last of them the green country and the long road, and beyond the road the whole of the Marches under the noon, and beyond the Marches, a long way off, low down where nobody in the Citadel ever looked, a grey stain along the bottom of the sky where the ash began.

He set two fingers to his wrist, and counted.

It went on under his fingers, steady and slow and warm, in the noon of the Citadel, as it had gone on in a cave and a carriage and a bed under a canopy of frost. And somewhere south, at the end of the long road, at the bottom of a mountain, in the dark, another heart was keeping time with it, in a chest that had held no heartbeat at all until a boy of eight knelt in the roses and counted one into it.

He owed a man his notes. There was a great deal of noon here, and somebody who had never seen any of it would want to know what colour it was.
