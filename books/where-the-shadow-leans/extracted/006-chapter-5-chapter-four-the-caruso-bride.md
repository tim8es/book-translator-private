## Chapter 5: Chapter Four - The Caruso Bride

*Of the bite there is little to be written, for among the Umbral houses it is not done, save between two who have chosen one another, and of those, fewer still will speak of it.*

— *Customs of the Umbral Houses*, compiled for the Court of Lys

\* \* \*

The marks faded after four days.

Each morning Caleb knelt by the pool at the foot of the water-stair, where the black stone beneath made a mirror of the water, and waited for the rings from the falling stream to smooth themselves away, and tilted his head to one side, and looked. On the first morning there were two crescents under his jaw, pale and cool to the touch, like a bruise that had never been allowed to happen. They were colder than the skin around them, and stayed cold however long he sat in the full blaze of the crystals, and his fingers kept going back to them. By the third they had gone the faint blue of a vein seen through the skin of a wrist. On the fourth he had to hunt for them, turning his chin this way and that until his neck ached, and when at last he could not find them at all, he sat back on his heels in the wet moss, surprised by the odd, hollow feeling of having had something taken from him twice.

He slept among the lilies in those four days, where the light lay warm on his eyelids even through sleep, and woke each time with gold pollen on his sleeves and his hand already at his throat. Sometimes, at the edge of sleep, he felt again the two points of cold there, sharp as splinters of ice. He ate on the iron stairs, where the white roses leaned through the rails to brush his sleeve and the twelve crystals burned overhead on their silver chains, as golden and untroubled as if nothing whatever had happened. He read the same page of the same book so many times that he could have recited it, and still had no notion what it was about. He kept the great doors closed now, even in the daytime, because an open door had begun to feel like a question, and he had no answer ready for whoever might come and stand in it.

When Vesna came battering at them with her trays he let her in and shut them again behind her. On the first morning she looked at his throat, and then at his face, and her mouth went thin. She set down broth instead of scones, and a pot of bitter Wildmarch tisane, and stood over him with her arms folded until he had drunk every drop of both. The broth was dark and rich, with a skin of gold on it, and it had plainly been simmering on her fire since long before he woke. The herbs tasted of wet hillsides and woodsmoke, and were so bitter that his eyes watered. He drank them anyway, because she was watching, and felt the warmth go down into the cold place under his jaw and settle there, and only then understood that he had been shivering since the gallery, in a garden as warm as an Alorian noon.

On the second morning she brought the scones back, and a blanket he had not asked for. It was undyed wool, coarse as a dog’s coat, and it smelled of cedar and the kitchen fire, and she shook it out and dropped it round his shoulders without a word. He slept in it that night, and the night after. On the third morning she sat down beside him on the stairs and stayed while he ate, with her heavy forearms on her knees, looking out over the terraces at the lemon trees, breathing slow, and the iron creaked under her. When she rose to go she put her broad, weathered hand on the top of his head, the way she did with Mirek. It was warm and heavy and smelled of flour, and it stayed there a while.

No one had touched him like that since he was a child. After the doors had closed behind her he sat on with his jaw set until his eyes stopped stinging, while the lemon trees looked on from their urns with what he considered quite unnecessary sympathy. He had done quite enough weeping in this fortress to last him a reign.

Paul kept his post outside the doors, in the violet dusk of the corridor, beside the thin gold line that showed beneath them. He held himself the way a man does around ribs that remember being broken, and once a day, while the doors stood open for Vesna’s tray, he put his head round the iron to ask whether His Highness required anything. His eyes went everywhere in the garden but to Caleb’s throat.

“I require the conversation you owe me,” Caleb insisted, on the second day. “Come in and sit down.”

“I owe Your Highness my life.” Paul looked at the floor. “I know it.”

“That is not what I —”

But Paul had bowed and gone. Caleb heard him settle against the iron outside, and the scrape of his boots on the stone, and knew that the man who had guarded him since he was fourteen sat a door’s thickness away, and further off than he had ever been.

It was Mirek, in the end, who brought him the news, because Mirek brought him everything.

“The Sovereign hasn’t left his rooms,” the boy said on the fourth evening, setting down the tea things with elaborate care, one silver spoon at a time. He had not dropped a tray since the day Caleb poured him a cup of tea and asked him to sit. “Not once. The physicians are in and out at all hours. My mother sends up blood-wine but it always comes back down again full.”

Caleb, who had been buttering a scone, put down the knife.

“Is he ill?”

Mirek looked at the tray. “Nobody’s saying, Highness.”

“That,” Caleb protested, “is not an answer, Mirek, as I think you are perfectly well aware, and I had begun to hope that we were friends enough by now to be spared the courtly kind.”

Mirek only shook his head, and after a moment Caleb understood that the boy had no answer to give. Nobody in the fortress had. The Sovereign of Vesperia had shut his door four days ago with Caleb’s light still burning in him, and the whole of Castel Nerezza had hushed itself around that door, as a house hushes round a sickroom, until it seemed that even the violet lamps in the galleries burned low for fear of disturbing him.

The butter had gone soft and yellow in the warmth. Caleb turned his plate a quarter turn, and then another, and saw again the gold at the corner of a white mouth, shining faintly in the dark like a lamp turned low. He told himself firmly that the health of the Sovereign of Vesperia was no concern of a hostage’s. Then he lay awake half the night among the lilies, with the coarse blanket pulled up to his chin, listening past the sound of the water to the great dark silence of the fortress overhead.

\* \* \*

He went to find Enzo on the fifth day.

He had been raised by a mother who held that an apology delayed was an apology half spoiled, like cream left standing in the sun, and it had been six days since he learned whose blood the debt was for.

“He won’t be in his office today,” Mirek admitted, after some persuasion, and looked extremely unhappy about it. “It’s the month-day. He’ll be in the lamp room.”

Caleb did not know what a month-day was, or a lamp room. He went to find out.

Since the night of the garden the last door of the east wing had stood unlocked. But he had been watched every day of his life, and he did not know what to do with a cage so large that he could not see the bars. So he had kept to the east wing and the garden, and gone further only to the cellar, and once, at a run, to the yard where Paul lay bleeding. Now he left the warmth of the garden at the iron doors, where the cold of the mountain stood waiting for him as patiently as a footman with a coat, and went the long way, through the heart of Castel Nerezza, and found it far fuller than he had supposed.

It had only seemed empty because it was quiet. The great galleries were full of people going about the business of a court in the dark, under violet lamps hung so high in the vaulting that their light came down soft and dusky, and the air smelled of cold stone and lamp oil and, faintly, everywhere, of blood-wine. Every so often a slow, warm draught came up the stairwells from somewhere far below and sank away again, so that the whole fortress seemed to be breathing in its sleep.

Vampire clerks passed in pairs with ledgers under their arms, murmuring in Vesperian. Lycan housemaids knelt scrubbing the black steps with brushes that hissed on the glassy stone, and lycan guards in Conti black stood at every arch. A fae courier in a coat of feathers the soft brown of a moth’s wing darted past with a satchel of sealed letters, stopped dead, and walked on backwards for several paces to keep staring. At the foot of the grand staircase a vampire in physician’s robes, who had the face of a man of thirty and a voice that creaked like an old hinge, was arguing in low, furious tones with a steward about a delivery of ice. Caleb did not let himself wonder whom the ice was for.

They all fell silent when they saw him, which he had expected. Then the guards at the arches straightened as he passed, as they would have for one of the household. The housemaids got up off their knees. The clerks stepped aside to let him by, and one of them bowed, and straightened in plain confusion, unable to think why he had done it. Every one of them looked first at his face, and then at his left hand. Word of that afternoon in the yard had plainly gone round Castel Nerezza a great deal faster than Caleb had.

He put his hand in his pocket and kept walking, and felt their eyes on his back all the way to the western side of the fortress, like a draught between his shoulder blades.

The lamp room lay off a quiet gallery there, where the noise of the court fell away behind him step by step until he could hear his own breathing. It was cut into the rock like everything else in Castel Nerezza, low and close and cool, and it was full of lamps. There were dozens of them, perhaps a hundred, set in niches up and down the walls, each a bowl of dark glass, garnet or smoke or deepest violet, with a single flame burning low in it. The black stone of the walls took every flame and gave it back, so that each one burned twice, once in its bowl and once, more softly, in the rock beside it, and the whole chamber glimmered like a hive of fireflies. The air was warm with oil and a sweet, dark resin, and so still that not one flame wavered.

Beneath each niche a name was cut into the stone. Some of the names were so old that the letters had worn almost smooth, and could have been read only with a fingertip. One of them, low on the wall to the right, was so newly cut that the edges of the letters were still sharp, and a pale dust of stone still lay in the bottom of them, where no one had yet found the heart to brush it away.

Enzo Conti sat on a bench before it with his hands between his knees. He wore plain black today instead of dove grey, and on his brow, just above the bridge of his nose, someone had drawn a crescent in black ash, and Caleb understood what a month-day was. Caleb had only ever seen the Consigliere upright, all straight lines and dry exactness. Now Enzo sat bowed over his own knees with the lamplight lying along the curve of his back, and he looked as worn as the oldest names on the wall.

He would have heard Caleb’s step a gallery away, and he kept his head bowed.

“Your Highness. You must have lost your way.”

“No. I came to find you.”

He had passed twenty-eight years at the most exacting court in the Dominion and had so seldom given anyone real cause for offence that he had scarcely ever needed to ask a pardon that mattered. He had rehearsed a dozen beginnings on the long walk from the garden and discarded every one, and now that he stood among the lamps not a word of any of them remained.

He went and knelt on the stone before the new name, so that he was lower than Enzo and had to look up at him. The floor was hard and cold through the silk at his knees, and he felt the chill of it climb into his bones. No prince of Aloria knelt to anyone but the Sun and the King. He knelt all the same.

“Consigliere,” he began. “I have come to ask for your pardon. I learned only a few days ago, by means I shall not trouble you with, that the lady who died at the Sanctuary was your wife. I did not know it when I stood in your hall, though that is no defence. I was frightened that night, and when I am frightened I become clever. So I made jests about vintages and chandlers in your hearing, while you stood across the hall with your lady not two months in the ground, and said nothing. You showed me more courtesy that night than I deserved. I grieve for her, and for you, and for every word of mine you were made to hear. I do not ask your forgiveness, for I have not earned it. I ask only that you know I am sorry, and shall be for as long as I live.”

Enzo was silent a long while. In their niches the flames stood straight up in their bowls, and somewhere deep in the rock behind the walls water was dripping, slow and patient as a dwarven clerk entering sums. Then at last he looked down at the prince kneeling on the floor of his dead.

“Get up, Highness. She had very firm views on young men who knelt on stone.”

Caleb stayed where he was. After a moment, Enzo seemed to accept this.

“Her name was Livia. She kept the Sanctuary. She liked anyone who ate.” He looked at the flame. “She would have liked you.”

It went in under every guard Caleb had. He had come with armour ready for coldness, or courtesy, or the Consigliere’s dry contempt, and had none at all for this. The hundred flames swam together into one soft blur of gold, and he let them, because it seemed to him that here, of all the rooms in the world, a man might weep for a woman he had never met and be forgiven for it.

“You were not at the vineyard,” Enzo said, still to the lamp. “My nephew has had your blood in him, and he says so. I believe him.” He let a silence pass. “That is enough, Highness. Stand up.”

*My nephew.* The Consigliere was the Sovereign’s uncle, then, and Livia had been his aunt. This time Caleb rose. His knees had stiffened with the cold, and he had to put a hand to the wall to straighten them, and his palm came to rest beside a name worn too smooth to read. He sat on the end of the bench, a careful distance from the Consigliere of Vesperia, and for a while the two of them looked at the flame together.

Two niches above Livia’s, the name cut into the stone was *Alessandro*. The flame in its bowl had burned down to a bead of blue, and the rock round the niche was darkened with long years of smoke.

“My brother. My nephew’s father, and Sovereign before him. Twenty years ago, at the Long Eclipse. They never found the men who did it.” He was quiet for a moment. “The Carusos wept very handsomely at his funeral.” He was quiet again. “On the Coast we say that a man’s shadow is the oldest thing about him. Theirs are older than ours.”

Enzo went on looking at the lamp, and the words hung in the warm, resinous air between them. Caleb felt the cold of the bench creep up into his spine and stay there.

Then Caleb asked, because it had been lodged in his chest for five days like a cherry stone swallowed in the nursery, “May I ask you something?”

“You may ask.”

“What did your nephew do to me?”

\* \* \*

Enzo was quiet for so long that Caleb thought he would not answer, and the lamps burned on in their bowls of garnet and smoke and violet, and somewhere in the rock the water went on with its sums. When he did, it was in the dry voice Caleb had heard coming down the crack in the rock, the voice he kept for reading figures into a ledger.

“When one of us bites, there is venom in it. It cools. It quiets the blood and slows the heart. Before the Accord, it stilled what we fed on.” He said this without expression, as he might have mentioned that the Contis had once kept sheep. “Our physicians use it now, in the tiniest measures, for fevers that will not break.”

Caleb was thinking of snow falling on a fire, and of the cold going out from his throat along every vein, and the heat going quiet under it, one place and then the next, like the lamps of a long gallery being put out one by one.

“What you had in the gallery was a fever, of a kind,” Enzo went on. “Your own light was burning through you with nowhere to go. He drew it off into himself, as one cuts a channel to let a river in flood run away into dry ground.”

“There were physicians,” Caleb protested. His voice came out strange. “Surely there was some other —”

“The nearest physician of your own kind is in the Harp Vales,” answered Enzo, “four days’ flight from here. You had perhaps four minutes.” At last he turned his head and looked at him. “He did not stop to consider it, Highness. He considers everything.”

Caleb looked down at his hands. They lay open in his lap, and in the lamplight they looked like someone else’s. He thought of the hands on the glass, one at his wrists and one beneath his head, and of how the second had shaken. For four days among the lilies he had been certain it was hunger.

“We live on blood-wine,” said Enzo, more slowly. “To drink from a living throat is not done. Not between strangers, or in anger, or for hunger. Only between two who have chosen one another, and most of us never in our lives.”

Caleb felt the heat climb his face from the collar to the hair. He said, with enormous dignity, “Then I appear to have been thoroughly compromised on the floor of a corridor, which my mother would consider by far the worst of it.”

Enzo’s face changed. It took Caleb a moment to recognise the change as the Consigliere trying hard not to smile, in the room where he kept his wife’s lamp, on the day he wore ash for her.

“I would not repeat it at dinner,” Enzo said gravely.

The smile went out of his face as quickly as it had come, and what replaced it looked uncommonly like fear. In the niche beside him a flame shivered in its glass, the first that had moved since Caleb came in.

“You heard me in the gallery, I think. Your blood is sunlight, Highness. To us it is poison. A single sip is death, and he knew it when he bit down.”

The lamp room had gone so quiet that Caleb could hear the wicks drinking at their oil.

Caleb’s heart missed, the way it had missed on the glass, and caught again. He heard, as plainly as he heard the water dripping in the rock, the words that had come down the gallery while he lay with his head in Arian’s hand. *It should have killed you.* He had heard them then, through the cold and the shame, and in all the days since he had kept himself from hearing them again. Now there was nowhere left to put them. His hands had found each other in his lap and locked together. The gold had gone out of the backs of them, and in the lamplight they were as pale as the stone dust in Livia’s name.

“Then why,” Caleb whispered, when he was finally able to speak, “is he alive?”

Enzo looked back at the flame.

“That is not mine to tell.”

“Is he —” Caleb stopped, but made himself go on. “Mirek says he has not left his rooms.”

“He has not. The burn is inside him, and his physicians have no cure for it. No one has ever lived long enough to need one.” Enzo said it flatly, and Caleb heard where Arian had learned to speak that way. “If the Council learns of it, the Carusos will have the Accord in one hand and his crown in the other.”

Caleb’s locked fingers tightened until the nails bit.

“He told me that a dead hostage was of no use to him.”

Enzo turned from the lamp to look at him. “Did you believe him?”

“Not at all.”

“No.” The Consigliere of Vesperia rose from the bench and stood a moment looking down at his wife’s name. Then he stooped and, with the side of his thumb, brushed the pale stone dust out of the letters one by one, and straightened. “Nor did I.”

He was almost at the door when Caleb called after him, without having quite decided to say it, “He was frightened. In the gallery. His hands were shaking.”

Enzo stopped with his hand on the doorframe.

“The bite goes both ways, Highness,” he said, without turning round. “Whatever you felt of him, he felt of you.”

Caleb thought of everything he had felt on the floor of that gallery, the terror and the shame and the relief he had hated himself for, and that last foolish longing to find whoever walked the dark house and take him by the hand. He had known, even on the glass, whose house it was. It had not occurred to him until now that its master might have felt him at the door.

The blood went out of his face and left him cold to the fingertips. He had lain open on that floor like a letter left unsealed on a desk, every line of him there for the reading, and never dreamed there was anyone to read it.

“Everything?” he asked faintly.

“I did not ask him.”

Then he was gone. Caleb sat on alone among the dead of House Conti, with his hands still locked in his lap, while a hundred flames burned steadily around him, and Livia’s, the newest of them all, burned low and gold before him.

\* \* \*

He did not go to Arian’s rooms.

Three times in the days that followed he got as far as the foot of the stairs to the Sovereign’s wing, and stood with one hand on the cold stone of the banister, which a thousand years of Conti hands had worn into a shallow dip, and looked up into the dark of the stairwell, where the violet lamps climbed and climbed and turned the corner out of sight. The second time, a door closed softly somewhere far above, and he was halfway back to the garden before he knew that he had turned round. Each time he went back in the end, and was short with a lemon tree that had done nothing whatever to deserve it.

*Thank you* was absurd. *How dare you* was no longer entirely fair. And there was no language he knew in which a hostage could say aloud to his gaoler, *I felt how lonely you are, and I wanted to take your hand, and I should very much like to know why.*

At night, which in the garden was only a word, he lay among the lilies with Vesna’s blanket round him and wondered whether the door of that dark house still stood open, and whether its master could feel him there, and then a hot, uneasy prickle would climb the back of his neck, and he would turn over and pull the blanket over his head like a boy of six.

So he asked Vesna instead. Every morning, in the lightest voice he possessed, the one he had used at the Citadel to enquire after the health of an ambassador’s aunt, he asked her how the Sovereign did. Every morning she answered, “Ask him yourself, *malyshka*,” and went away.

The days went by in the garden as days went by there, without dusk or dawn, marked only by Vesna’s trays and the slow yellowing of the lemons on their boughs. On the eighth day she said, “Better.”

Caleb, who was pouring his tea, missed the cup entirely. Vesna watched him mop the saucer with his cuff, and took herself off without a word.

On the tenth she said, “He is up.”

When she had gone, Caleb went down to the pool at the foot of the water-stair and put his hands into the cold water up to the wrists. Under the water the gold ran so bright along his fingers that the whole pool shone with it, and he held them there until they were steady.

On the eleventh, Valentina Caruso arrived.

\* \* \*

She was four days later than her grandfather had promised, and the whole fortress had a theory as to why.

“So the Council feels the want of her,” said Mirek. “Makes them hungry.”

“So she can make an entrance,” said Vesna.

Caleb, who had grown up among the most accomplished entrances in the known world, thought it was likely both, and was impressed despite himself.

He was invited to the great hall to witness it.

The invitation came from the Council, not from Arian, on a card of heavy black stock edged in silver and still smelling faintly of sealing wax. *The Council of Nine requests the presence of His Highness the Prince of Aloria, held under the Accord.* The secretary who brought it left without waiting for a reply. None was expected. A prince of the Solar House was invited, and entreated, and on rare occasions implored. In all his life he had been summoned by no one but his parents, the King and Queen of Aloria. Whoever had written that card had found a way to do both at once, and knew it full well. Caleb had seen a great many captured standards carried through the Citadel in his time, on days when the court wished to be reminded of its victories. He knew what it meant to be brought out to be looked at.

So he dressed for it.

He dressed with greater care than he had ever dressed for anything, which was saying a great deal. He chose a coat of white silk, stiff with gold thread, that someone at the Citadel had packed for him in tissue, with a thoroughness he preferred not to think about, though it was too fine for anything short of a coronation. When he lifted it from its folds the tissue sighed, and up out of it came a breath of cedar presses and the orange blossom water the Citadel laundresses sprinkled on the linen, and for a moment he stood with the coat in his arms and could not move.

Then he put it on. He chose the waistcoat embroidered with white stars, and cuffs of lace so fine that the air moved through them, and he had Mirek brush his hair until it shone like a chestnut in the candlelight. He left his collar open at the throat. There was nothing there now for anyone to see, and he wished them all to see it. He stood before the black glass, settling his face into its most gracious and luminous expression, the one the broadsheets had been reproducing since he was sixteen, until it sat on him as lightly as the silk. Then he walked down through the fortress to be looked at, with the gold running bright under his skin from nearly three weeks in the garden, and made sure that he would be worth the looking.

The great hall was not dark tonight.

He had last seen it lit only by those few bruised violet lamps, with a desk at the far end and someone sitting in the shadows behind it. Tonight every lamp on every chain was burning, and there were candles in iron stands along the walls, hundreds of them, so that the air shimmered with their heat and smelled of hot wax, and the black columns of volcanic glass had been polished until they threw the light back a hundredfold. The whole of Vesperia seemed to be in the room, and the sound of it rose into the vaulting in a low, unbroken murmur, like the sea heard in a shell. The Nine sat in their high-backed chairs along one side, in silks the colours of old wine and garnet, and behind them crowded the heads of lesser houses with their wives and husbands and heirs.

Along the walls stood the Sovereign’s lycans in Conti black. Among them, taller than any, the grey-flecked sentinel who had torn Paul open met Caleb’s eye as he came in and inclined his head a little, as a soldier does to an officer. Caleb returned it with the coolest courtesy he possessed.

And against the far wall, on the arm of a chair, sat the fae in the coat of iridescent feathers, walking her copper coin across her knuckles. She saw Caleb and her whole face lit with delight, and she waggled her fingers at him, like a girl at a ball.

The room turned to look at him. He felt it turn upon him like the heat from an opened oven. He walked out into the middle of it without hurrying, with his chin up and his hands clasped lightly behind his back, and let every one of them look. Under the white stars of his waistcoat his heart was going hard, and he could only trust that in a hall so full of noise not even a vampire would trouble to listen for it.

Then the doors at the far end opened, and the room turned again.

\* \* \*

Valentina Caruso came in on her grandfather’s arm, and Caleb, who had been prepared to dislike her, saw at once that it was going to take some effort.

She was tall, and dark, and beautifully made, with the Caruso pallor and a mouth that seemed to find the whole room faintly amusing. She wore black, as most of the hall did, but it was a black so rich that it looked almost red where the candlelight caught the folds, and at her throat, on a chain of fine dark gold, hung a single black thorn. She walked like a woman for whom the hall had been built, and gave the Nine in their high-backed chairs the degree of courtesy each of them was owed, and where she had passed the warm air smelled faintly of smoke and crushed violets.

She stopped a few paces from Caleb and looked at him, and Caleb looked back.

He had met a thousand young women across a thousand ballrooms who had been told he might be worth marrying. He knew every variety of the look they gave him. This was none of them. She looked at him the way Eleanor looked at a column of figures, taking his measure to the last decimal, and at the end of it she smiled a little, as if the sum had come out higher than she had expected.

“So you are the Pilgrim Prince.” Her voice was low and amused. “I have seen your portraits. They do you no justice at all.”

“How very kind,” murmured Caleb. “I am told the painter improved upon my jaw. I have never had the heart to tell him that he improved it in the wrong direction.”

Something moved in her eyes that in another life might have been liking.

“Highness.” She sank into a curtsey that was a perfect insult, deep enough to honour a prince and not one inch deeper than a hostage deserved.

Caleb bowed back, and gave her not a quarter inch more.

He heard the fae laugh aloud against the wall. Then the room went still, the way it had gone still on the first night, and every shadow in the hall leaned the same way at once. Caleb felt the air cool on the back of his neck before he had so much as turned his head.

Arian had come in.

\* \* \*

Caleb had not seen him since the night of the gallery.

He had half expected, after eleven days, to feel nothing at all. Instead it was like a missed step on a stair, a lurch beneath the breastbone. Arian came in through a side door at the head of the hall, without ceremony, in black silk buttoned to the throat, and he was thinner. There was a new hollowness under the fine bones of his face, and a darkness beneath his eyes like thumbprints of ash, and his skin had lost its sheen, like marble left too long out of doors. He went to the high seat and sat down in it like one who had walked a long way. The candles nearest the side door bowed as he passed and were slow to straighten, and the shadow that pooled about the high seat lay thin at its edges, like a cloak worn through.

Caleb thought of blood-wine sent up and coming back down full, and of physicians on the stairs at all hours, and of a delivery of ice argued over in low, furious tones. His stomach turned over. He kept his face where he had put it, gracious and luminous, and did not let it slip.

Before he had even sat, Arian had looked straight down the length of the hall to Caleb, and kept looking. His face gave the hall nothing. But the look came to rest on Caleb the way the thumb had rested on his pulse in the gallery, lightly, making sure of him, and did not move on. The place under his jaw where the marks had been went cold, and then warm, and for the length of that look he forgot to hold his face in place.

Then Don Salvatore Caruso cleared his throat, as softly as a man turning a page in a library, and the moment broke.

“Sovereign,” said Don Salvatore. “We are grateful to see you so much restored.”

It was the voice Caleb had last heard drifting down through the old vent above the cellar, dry and unhurried as it had been in the rock, though it came from a face that looked no older than thirty, and he said it with such warmth that nobody in the room could possibly have mistaken it for anything but a threat.

“The Council has asked me to speak,” Don Salvatore went on, “and I shall be brief, for I am old and the matter is simple. The treasury bleeds. The Alorians sit on our border and on our coffers. And the Coast owes the vault-banks of Durnhollow a great deal of money, which falls due at the new moon.” He spread his long, ringed hands. “Or rather, it owes it to me. The Consigliere will have noticed that the notes of the Conti debt have lately changed hands.”

A ripple went through the Nine, a rustle of garnet silk. Enzo, at the end of their row, sat as still as the columns.

“Vesperia needs to look united, Sovereign,” Don Salvatore continued, “and there is no better way under the sky, or under the cloud, for two great houses to look united than to become one house.”

He turned, and drew his granddaughter forward by the hand into the full blaze of the candles, so that the black thorn at her throat glittered like a splinter of jet.

“House Caruso offers the Sovereign its heir,” he announced. “My granddaughter, Valentina, is of age and of the blood, and she is willing. On the day she weds, I shall burn every note of the Coast’s debt at the wedding feast. Let the Conti and the Caruso stand together, as they have not stood in a thousand years, and let the Alorians see it and go home.”

A murmur went around the hall, and Caleb heard it for what it was. The Nine were tired and the treasury was empty, and here was a remedy that cost nothing but a wedding.

Valentina stood straight beside her grandfather, and looked up the hall at the Sovereign of Vesperia, and waited. About the high seat, the shadow that had lain so thin drew in upon itself and darkened, like dusk gathering in a valley before rain.

Arian said, “No.”

\* \* \*

The murmur stopped all at once, as if someone had closed a door on it, and in the silence Caleb heard the fae’s coin chime once against her rings and fall still.

Don Salvatore smiled, and Caleb thought he had expected nothing else.

“The Sovereign is young,” said Don Salvatore, gently. “The Council is of one mind, Sovereign. It is not a question of whether you will marry. It is a question of when. And the Sovereign will recall that a match offered before the Council, and refused without cause, is not a thing House Caruso forgives. Our houses have kept the peace a long while. It would be a great pity to end it over a young man’s pride.”

“No,” said Arian again. “It is not a question of when.”

He stood.

When Arian rose from the high seat, nobody bowed. Caleb had watched his father rise from his throne ten thousand times, and the court had always bowed to him as he rose. Here, instead, everything in the hall leaned towards him. The candle flames along the walls bent, every one of them, towards the head of the hall. The shadows under the chairs of the Nine stretched out across the black floor like hounds straining at the leash. And Caleb, forty paces away, felt the cold reach him where he stood, as the first breath of winter finds its way into a house through the crack beneath a door.

“I am already promised,” Arian said.

If the silence before had been a door closing, this was a house falling in, and nobody moved, and so far as Caleb could tell nobody breathed. High in the vaulting a crystal lamp swung on its chain, and its violet light slid across a hundred upturned faces and back again. Valentina’s face did not change by a hair, but her hand, where it rested on her grandfather’s arm, had gone white at the knuckles.

It was Don Salvatore who found his voice first. He did not sound amused any longer.

“Promised,” he repeated, and his eyes travelled slowly down the hall to Caleb’s left hand, as though he had known all along where they would end. “Then say it, Sovereign. Before the Council. To whom?”

All at once the ring on Caleb’s finger was the heaviest thing he had ever worn.

He had become accustomed to the ring and no longer noticed the weight of it. It had sat there so long that it had become part of his hand, like a knuckle or an old scar. Now, under the Don’s gaze, he could feel nothing else. And it was warm. He did not know when it had grown warm. He curled his fingers over it behind his back, and knew even as he did it that it was far too late, because every eye in the hall had followed Don Salvatore’s down to his hand and found it there.

Arian did not answer. He descended from the dais the way smoke comes down a stair, soundless and slow, and all the shadows followed after him.

He came the whole length of the hall, past the Nine in their high-backed chairs and the lesser houses crowding behind them, past the lycans along the walls and the secretaries with their pens lifted and forgotten, past the fae on the arm of her chair, whose coin had fallen silent between her fingers. He did not hurry, for he never hurried. He came as dusk comes over the Marches, and the crowd opened before him without being asked, and closed again behind him, and every head in Vesperia turned to watch him go.

An hour ago Caleb had walked out into the middle of this hall in coronation silk and let every one of them stare. Now he stood with his hands clasped behind his back so tightly that the iron bit into his finger, and watched the Sovereign of Vesperia come towards him through his own court, and could not have taken a single step to save his life.

Arian stopped in front of him, close enough that Caleb had to tip his head back to meet Arian’s eyes, as he had on the first night.

This close, the cold of him was a weather of its own. Caleb felt it on his face, and on the fine hairs at his wrists, and at the open collar of his shirt, where two marks had lain and faded. And beneath his breastbone, low and steady, came the pull he had felt on the first night in this hall, the tug of deep water at the end of a pier. That night he had set his heels into the stone against it. He found that he had forgotten how.

“To him,” said Arian.

Caleb heard the words and understood each one of them separately, and could not for the life of him make them add up.

Somewhere very far away, Don Salvatore was speaking. “The Accord gives you His Highness to keep, Sovereign. Not to wed.”

“The Accord gives him wholly into my keeping.” Arian did not look away from Caleb’s face. “I choose to keep him as my consort.”

And for one moment the great hall went away.

There was only Arian’s face above him, white and severe and utterly still, and his eyes, so dark that there was no telling where the black of them ended. Caleb had been raised at court. He knew a move on a board when he saw one, and some cool, distant, courtly part of him stood back even now and admired this one, because it was brilliant. In a single stroke it had shut the Carusos out and put a Solar prince at the Sovereign’s side for all the Coast to see.

But he had been on the floor of the gallery and felt the hands that shook, and through two points of cold at his throat he had felt a dark house with every door standing open on an empty room, and someone walking it alone, year after year. He looked up into that still white face and could not tell whether he was looking at a statesman making his move or at the man who walked that house alone.

Then Arian bent his head, and kissed him.

\* \* \*

It was not long. Afterwards, when Caleb tried to remember it, he could never decide how long it had been.

Arian’s hand came to rest at the small of his back, as it had for a heartbeat on the threshold of the garden, and his mouth touched Caleb’s, firm and certain and cool, like the first step into shade after a long hot day. It tasted faintly of iron, and of snow.

Then, instead of pulling back, Arian pressed in for a single heartbeat. It was too slight for anyone in the hall to see, but his mouth grew firmer on Caleb’s, and the hand at his back drew him a fraction nearer, as if something in Arian had leaned towards him the way the shadows leaned, and had not been able to help it. The hand trembled once, the way it had trembled under his head on the glass, and Caleb, who had not thought to move at all, felt his own breath catch against the cold of it before he knew it had happened.

Then Arian lifted his head and stepped back a pace, and the hand was gone from his back, and the whole of Vesperia was staring at them. All along the walls the candle flames, which had leaned towards the two of them the whole while, came upright one by one, like courtiers caught at a keyhole.

Caleb stood perfectly still.

His mouth was cold. The cold lay on his lips like the first frost on a white rose, and beneath it, where the blood was rushing back, they had begun to burn.

He had been kissed before. He was a prince of the Solar House and the darling of the fae broadsheets. He had been kissed at masked balls and on midsummer terraces, and once, in the back of a hired carriage, by the second daughter of a marquess who had afterwards written a very long poem about it. He had never in his life been kissed before an entire court, without warning, by a man who had drunk from his throat eleven days ago.

And under the shock, under the noise of his own heart, he found something he had not expected at all.

He had been sent here as the price of a debt, handed over in his sleep like a parcel by a father who had not said one word to him, and from first to last nobody had asked him whether he wished to go. On the first night he had stood in this hall and understood that he was a thing to be kept. And now, before the whole of Vesperia, someone had stood up and said *him*, as plainly as if there had never been any question of anyone else at all.

He found that he had nothing to say, no quip and no barb and not so much as a raised eyebrow. His famous wit had carried him through a drugged wine, a melted seal and a lycan with his claws out, and now it had simply excused itself and withdrawn. He stood there in his coronation silk with his lips still cold from someone else’s and his heart going like a rabbit’s, and he knew that every creature in the hall could hear it, and he could not even find it in himself to mind.

Arian was looking at him, and to anyone watching, his face was as blank as new snow. But the candles at Caleb’s back had not all come upright, and two of them burned in Arian’s eyes still leaning, the last in the hall to let go. Caleb saw them, and thought, quite distinctly, *He is as frightened as I am.*

Then Arian turned back to the hall.

“The Council,” he said, “may consider the matter settled.”

He walked away up the hall, and the shadows slid after him across the black floor like the hem of a mantle, and he went out through the side door he had come in by. The door closed behind him, and the room broke apart into noise.

\* \* \*

The next part came back to Caleb only in pieces.

There was shouting, most of it from the Nine, and it rang off the black columns and came back doubled. Enzo appeared at his elbow with his face shut like a vault door and said *Highness* in a voice that meant *walk*, and he walked, though he could not feel his feet. At some point Valentina Caruso let go of her grandfather’s arm and came across the floor to him through the uproar, as calmly as she might have crossed a ballroom to claim a dance.

She looked at him a while, and the candlelight slid along the black thorn at her throat. He braced himself for rage, or tears, or a slap, which he would at least have known how to answer.

She smiled.

“Congratulations, Highness,” she said, and sank again into that exquisite, insulting curtsey. “I do hope you find the Coast warmer than I should have.”

Then she was gone, back into the crowd, and Caleb stood looking after her with his lips still burning and understood, with a cold drop of the heart, that he had just been added to a list.

The last thing he saw as Enzo steered him out through the great doors was the fae against the far wall, writing fast in a little book bound in green leather, with her coin singing on the arm of the chair beside her like a kettle coming to the boil. Then the great doors shut behind him, and the noise of the hall shut with them as neatly as the lid of a sewing box, and there was only the cold gallery and the violet lamps and Enzo’s hand at his elbow.

\* \* \*

The broadsheet was on his breakfast tray the next morning.

He had sat the whole night through on the water-stair with his knees drawn up, watching the water fall over the black stone while the crystals blazed on overhead without the least regard for the hour, and he was still sitting there in last night’s silk, creased now past all saving, when Mirek brought the sheet up with the tea, holding it at arm’s length between finger and thumb like a dead mouse, and laid it down beside the scones and retreated to the doors. Caleb looked at it a long time before he picked it up.

It was *The Lantern of Lutèce*, the most scandalous of all the fae sheets, the one his mother had banned from the Citadel twice and which the whole Citadel read anyway. His own face smiled up at him from the front of it, beside an engraving of Arian, very dark and very handsome and quite obviously drawn by someone who had never seen him, and between them, in letters of crimson ink an inch high, *THE PILGRIM PRINCE: BETROTHED?*

He read it through once, quickly, the way one pulls out a splinter. Then he read it again, slowly.

The fae could not lie. Every child in every realm knew that. So the *Lantern* did not say that the Sovereign of Vesperia had kissed the youngest son of Aloria before his whole court. It said that *our correspondent was privileged to observe a salute of a most particular character.* It did not say that the Solar Throne had sold its prince to settle a debt. It said that *the Solar Throne has declined to comment on the whereabouts of a son it continues to describe as on pilgrimage, and the* Lantern *invites its readers to draw their own conclusions.* It was funny, and clever, and every word of it was true, and Caleb could imagine with perfect clarity his mother’s face as she read it.

Then, near the bottom of the second column, he came to two paragraphs that made him set down his teacup, so sharply that the tea leapt into the saucer.

*Readers of a romantic disposition,* it said, *will wish to know that the Prince has worn, since the very night of his arrival on the Coast, a ring of black iron bearing the closed crescent of House Conti and a single empty setting. Our correspondent, who has made a lifelong study of the customs of the Umbral houses, is able to inform them that it is a betrothal ring in the old Vesperian style, of a kind not seen at the Sovereign’s court in three generations. The setting is left empty by tradition, to be filled on the wedding day with a stone of the bridegroom’s choosing. The* Lantern *leaves its readers to consider what manner of hostage is given a betrothal ring on the night of his capture, and in how many realms such a thing is usual.*

*They may also wish to recall that by the old custom of the Umbral houses, a match offered before the Council may be refused without offence only by a party already promised. How long the Sovereign has been so, the* Lantern *will not presume to say. But it notes that the ring was on the Prince’s hand some five weeks before the Caruso heiress set out from the Ashward, the seat of her house.*

Caleb looked down at his left hand.

The black iron sat where it had sat for six weeks, warm and immovable, and in the crystal light the little closed crescent of House Conti glinted on its face like a moon keeping a secret. He remembered the sentries in the galleries whose eyes had gone past their Sovereign to his hand, and the clerk who had bowed to him without knowing why. He remembered the lower yard, and a dozen lycans stepping back one by one from a hostage they could have torn apart without breaking stride.

“Mirek,” he said. “Did you know?”

The boy’s ears went red to the tips. “Everyone knew, Highness,” he confessed miserably. “I thought you did.”

Everyone except him. He had walked the length of the fortress with that hand in his pocket, and been bowed to, and stepped aside for, and never once asked himself why. And far down, in a place he declined to look at, his heart had begun to race.

Caleb put down the *Lantern*, and rose, and went to find the Sovereign of Vesperia.

\* \* \*

Arian’s study lay at the head of the same stairs where Caleb had stood three times in the last week. This time he took them two at a time, with the *Lantern* crushed in one fist and the worn stone of the banister sliding cold under his other palm. The lycans on guard at the top took one look at his face and his left hand and stood aside.

He did not knock. He considered it briefly, and decided that anyone who kissed a man before the whole Council without asking could very well be walked in on.

Caleb had expected the study to be all black glass and violet lamps. Instead it was a long, low room lined from floor to ceiling with books and lit with candles, scores of them, in stands and sconces and on every flat surface that would bear one, so that the whole of it swam in a warm and wavering gold. It smelled of beeswax and old leather and woodsmoke. A fire burned in a great stone hearth, which struck Caleb as strange, and the heat of it reached him at the door and lay against his face like a hand. Beyond the tall window the violet twilight of the Coast pressed against the glass and came no further. Behind a desk of black wood by the window, heaped with papers, in his shirtsleeves, with a pen in his hand, sat Arian.

He looked up as the door opened and laid down the pen. By candlelight, and this near, the hollows beneath his cheekbones were plainer than they had been in the hall, and the hand that let it go was thin enough to show every bone. The fire roared not three paces from him, and still a fine rime of frost had gathered on the inkwell at his elbow.

Caleb shut the door behind him and walked up to the desk and laid the *Lantern* on it, face up, on top of whatever Arian had been writing. Then he held up his left hand, so that the black iron caught the candlelight.

“A betrothal ring. On the first night.”

“Yes.”

“In the old style. Not seen at your court in three generations.”

“Yes.”

“And every creature in this fortress knew it but me. The lycans knew. The fae knew. The Consigliere knew.” He heard his own voice beginning to climb, and for once he let it, since the only witness had more than earned it. “I have been walking about your house for six weeks wearing a proposal of marriage on my finger, Sovereign, and I thought it was a *collar*.”

Arian looked at the ring. Then he looked at Caleb.

“My uncle advised against it,” he said.

It was so perfectly the wrong thing to say, and said so gravely, that Caleb felt his composure give way beneath him like a rotten floorboard, and for one appalling moment he was afraid he was going to laugh. He sat down instead, rather suddenly, in the chair on the other side of the desk, and put his hand over his eyes.

“Why?” he asked from behind it.

He heard Arian move. When he lowered his hand, Arian had only leaned back in his chair, and was watching him with the patient attention he turned on everything.

“As a hostage,” Arian answered, “you belong to the Accord, and any Council may trade you, or bleed you, or send you home to hang. As my consort you belong to my house.”

Caleb saw where it ended, as he had been taught to see the end of a clause before the clerk had finished reading it. “And a house does not take a blood-debt from itself. So the debt for the Sanctuary is closed.”

“Yes.”

“And Don Salvatore? He told the whole hall that his house does not forgive a refusal.”

“He cannot call it one. I was promised first. The whole Coast has seen the ring.”

“And the debt he holds?”

“My uncle is telling the Nine tonight that a Solar consort is worth more to the crystal trade than any Caruso dowry.” A log settled in the hearth with a soft collapse of sparks.

“That,” said Caleb, “is not an answer to how you mean to pay it.”

“No,” Arian agreed.

“Then I confess I do not understand why a house would move heaven and earth to marry a man it so plainly hates.”

“A Sovereign’s widow rules for his child. Salvatore has waited a long time for a widow.”

The fire went on burning in the hearth, and the room felt suddenly much colder. Caleb thought of a bead of blue flame two niches above Livia’s, and of the Carusos weeping handsomely at a graveside, and the warmth went out of his hands.

They were good answers, or most of them were. They were the answers of a statesman, cool and sensible and true. Caleb had been raised by statesmen, and he knew what a true answer sounded like when it was standing in front of a larger one.

“And the kiss?” he asked. “Was that statecraft too?”

Arian looked at him across the desk, and every candle in the room stood still.

“No.”

Caleb had nothing whatever to say to that, for the second time in two days. It was becoming a habit, and he did not care for it at all. His mouth remembered the cold before he could forbid it to. He looked away, at the candles and the books and the fire burning in a hearth for someone who could not feel it, and turned to another matter.

“Your uncle told me what you did. In the gallery.”

Across the desk Arian grew stiller, and the candle flames nearest him sank low in their cups.

“My uncle talks too much.”

“Your uncle talks precisely as much as he means to. He told me what my blood does to your kind.” Caleb made himself look back. “You might have died.”

“I did not.”

It was said with so little interest that Caleb’s hands closed on the arms of the chair, and he wanted badly to come round the desk and shake him.

“That is not the point, and you know it is not.” He took a breath. “I have not forgiven you the floor. I am not certain I ever shall. But I know now that you did not do it for yourself, and I should not like you to think I could take my life from anyone’s hands without a word of thanks. So I thank you, Sovereign, for not letting me burn.”

Arian was silent. Around the room every candle flame leaned towards the desk, and then stood straight again.

“I did not ask to be forgiven,” he murmured at last.

“No,” said Caleb. “I had noticed.”

\* \* \*

He rose and walked once along the shelves while his heart settled, past histories in cracked black calf and ledgers bound in faded red and a long shelf of the Coast’s poets, whose bindings were black to the last volume, and ran a finger along the spines. The leather was cool and soft with age, and his fingertip came away grey with dust. When he turned back he had his face in order again, and his voice, and nearly the whole of his dignity.

“Very well. It seems that I am betrothed. I did not ask to be, and I was not consulted, and I learned the particulars of my own engagement from a scandal sheet over breakfast, which I assure you is not how these things are done in civilised countries. But I am not a fool, Sovereign, and I can see perfectly well that I am safer as your intended than as your prisoner. So I shall be your intended, but you will find me an intended with terms, and I warn you that I have been drafting them since breakfast.”

Arian inclined his head to the very depth that Caleb had inclined his in the hall on the first night, not one hair further.

“Name them.”

“First. I am to have the freedom of the fortress, every door and every stair and every gallery, openly and by your leave, and not merely because no one has yet thought to stop me.”

“Every door,” said Arian, “but the lower yard, unless you have a guard.”

“Paul will —”

“Vesna’s boy. Mirek.”

It was said evenly, but there was an edge beneath it. Caleb let it go and put it away with everything else he was not yet thinking about.

“Mirek, then. Second. I am to be allowed to write to my sisters.”

“My uncle will read your letters.”

“The Consigliere may read every word with my blessing, and correct my spelling, and I daresay he will find a great deal to correct.” Caleb drew himself up. “And third.”

He had meant to say it lightly. When it came to it, his mouth had gone dry.

“Third. If you are going to kiss me again, Sovereign, I should like fair warning first.”

Arian looked at him across the desk, across the scattered papers and the *Lantern* with Caleb’s own face smiling up from it, and the candlelight moved in his black eyes, and he did not smile. He never did. But for as long as it takes a candle flame to dip and right itself, something went over that still face and was gone, and Caleb was never afterwards sure that he had seen it.

“Agreed,” said Arian.

“Well. Good. Excellent. I am glad we understand one another.”

“May I kiss you?”

It was said in the same voice, flat and grave and perfectly courteous, as if he were asking whether Caleb would care for more tea.

“*No*,” said Caleb, far too loudly.

He felt his face go hot to the roots of his hair, and the gold rose with the heat until he could see it shining at his own wrists. Arian did not move from his chair. He considered him a moment longer in grave silence, as a man considers a string that will not hold its pitch.

“Then what,” he said, “is the point of asking, if I am only to be refused?”

Caleb opened his mouth and waited for something to come out of it. Nothing did. He shut it again, and turned, and walked out of the study with his head high, at a pace only a little short of a run, and he did not look back once all the way down the stair.

He could have sworn that behind him he heard something that in any other man he would have called a laugh, and all the way down the stairwell the violet lamps flared up in their glass, like a hearth when a door is flung open on it.

\* \* \*

He went to the garden, because it was his, and because it was the only place in the whole of Castel Nerezza where he was certain that the Sovereign of Vesperia could not follow him. The runes kindled gold under his ring, and the great doors let him in to the warmth and the roses and the running water, and shut out everything else.

He sat on the warm stone of the water-stair among the golden lilies, still in his coronation silk. The water ran down beside him over the black stone. The lemon trees gave off their sharp green scent in the heat, and the light lay on his face and his hands and sank into him as it always did, all but the place beneath his breastbone that was still going too fast. Every so often the question came back to him in that flat, courteous voice. *May I kiss you?* Each time he said something unprincely to the lilies.

He held up his left hand and looked at the empty setting. In the blaze of the crystals a bead of gold light gathered in it, the way the sun gathers in a drop of water, and slid away when he turned his hand. He wondered what stone it would be. He had not thought to ask. He tried them against the black iron in his mind, one after another, sapphire and diamond and ruby and all the jewels he had ever worn at court, and none of them sat right. He wanted very much to know.

He touched his fingers to his mouth. His lips were warm again, as warm as the rest of him in the blaze of the crystals, and the cold that had lain on them was gone, as the marks had gone.

It was only much later, lying back among the lilies with the crystals turning slowly overhead, that it occurred to him that he had marched into the Sovereign’s study, and argued every term of his betrothal, and come away with each of them granted. And in all that time he had not once, not even for a moment, thought to refuse the betrothal itself.

\* \* \*

In the guest wing, in rooms that had been hung with Caruso garnet in her honour, Valentina Caruso drew off her gloves one finger at a time and laid them side by side on the dressing table. They were black kid, fine as a second skin, and she smoothed each one flat before she let it go.

Her grandfather sat by the cold hearth and watched her do it. He had lost a great many things in his long life, and had learned that nothing was gained by shouting about any of them. A single candle burned on the dressing table, and the garnet hangings took what light it gave and returned only a low red gleam, like the last of the blood-wine at the bottom of a glass.

“Well, *nonno*,” said Valentina at last, to the glass above the dressing table, which she had had draped in black on her arrival, because mirrors in a strange house were unlucky. “You told me the Sovereign had no weakness.”

“He had none,” answered Don Salvatore. “Last month.”

Valentina smiled at the draped glass. It was the same smile she had given Caleb across the hall, with a great deal of liking in it, and none whatever of mercy.

“Then he has one now,” she said. “Let us find out what it costs.”
