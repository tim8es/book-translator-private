## Chapter 17: Chapter Sixteen - The Durnhollow Vault

*Item, to the Undergate of Dornhöhle by air, for the cellars of the Holds: three casks, blood-wine of the Coast. Item, one cask, the same, consigned by House Conti, to travel upright and not to be broached. Weight found somewhat less than declared. No remark entered.*

— From a bill of lading of Van Houten & Daughters of Lowmere

\* \* \*

The Coast had not sent its Sovereign anywhere in a cask in a thousand years, and Maartje Bosboom wished it understood, before anybody climbed into one, that Van Houten and Daughters did not mean to make a habit of it.

“It’s the lining, Sovereign.” She had planted herself at the top of the gangway with her ledger open on her arm, and the quay lamps behind her made a lantern of her starched cap. “The hold’s leaded, every plank, on account of the wine, which turns if the sun so much as looks at it. But the hatches aren’t, and a Lowmere yacht over the Marches at noon is about as dark as a dairy. If you’re to go up by day you go up in a cask, and I put my own hand on the bung.” She looked down at the cask on the quay, oak hooped in black iron and roomy enough for a goat to winter in. “I’ve had it scrubbed. It smelled of the Coast’s best for forty years. It smells of the Coast’s best now, only less.”

“It will do.”

“It’ll have to. She sails at the bell.” Maartje shut the ledger on her thumb, and her round brown face lost its shine all at once, like a kettle taken off the boil. “How is he?”

Arian did not need to listen. He carried the count under his own ribs.

“Eight in a hundred.”

Maartje opened her mouth, and shut it, and said nothing, which he supposed was what Van Houten did instead of entering a remark.

The yacht stood on the yacht court at the end of the long quay, a Lowmere freighter of the broad-bellied kind, tarred black from her keel to her rails, with her name in tired gold across her stern. *Spaarzaamheid*. It meant Thrift, and the old gnome of Van Houten’s had named her, and nobody in Lowmere had ever been able to tell whether it was a joke.

He looked back up at the mountain.

Castel Nerezza stood over the harbour against the ash, black on black, with its violet windows lit along its flanks like the scales of something asleep. The guest wing was lit in every window, as if for a feast. The Ashward had not left, and Don Salvatore sat up there in his garnet silk being gracious, while the Consigliere held the fortress with nothing but the law and his own two hands. And at the crown of the mountain, where the old vent came up through the rock under a dome of dwarven glass, the ash had a faint gold light on its underside, like the gleam left in the bowl of a spoon when the honey has been eaten.

He had never once seen the garden from outside. It had not occurred to him that it showed.

He looked at the lit windows of the guest wing a while longer. It would have taken him less time to go up the switchback and put out every lamp in that wing than to climb into a cask, and the Don with the lamps, and every Caruso under that roof who had sat at the Don’s table or carried his word down the hill, and he would have come down again with the frost not so much as stirred on his sleeves. He thought of it without heat, as a carter thinks of the shorter road. It would not have put a phial in his hand.

He left them their lamps.

“The fortress is open, Sovereign,” called the sergeant from the foot of the gangway, as a soldier reports the weather.

“Yes.”

The sergeant nodded, as if that were the answer he had been sent to fetch, and went up the gangway with the two lycans of the escort behind him. Arian had chosen them outside the garden with one look apiece. The two had carried their wounded down the goat track to *Kolyska*, and the sergeant’s heart still had its old catch in it, which was the sergeant’s affair and no one else’s. All three had seen the prince walk.

He climbed into the cask.

He had sat on a great many thrones. None of them had smelled so strongly of what was poured at their feet, and none of them had been quite so frank about what a Sovereign was, in the end, when he was carried anywhere. Maartje looked down at him from the gangway, with the lid hanging ready on its tackle.

“Upright. Not to be broached.” She wrote it in her ledger. Then she wrote something more, and frowned at it, and struck it through, and the lid came down, and the dark was only the dark of oak, which had grain like any other.

He heard her knock twice on the lid with her knuckles, as the Holds knock on a lamp.

\* \* \*

The *Spaarzaamheid* went up at the bell, and north through the ash, and out of it into the day.

He knew when the sun found her. It came onto the deck over his head at the third hour as a weight comes onto a floor, and the lead in her planks kept it out, and he sat in the dark of the oak and felt it walk the length of the deck above him all day, slowly, like a householder going round his rooms at night to see that every shutter is barred.

He had not drunk since the gala. He had tried, on the second night at the threshold, and Vesna had watched him set the glass down full on the stone of the corridor and taken it away without a word. Everything he had put in his mouth since the hearthstone had tasted of a white flower opening in the dark. The hunger had stopped asking some while ago. It had gone down into the roots of his teeth and lodged there as an ache, such as a man gets from biting down on river ice, and it had begun to take the frost off him from the inside, as a fire lit behind a window takes the rime off the glass.

He did not sleep. He counted.

He did not need two fingers on a wrist to do it. A mountain and a strait and half the Marches lay between him and a bed of golden lilies, and under his own ribs the prince’s heart went on, and his own with it, beat for beat. It went for eleven beats and missed. It went for nine, and missed, and caught too hard. Somewhere over the high Marches it missed twice running, and his own missed with it, and for those two beats his chest was as still as it had been through all his boyhood, before a glasshouse.

Then it caught. Both of them caught.

He found that he had put his hand flat against the staves of the cask, and that the oak under his palm had gone white with frost to the hoops.

\* \* \*

They came into Dornhöhle at the last hammer, as the city struck the end of its day and its hum settled lower for the night.

The Holds had a landing for yachts high on the mountain’s eastern face, a long shelf of rock under an awning of riveted iron that ran out over the valley like the peak of a cap, so that a client of the Coast might be landed by daylight and never see the sky. They weighed the cask there, as they weighed everything landed on them. Then the lid came up, and Arian stood up out of forty years of the Coast’s best into the light of one lamp, and the clerk of the landing bowed to him across the scale as deep as the Sovereign of the Coast was owed, to the hair, and wrote one line in his book, and asked nothing at all.

“I’ll wait,” said Maartje. “Van Houten never sails light.”

He took the long stairs down with the sergeant and the two lycans a few steps behind him. The city kept its night. Its runes had been turned low, so that the stair rails glowed no brighter than the bowl of a pipe, and far below, at the bottom of everything, the forges burned in their rings as they had burned when he was nine. A lamplighter on a parapet stopped knocking to watch the Sovereign go by, and went on knocking. Nobody stopped him. The Holds did not stop clients. They wrote them down.

The Hall of the Chain stood open.

It always did. There was nothing in it to steal but promises, and the Holds had never yet found a thief who wanted those. The red runes of its dome had sunk to the colour of a coal raked over for the night, and the apprentice of the windlass was asleep at his crank with his cap over one eye. The chain hung where it had always hung, down out of the dome and into the breathing dark, and its links chimed against each other as it turned.

He had stood at this rim at nine, with his father standing back from it in the dark of the door, and leaned over it as far as his father’s voice would let him to look down after the chain. *Does it go all the way down?* he had asked the Master Shaper, and she had looked at him from under her brows as at a blade brought in with a nick in it, and told him to come back when he had something to hang on it.

He had come back. He had hung something on it.

It was not the topmost link now. A fortnight of the Holds’ promises had gone on above it, coopers and lamp-makers and a widow of the high pastures who had married a smith, and the chain had gone down by a link for each of them. He found it all the same, as he knew the prince’s step on a stair. It hung nineteen links below the top, black and plain, with the marks of two hammer strokes crisp on it, one for each of them, and he reached out over the hole and closed his hand on it.

The thread pulled.

It went tight from the link through his palm and up his arm and into his chest, as it had drawn tight when Hild’s hammer came down the second time, and at the far end of it, a long way south, lying in the gold, the prince’s heart missed. He felt it miss in the iron under his hand. He felt it catch.

He let go.

“Sovereign,” murmured the sergeant from the door, with a lycan at either jamb. “If you’re not up by the first hammer.”

“Then take the yacht home, and tell my uncle.”

“I’ll tell Vesna. Your uncle can hear it from her.”

“Keep the door.”

He sat down on the rim, and swung his legs over, and took the chain in both hands, and went down.

\* \* \*

He went down hand under hand, out of the light of the dome, and the chime of the links went lower and slower as they aged, as a voice does. The breath of the mountain came up past him. He could not feel its warmth. He could see it, shaking the dark above the forges like the air over a road in summer, and the frost on his sleeves did not melt in it and did not mean to.

The shaft opened into arches.

They went round him on every side, ring below ring, and in every arch a hearth stood open like an oven door at baking time, with a smith black against it, and the noise of the hammers came in at him from all sides at once, as rain comes in at a hayloft. A woman of the smiths’ guild looked up from her anvil and saw him go by across the mouth of her arch on the chain of the Holds, hand under hand. She looked at him for as long as it took a drop of sweat to run down her nose into her beard. Then she brought her hammer down and went on with her link. Lutèce would have asked why. The Holds asked only whether, and the Sovereign of the Coast was plainly going down.

The forges went up past him, ring after ring, and were gone, and below them the links were old, rusted to the brown of dead bracken at the end of winter, and here and there two had rusted into one and hung stiff, like the knee of an old man who will not bend it in a temple. He wondered who had promised what to whom five hundred years ago, and whether any of them had kept it. The iron did not tell him.

He counted as he went. Ten. Miss. Eight. Miss.

Then the rock round the shaft turned to iron.

He knew it before he saw it, before the walls of the shaft went from rough black rock to black plates riveted edge to edge, runed and dully gleaming, close enough to touch. He knew it because the dark stopped answering him.

It went all at once, without a sound, as the water goes out of a lock when the sluice is drawn. The shadows of the Coast had come to his cradle and lain on his coverlet, and gone ahead of him down every stair of his life, and leaned to him across every room he had ever entered. Here they did not know him. The dark lost its grain and was only dark, flat and black, the dark a mortal sees when he shuts his eyes, and nothing in it turned when he called.

Only his own shadow stayed. It hung below him down the iron, where it had hung at his feet at every hour since he was born. It was his own share of the dark, the piece of it that would hold his years off him once it set, and the iron could make the Coast’s dark deaf to him but could not take that. He could not see it. He knew it was there as a man knows his own feet in the dark, and he hung above it on the chain, blind, with nothing else about him but iron and the smell of iron.

And under his ribs, nothing moved.

He had known it would happen. He had learned it at Brandt’s oak table on the afternoon he pledged his ring, when his heart had stopped as he crossed the threshold, as quietly as a top runs down on a nursery floor when the child has gone to supper, and come back to him at the door. It was the light in it. A boy had put it there with his two hands in a glasshouse, and cold-iron allows no light, and so in cold-iron Arian had no heart, and was what the tutors of the Umbral houses had always said he was.

He had not known that it would be like this. The prince was missing eight in a hundred in the lilies, a world away, and Arian could not hear it. If the prince’s heart stopped while he hung here, his own would not tell him so, because his own had already stopped, and in the dark there would be no difference between the one and the other.

He went down.

He went down slower now, because the iron pulled at whatever he was made of as a current pulls at a swimmer’s legs, steadily and without malice, and he counted links, since there was nothing else to count. In the iron he had nothing of the Coast in him but the body it had made, and that body had not been fed since the gala. By the fortieth link his arms had begun to shake on the chain, as an old man’s arms shake on a banister at the top of a long stair. Somewhere past fifty his boot went out sideways into nothing, and he felt with his foot and found the edge of an opening in the wall of the shaft, and a sill.

There was a light in it, one lamp on a chain from the roof of a low iron room beyond the opening, and under it, at the same plain oak table the Seventh set in every counting room it kept, with a ledger open before him and his spectacles on their ribbon on his chest, sat Master Ulrich Brandt of the Seventh Chamber, who looked up from his page as Arian’s boot found the sill, and rose, and laid down his pen.

“The Chamber keeps every client’s hours, Sovereign. The Coast’s are only the longest.”

Arian stepped off the chain onto the sill, and into the room.

It was a vault, low and long, iron underfoot and iron overhead, riveted plate on plate, and the one lamp lit the table and a few yards of floor round it as a candle lights a page and leaves the rest of the library to the night. Iron shelves went away from the table on either side, row upon row, further than the lamp would reach. On them, a hand’s breadth apart, as the Holds set out everything, stood caskets of every wood and every size, some no bigger than a snuffbox and some as long as a child, every one bound in iron bars and every one with a number painted on its end in white, and nothing else. The Seventh Chamber kept its clients’ pledges here, at the bottom of everything, a wall’s breadth from the chain, so that whatever a client had given up to the Holds might lie in the dark and listen to what promises cost.

“You were waiting.”

“A cask of the Coast’s vintage was landed at the last hammer,” said Master Brandt, “weighing somewhat less than a cask of the Coast’s vintage, and walked to the Hall of the Chain.” He took off his spectacles. “The Holds weigh everything, Sovereign. The Chamber reads what the Holds weigh.”

Arian was looking past him, down the nearest row of shelves.

It stood on the third shelf at the height of a man’s heart, a casket of black wood barred seven times across with iron, and the number painted on its end was the Coast’s. He had watched a grey clerk carry it in to Brandt’s table on the afternoon he came to Dornhöhle, and he had laid the ring on the grey velvet inside it with his own hand and watched the lid close.

He went to it. He put his hand on the lid.

The wood was cold, and he was colder, and the cold of his hand went into it and found nothing there to answer. Outside the iron the shadows of any room he stood in would have told him what lay under his palm. They had gone to that ring on his hand for eleven years as moths go to the one lit window in a street, and they would have gone to it through seven bars of iron and a wall of oak, and leaned there, and pointed. Here nothing leaned. His own shadow lay at his feet under Brandt’s lamp like any client’s, and went no further, and he could not have told by any sense he had left whether the thing he had worn for eleven years lay an inch beneath his palm or a thousand leagues away.

“The Sovereign will not open it,” said Master Brandt.

He had not moved from his table, and the lamp above him burned without a tremor, with no draught in that room to bend it.

“No,” said Arian.

He took his hand away.

“I gave it by day. I do not take it back by night.”

“The Chamber did not suppose the Sovereign would.”

“Is it well kept?”

Master Brandt was silent for the space of a breath. In any other room there would have been something in that silence to listen to, the dwarf’s heart behind his three plaits, his own heart under his ribs keeping another man’s time. There was nothing in it at all.

“The Chamber keeps what is given into its keeping, Sovereign. The Coast said so, at the Long Hall, before the masters. The Chamber took it kindly.”

It was as much as a dwarf of the Seventh Chamber had ever said about anything. Arian let the silence after it stand, and Master Brandt put his spectacles on.

“The Sovereign did not come down the chain of the Holds by night to ask after a ring. The Sovereign has come for the floor.”

And he looked at it.

Every dwarf who had ever told Oriane de Chardon that there was no Eighth Chamber had looked at the floor while he said it, and she had thought, in seventy years, that it was shame. Arian looked where Master Brandt was looking, and saw that it was only good manners. The Holds had always told the truth about where things were.

It lay in the middle of the vault, under the lamp, a square door laid flat in the iron of the floor, wide enough for a man to go down it with his arms at his sides, with no handle and no lock. Across it, in iron brackets, lay eight bars, each a beam of black cold-iron as thick as his arm, laid edge to edge so close that no knife could have gone between them.

“There is no Eighth Chamber,” said Master Brandt.

Arian went on looking at the bars.

“The Chamber minds nothing that does not exist.” He sat down at his table and drew his ledger to him and dipped his pen. “The Chamber writes it down. The Sovereign will want the terms.”

Arian waited.

“The Holds do not lock what lies under the Seventh. They weigh it down. Whoever would go below lifts the bars with his own hands, as the client who laid his goods there lifted them first. At the first hammer of every morning the mountain lets the bars down again, whoever is beneath them.” He wrote as he spoke, in his square printed hand, without looking at the page. “And should any hand take anything from under a mark that is not its own, the bars come down at once. They come down one after another, at the mountain’s pace, and not the Chamber’s.” He looked up. “The Chamber has never yet seen a client reach the top of the stair before the eighth.”

“Have any tried?”

“Two. The Chamber has them still. The Chamber will not help the Sovereign lift the bars. The Chamber will not stop him. The Chamber will write down what he does.”

“Then write.” Arian bent to the first bar.

The bars had no edge to take hold of. They lay so close that no knife could have gone between them, and he had no knife, only his hands, and so he drove his fingers down into the seam between the first bar and its bracket until the iron had them to the second knuckle, and lifted.

It came up as a bar comes off a barn door on a winter morning when the frost has got into the brackets, with a shriek of iron on iron that went away down the rows of caskets into the dark. The iron did not want to be lifted, and it did not want him. It pulled at him through his palms as the chain had pulled, and he swung it off the door and laid it down on the floor of the vault, and his arms shook. His nails had stayed behind in the seam, ten pale crescents on the black, as the Holds keep everything a client gives them. The blood came after them out of the ends of his fingers, slowly, dark and thin, the blood of a thing that has gone too long unfed, and there was so little of it that it did not fall.

Behind him the pen scratched once and stopped.

The second bar was heavier. So was the third. At the third something in his right shoulder went out of its seat and back into it with a sound like a boot drawn out of deep mud, and he did not stop for it. He counted them as the prince counted a pulse, without moving his lips, and somewhere about the fifth he understood that the Holds had made every bar a little heavier than the one before, as they made every link of the chain a little older, so that a man who came down here should know at every bar how much further he had to go, and how much less of him there was to go with. At the eighth he stood bent over the last bar for so long with his hands on it that the pen behind him stopped and waited.

He thought of a pale face on a folded coat at the foot of a well of light, and of a bed of lilies all turned up to a door.

The eighth bar came up.

He laid it down with the others, and when he put his hand to the edge of the bare door it swung down and away from him on hinges he could not see, without a sound, as if it had been waiting an age for somebody to stop being polite about it. A stair went down. The lamp went down it three steps, and gave up.

“Sovereign,” called Master Brandt.

The dwarf had not risen. He sat with his pen lifted over the ledger and the lamp lying on his three plaits, and his face gave nothing.

“The Chamber does not lend lamps below the Seventh. Below the iron, the dark will know the Sovereign again.”

Arian inclined his head to him, the one bow the Sovereign of the Coast gives a creditor. Then he went down into the floor of the Seventh Chamber of Dornhöhle, which had no Eighth beneath it, and the lamp stayed where it was.

\* \* \*

The stair went round. It went round on itself, cut into the rock, as narrow as the stair of a bell tower, and for the first turn it was iron, and the dark was flat, and he went down it by his hands on the walls like a blind man going down to his own cellar.

On the fourth step his boot came down on something that was not iron, and it gave under him as dry kindling gives. He crouched and felt for it. It lay in the angle of the stair, where the steps had let it fall, and it was bone, a great deal of it, gone light and dry and full of holes, like the pumice the vent throws up on the Coast’s beaches. There was more on the step below. Among it his fingers found a plait of coarse hair still bound in a ring of brass, and the bones of a hand that had closed on something and had never let go. The bars had come down across the door above, one after another, edge to edge, and whatever had been standing in the door had been cut where it stood, and what lay below the cut had come down the stair four steps and stopped.

The Chamber had them still.

He stepped over them and went on.

At the second turn the iron ended.

He felt it end under his boot, a lip of black plate and then the rough rock of the mountain, and the dark knew him again. The shadows came all at once, out of the walls and up off the steps below, as the starving come to a door where bread is given out, and lay along him, and the dark had grain again, every chisel mark on the stair standing up in it as clear as print. He put his hand on the wall and stood, and waited.

On the ninth stair below the iron, under his ribs, something moved.

It came missing. It came hard and late, as a knock comes at a door nobody has answered in years, and then there was nothing, and he stood with his hand on the rock and waited, as the prince had waited at an anvil with his hand on his coat, for a thing to be true or not.

It came again.

He let go of the wall.

Twelve, and miss. Ten, and miss. Nine. He went down the rest of the stair counting, and faster.

\* \* \*

The Eighth Chamber of Dornhöhle was quiet.

The hum had been in every room of the city, in the soles of his boots and the bones of his hands, so long that he had stopped hearing it, as a miller stops hearing his own wheel. Here it was gone. The mountain had shut its hand over the Eighth as a hand is shut over a coin, and there was no sound at the bottom of everything but his own boots and, somewhere, a drop of water falling into water a long way off, once, and a long while after, once again.

It was the black living rock of the mountain, never dressed, cut by dwarves who had not yet learned the use of cold-iron, or perhaps their own letters. The runes in its floor were not the square runes of the city. They were older and stranger, long and leaning, like grass laid flat by a wind, and they woke red under his boots as he walked and stayed lit behind him, so that when he looked back he saw his own way across that floor written out in a line of dull red, and nothing else. The Eighth wrote nothing down. It wrote down whoever walked in it.

And in the middle of it, out of a hole in the roof no wider than the chain itself, the chain of the Holds came down.

It came down through the rock and hung in the quiet, and its links had gone past rust into something black and smooth and glossy, as old bones go to stone in a riverbank. The last of them went down into a ring of the mountain’s own rock that had been left standing when everything round it was cut away, and the stone had closed round the link as a tree grows round a nail driven into it in some other century, until there was no telling where the one ended and the other began.

It went all the way down.

He stood and looked at it, and he was nine, leaning out over a rim as far as his father’s voice would let him, and then he was not, and he went on.

The walls were niches. They went back from the chain in long galleries cut into the dark, row upon row from the floor to the roof, and in every niche there was something, and over every niche a mark was cut into the rock and filled with lead, and nothing was labelled. Here was a sword with no hilt, and a child’s shoe, and a jar stopped with wax with the print of a thumb in the wax. Here was a letter folded so many times that it had become a hard square knot of paper, and a wooden horse with one ear. In one niche stood a whole door with its hinges and its latch, painted green and shut, opening onto nothing, and since the Caruso quarter he had no liking for doors of that kind. Everything the dwarves were paid never to write down lay at the foot of the promises they had written, and he went down the galleries reading the marks.

He found it at the end of the third.

It was a thorn. It was cut over the niche in the old leaning hand and filled with lead, a single black thorn on a stem, and he had last seen it at a white throat in his own hall, on a fine dark gold chain, with a glove split across the knuckle closing on it.

The niche was caged in sun-forged silver.

It was wire, fine and bright, wound across the mouth of the niche close and fast and twisted at every crossing, as the wire is twisted over the cork of a bottle of Lyssois wine that is meant to keep its breath. Behind it, on shelves cut into the rock, stood phials in rows, brown glass and green and a red so dark it was nearly black, all stoppered in lead, and every stopper stamped with the thorn. These were Nan Corrow’s corks, and her mother’s before her, laid down like a cellar in a good year.

And at the end of the second row, among the brown and the green, a phial of white glass.

It was milk-white, like the inside of a shell, and no longer than his finger, and the lead of its stopper had gone dull, and he looked at it through the silver and knew it, as he had known a black link nineteen from the top.

He had once crushed a seal of sun-forged silver in his own hall, for the benefit of a prince in white who had lectured him on seals, and it had blistered his palm to the bone. He had been fed then. He had not fed now in five nights.

He put his hands into the wire.

It sang. He had heard sun-forged silver sing once before, in an open wound in a glasshouse, and he had thought he would never again hear anything so like a voice and so unlike one, high and thin and pleased with itself. It sang in his palms as he took the wire in his two fists and pulled. It went through the white of his skin to whatever lay under the white, and smoked there, and the smell came up into his face, bright and sour as a coin held on the tongue. Here the skin did not trouble to blister, as it had on the seal. It parted along the wire as a wet page parts under a nib, and the wire went down into the parting and found what lay beneath, and sang the louder for it. The wire had been twisted by somebody who knew what would come to it one day and wished that day to cost him, and it held at every crossing, and every crossing bit, and he tore the cage off the mouth of the niche a span at a time and dropped it on the floor in loops that went on singing to themselves on the rock after his hands had let them go. Not all of the loops were bright. Some were dark with what they had taken from him, and those smoked where they lay.

His palms were open to the tendon. When he tried to close his hands he could see the pale cords that close them moving in the open wounds, with the silver laid along them in threads as fine as the wire had been. He looked at them, once, and did not look again.

The rows of phials stood bare in the niche, the Don’s whole cellar. He could have swept them off their shelves with one arm into the dark and left the Ashward nothing to sell to anyone, ever again, and he stood for the space of one of the prince’s heartbeats and wished to, as he had wished on a threshold to cross it.

He had come for one.

He reached into the niche with a hand that would not quite close, and took the white phial from under the thorn.

\* \* \*

The first bar fell.

He heard it come down far above him, a long way up the stair, through the rock and the iron, a single deep note like the first stroke of a passing bell, and the runes of his own way across the floor of the Eighth flared red from end to end.

He ran.

The phial went between his teeth, because his hands were no use for holding anything, and he went back up the gallery along the line of his own red footsteps faster than an echo goes down a well, past the green door and the wooden horse, and the second bar came down as he passed the chain. The third came down as he reached the stair. He took it four steps at a time, round and round in the dark, with the shadows streaming up ahead of him like smoke up a flue, and the fourth bar came down, and the fifth, each deeper than the last, the whole mountain sounding with them. He climbed in the prince’s time, because he had no other.

At the second turn the iron began, and the dark went deaf to him, and his heart stopped.

He went on in the flat dark with no heart in him and nothing to keep him company but his own shadow. The sixth bar came down, as a door slams in a house where a man is the only one awake. The seventh came down so near overhead that the stair rang under his boots and the air pressed down on him like a hand. Four steps below the door his boot went through what the Chamber still had, and it went away down the stair behind him, light and dry and rattling, and he did not stop for it. There was a square of lamplight a little way up, a door in a floor, and the last bar was already moving in its brackets, swinging down across the square, slowly, at the mountain’s pace and not the Chamber’s.

He went up into it.

He went up through the door with his head and his shoulders, and the eighth bar came down on his back.

It came down across his shoulders as a yoke comes down on an ox, and it did not stop. It was the heaviest weight that had ever been laid on him, and it bore him down into the door, and he set his ruined hands flat on the iron on either side of the opening and held it there, with his head bowed and his teeth closed on a phial of white glass, and did not go down.

The silver in his palms ground against the plate. Something in his back that had been made to bend one way was being asked to bend another, and he felt it weigh the question. A man under a weight sets his jaw against it. He could not. His teeth held a phial no thicker than the shell of a wren’s egg, and so he held the eighth bar of the Holds on his back and his two flayed hands, with his teeth as light on the glass as a cat’s on the scruff of her kitten.

He could not see. The lamp was behind him.

Then it was not. Somewhere overhead a chain ran out through a pulley, and the lamp came down out of the roof and hung a hand’s breadth above the iron by his right hand, swinging a little, and he saw the edge of the door, and the brackets, and his own hands, red to the wrist.

“The Chamber does not lift a client’s bar,” said Master Brandt from his table, and made the lamp’s chain fast.

Arian pushed.

He pushed up against the bar with his back and against the floor with his hands, and the silver in his palms sang against the iron, and something in his left side went with a sound like a green stick broken over a knee. The next breath he took came in past it as past the point of a knife, and brought blood up into his mouth round the phial, dark and cold, and he did not swallow it, and did not open his teeth. He came up out of the door a hand’s breadth, and another, and the bar came up with him across his shoulders, grinding in its brackets, and he got a knee onto the iron, and then the other, and threw himself sideways out from under it onto the floor of the vault.

The bar came down into its brackets behind him with a long iron note that went round the vault and out into the shaft and away up it, as if the mountain had cleared its throat.

He lay on his side on the floor of the Seventh Chamber with the lamp by his face, and breathed, and his side did not like it.

The pen scratched.

“Received of the Eighth,” said Master Brandt, writing, “from under the mark of a client of the Chamber, one phial of white glass, stoppered in lead. By the Sovereign of the Coast, by his own hand.” The pen lifted. “The bars down. The Sovereign above them.”

Arian took the phial out of his mouth with two fingers that bent, and closed it into his palm, and the silver sang in his palm round the glass.

“Send the Chamber’s account to my uncle.”

“The Chamber makes no charge for the Eighth.” Master Brandt blotted his page. “The Eighth does not exist.” He looked at the Sovereign of the Coast lying on the floor of his vault by his own lamp, with the frost growing white again along his sleeves, and something went over his flat face under the plaits and was entered nowhere. “The Chamber has seen a client reach the top before the eighth bar.”

“Write it down.”

“It is written,” said Master Brandt.

\* \* \*

He went up the chain with the phial in his teeth.

There was no other way to go. His hands would not close, and so he did not close them. He hooked them through the links wrist-deep and hung by the bones of his wrists and went up a link at a time, with the silver singing in his palms against the iron of every link. The links bore on the bones of his wrists with all of his weight, and the old iron ground there, iron on bone, as a quern grinds when the grain has run out and nobody has stopped turning it. At every reach the broken ends in his side came apart and met again, as a latch lifts and drops in a door the wind is working at. The caskets of the Seventh went away below him with their lamp, and he climbed heartless through the flat dark, counting links, as he had come down.

Then the plates of the shaft gave way to rock, and the shadows of the Coast came back to him, and under his ribs the prince’s heart came back, and his own with it.

Eleven, and miss. Nine, and miss.

He climbed.

The forges came down round him, ring after ring of red and gold arches, and at the nearest the woman of the smiths’ guild was at her anvil still, or again. She looked up as he came by with a phial of white glass in his teeth and his ruined hands hooked through the chain of the Holds, and she shifted her hammer to her other hand and put the right one flat to her chest, over her heart, as the lycans of the Coast do, and held it there until he had gone up past her into the dark.

The links went from black to bright.

Then the first hammer of the morning struck, one great slow stroke out of the deep of the mountain, and went through the chain into his wrists and his teeth and the bones of his face, and above him the dome of the Hall of the Chain went from the colour of a raked coal to red, and the rim of the hole was there, just out of reach.

The chain moved under his wrists.

It went up with a long iron grumble out of the dome, link over link, and drew him up out of the hole as easily as a link is drawn out of a quenching trough, and stopped with the rim at his knees. He got himself onto it.

The Master Shaper of the Holds stood at the windlass in her scorched apron, with the crank in one broad fist seamed with old white burns and a hammer in the other, and looked at him without hurry, as she had once looked at a boy of nine leaning over that rim. Behind her at the door the sergeant stood with his fist on his heart, and the apprentice of the windlass had woken and stood clear of his crank with his cap in both hands.

Arian took the phial from between his teeth.

“Well?” asked Hild.

“It goes all the way down,” answered Arian.

“So it does.” She looked at his hands, and at the frost on his sleeves, and at the way he sat on the rim with his left arm held in against his side. “Silver.”

“Yes.”

She looked at the phial in his fingers, milk-white, with the thorn on its stopper, and asked nothing about it.

“The Holds forgive anything once, Sovereign. Do not come up my chain again.” She let go of the crank and turned back towards her anvil, where the forge had been woken and the apprentice of the bellows was already at work, and the first link of the morning lay white in the coals. “The next time you come into this hall, you will come in by the door, on your feet, and stand on your line like a client of the Holds.”

Under the floor the mountain struck again, and she lifted her own hammer to meet it.

\* \* \*

In the hold, before the sun was up, the sergeant tore his own shirt into strips and laid them on the head of a cask and went back to the foot of the ladder, and Arian bound his hands himself. He did it with his teeth and the heels of his palms, round and round, badly, as a man does a thing he has only ever watched done, and bound the left side after, tight, so that he could feel the two broken ends lying against one another under the linen. The silver went on singing under the binding. Nothing closes sun-forged silver. He had told a boy so once, in a glasshouse.

“There’s wine, Sovereign,” offered the sergeant, when it was done.

Arian looked at the cup in the sergeant’s fist, with the dark steam curling off it. He had been hungry since the gala and had not let himself know it, and now he had no strength left to keep it from himself. It rose in him as it had risen in a black gallery, as water rises in a mine when the pumps stop, and it did not want what was in the cup. It knew what silver wanted. It knew there was only one thing in the world that had ever closed silver in him.

“No.”

He went home in the same cask, with the phial inside his coat, and the sun went over the deck all the long day. Ten in a hundred, now. Eleven. He did not count after that. The *Spaarzaamheid* went south over the high Marches and down the long dark spine of the Coast into the ash with her lamps lit, and once an hour Maartje Bosboom came down the ladder and knocked twice on the lid, as the Holds knock on a lamp, and went up again without a word.

It was the Coast’s evening when she set down on the quay she had taken him from.

He did not remember the switchback road afterwards. He remembered the guest wing going by above him, lit, and a figure in one of its windows in garnet, watching the road. The figure lifted a glass to him. He remembered that too, and put it away where he kept such things, with the window it had been lifted in. He remembered his uncle in the violet dark of a corridor, six paces back from the light, with his face composed.

The iron doors of the garden stood open, as they had stood for four nights, with the gold lying across the sill and the white print of his own hand still on the frame.

He stood on the threshold and looked down.

The prince lay where he had lain, in the lilies by the water-stair, with his hands in the water to the wrist and his head on a folded rug. He was as white as the sacking under him. The light still came out of him under the water, now and then, but so faintly that it barely lit the stair, and his heart went on, eight beats, nine, and was not there, and was not there, and came.

Arian’s own came with it.

Vesna had heard him on the giants’ stair before he was halfway down it. She came up the iron stairs running, with her skirts in her fist, as she had run only once before in his house, and stopped on the mezzanine in the full light, well short of the sill, where the household always stopped short of him, and looked at the red linen on his hands, and her mouth went into a line.

He could not close his fingers on the phial to give it. He bent and tipped it out of his bound palm onto the iron of the sill, into the gold, and stepped back into the dark. It lay there, white glass and grey lead and a black thorn, and Vesna looked at it, and at him, and did not ask what it had cost, and came and took it up.

“All of it,” he murmured. “Slowly.”

Walker was on the water-stair. Arian could hear his heart from the sill, going like a hare’s, high and fast and wrong, as it had gone on every mile of the Holds road a fortnight since, and home by the Fault, and it was Walker who had held the cup to the prince’s mouth for four nights. Any other man whose heart ran like that on Arian’s stair would not have reached the foot of it, and Arian would not have turned his head to watch him go. The prince loved this one. Arian watched him rise and stand aside for Vesna with his big hands empty, and did not call him up, and did not ask.

Vesna lifted the prince’s head into the crook of her arm. She worked the lead out of the white glass with her thumbnail, and set the phial to his mouth, and tipped it.

Arian listened.

It went on as it had gone. Nine, and miss. Eleven. Ten, and miss. He stood on the sill and counted, and in the lilies Vesna counted with him, her lips moving. Twelve. Fifteen. Then thirty, and no miss, and he did not let himself hear it. Then fifty. Then a hundred.

Somewhere past a hundred, the light stopped coming out of the prince’s wrists into the water.

Somewhere past two hundred, his own heart, which had gone missing with the prince’s for four nights and a day, went on beating, slow and even and warm, in the old time a boy had given it in a glasshouse, and did not miss, and did not miss.

At three hundred he sat down on the sill, with his bound hands in his lap, beside the one shadow he had left there, which still lay out across the threshold into the gold.

“You must drink,” said his uncle, out of the dark behind him.

“When he wakes.”

Enzo was silent for a long time. Then Arian heard him take off his coat and hold it out to the sergeant, who came the six paces that Enzo’s blood would not allow him and laid it on the sill an arm’s length from Arian, folded, as a coat is left on the arm of a chair where a boy has fallen asleep, and went back the six paces. After a while Arian drew it round his shoulders with his wrists.

“It held,” said Enzo.

Arian did not ask what. He rested his head on the iron of the frame, beside the frost-print of his hand, and closed his eyes, and listened to the prince’s heart go on down among the lilies, steady, and his own with it.

It closed.

He felt it close as he had felt it open, under his breastbone, petal by petal, only the other way. Each petal came in over the last, rib over rib, as a lady folds her fan when the last dance is called, and with every one the cold went back out of the roads of his blood and drew itself in tighter, until there was nothing left of it but a taste at the back of his tongue. It tasted of lead, and of something cold and green, like the water deep in a well where the ferns grow down the sides.

And the Grace stopped hunting.

It had run up and down in him for four nights, nose to the ground, finding the enemy everywhere and biting wherever it found it. Now it lifted its head, and stood, and found that there was nothing in any of his roads but himself, and turned, and came home. It came quietly. It lay down in his palms where it had lain since he was six, warm, and waited to be wanted.

Caleb opened his eyes.

The crystals turned slowly over him, and the roses on the terrace wall had all turned their faces away from him to the top of the garden, as if somebody up there had said something to them. His head lay in the crook of a broad brown arm that smelled of flour and woodsmoke, and a voice over him was counting in the Wildmarch tongue, low, as a woman counts stitches.

“*Sto*,” Vesna counted. “*Sto odin*.”

“You are cheating.” Caleb’s voice came out of him like a drawer that has swollen in the damp. “I told you so. Wildmarch numbers are shorter.”

Vesna stopped counting.

She sat in the lilies with his head in her arm and an empty phial of white glass in her other fist, and her face went as it had gone on the morning she found him asleep in the garden with the soil still on him, and she put the back of her free hand against his forehead, and left it there.

“His Highness is cool.”

“His Highness is delighted to hear it.”

His hands lay out of the water on a towel on his chest, the fingers puckered white and soft, as they had gone at five when he would not be got out of a gold bath.

“How long?”

“Four nights, *malyshka*.” She took her hand off his brow. “And a day.”

He turned his head, and the thing under it was not his coat.

It was a rug, folded, grey wool with a fringe. He put his hand up to it, and Vesna’s hand came down over his at once and held it there, quite hard, and she leaned down until her mouth was by his ear, as if she meant to tell him to eat something.

“In my linen room,” she whispered. “Under my own key, in the press with the good tablecloths, folded the way I folded it, and nobody’s hand in it but mine. The new one comes up from the seamstresses in two days. I’ll do the lining myself.” Her breath went out of her. “With my own thread.”

“You will need your spectacles,” Caleb teased.

“I’ll borrow some.”

He laughed. It hurt a little, where the cold had been, and the lilies crushed under him smelled green and warm as he moved.

Paul was coming down the water-stair with a cup.

He came down carefully, as if the cup were full to the brim, and knelt in the lilies on Caleb’s other side and held it to his mouth. The water was so cold it ached in his teeth, as if it had run straight down out of the mountain’s heart. Caleb drank all of it.

“Thank you, Paul.”

Paul did not answer. He knelt with the empty cup in his big hands and looked at Caleb’s face, and at his hands with no light coming out of them, and he had the look he had worn in the lower yard with his side torn open, when a prince knelt in his blood and told him to be quiet. Then he got up and went back up the water-stair to the bend above the second terrace, and stood there with his back to them, and lifted his cupped hands to his mouth.

Caleb had seen him pray so every night since Dornhöhle, in the way of the Highland March. Nothing in the garden would carry his thanks off, and there was a mile of mountain between him and any moon, and Paul gave them into his hands anyway. Caleb found that his eyes had begun to sting, and put it down to the light.

“Where is he?” he asked.

Vesna did not answer. She looked up the garden instead, terrace over terrace, past the roses with their faces all turned one way, to the top, and Caleb looked where she was looking.

The doors stood open. In the doorway, at the edge of the gold, the Sovereign of the Obsidian Coast sat on the sill with his back against the frame, as he had told Caleb once he sat there every night, with his boots in the dark of the corridor and his head laid back against the iron.

There was a coat over his shoulders that was not his. It was dove grey. And his hands lay in his lap, and they were bound up in linen to the wrist, round and round, and the linen had been white once.

“He came in at the seventh bell with that in his hand,” said Vesna, and turned the empty phial over in her fist so that the black thorn on its stopper caught the light. “And his hands like that, and he’s not moved off that stone since, nor drunk, nor gone up to be seen to.” She sniffed. “He went to Durnhollow and back in two nights and two days. In a wine cask.”

Caleb looked at her.

“In a —”

“Maartje Bosboom told me so herself on the kitchen stair, and wept all down her apron, and ate four scones, and went back to her yacht.” Vesna laid the phial down in the lilies. “There’s nobody on this mountain with any sense. I’ve said so for years.”

He got up.

His legs had opinions about it. He let them have them, and stood in his shirtsleeves among the trampled lilies, with Vesna’s hand under his elbow, and the gold of the crystals came down on him and went in and stayed, as the light fills a room when the shutters are put back, and the gold came up in his own skin to meet it, warm and slow and his own.

“His Highness is not to go up those stairs,” said Vesna.

“His Highness is going up those stairs.”

“His Highness hasn’t stood up in four nights.”

“Then he had better begin,” said Caleb, “before he forgets how,” and went.

Paul came down from the turn of the water-stair as Caleb reached the foot of the iron stairs, quickly, with his hands held out a little from his sides, ready to catch, as they had been ready on a Citadel stair the year Caleb was fourteen.

“No, Paul. Not this time.”

Paul stopped. He stood on the terrace with his hands out, and then he let them fall.

Caleb climbed.

He climbed the iron stairs of his own garden as he had climbed them on the first morning, with his hand on the rail, past the lemon trees and the white roses spilling over the terrace walls, and every flower in the garden had its face turned up the way he was going, as if they had all been waiting four nights for somebody to go up and see. Halfway he had to stop. He stood with his breath short and the light pouring down past him, and looked up, and the man on the sill had opened his eyes, and was watching him come.

He went on.

At the top of the last flight he crossed the mezzanine, and the light went with him to the sill and lay down across the iron in its long gold line, and stopped. Arian sat on the far side of it, in the dark of the corridor.

He looked terrible.

He looked as a candle looks that has burned all night in a draught, hollowed on one side and running down. The frost on him had gone thin and grey. His face had fallen in under its bones, and a rim of crimson came and went round the black of his eyes, like the glow at the edge of a coal that somebody keeps blowing on. Under Enzo’s dove-grey coat he held his left arm in against his side, and in the dark stains on the linen round his hands, here and there, something glittered. Where his fingers came out of the binding they had no nails.

Caleb stepped over the sill.

He stepped out of the light into the cold violet dark of the corridor, which an elf may do whenever he pleases, and knelt on the black stone in front of the Sovereign of Vesperia, close, so that the gold of his own skin was the only light on that side of the door.

“You look terrible.”

Arian was a long moment answering.

“I said that to you.”

“You did. I have been waiting the better part of two months to say it back, and you are most provoking about it. You always look splendid.” He took one of the bound hands in both of his, carefully, and turned it to the light coming out of him, and saw what glittered in the stains. “Silver.”

“Yes.”

“Was there silver on the bottle?”

“Round it.” Arian’s voice was thin, and dry, and perfectly level.

Caleb looked at what the silver had left in the palm, a bright tracery worked in to the tendon in lines as fine as wire, crossed and crossed again, and could read for himself how it had come off.

“With your hands.”

“Yes.”

Caleb laid the hand back in Arian’s lap, gently, as one lays down something that has been dropped and might break.

“You have not fed.”

“No.”

“Since?”

“The gala.”

“You went to Durnhollow in a wine cask,” said Caleb, “and you did not drink the wine.”

“It was for the Holds.”

“It was for you, you impossible man, and the Holds would have forgiven it. They forgive anything once. I am told so by a great many people.” He sat back on his heels. “You are starving, with silver in both hands and something broken in your side, and you have sat on that stone since the seventh bell in your uncle’s coat for the satisfaction of not going upstairs.”

The crimson came round the edges of Arian’s eyes, and went.

“Yes.”

Caleb looked at him. Then he put up his hands to his own collar.

It was only a shirt, after four nights in the lilies, with no jet buttons and no black crescent worked over the heart. He undid it at the throat, one button and the next, and laid it open, and turned his head to the side and a little back, as it had once been turned for him on the floor of a black gallery by a hand that would not be argued with, and held it there himself.

He felt Arian go still.

It went out of him into the corridor. The violet lamps went down in their cages all along the wall, one after another, and the one long shadow that had lain on the sill for two nights pointing down into the garden lifted, as a hound lifts its head from its paws.

“No,” said Arian.

“You have not heard what I am offering.”

“I heard your heart on every stair.”

“Then you know that it means it.” Caleb did not turn his head back. He spoke to the far wall of the corridor, lightly, as he had once spoken to the wall of a gallery about creditors and bills. “I have been fattened on scones for ten weeks in a paddock of white roses, Sovereign, at ruinous expense, under twelve stones that my sister priced to the penny. It would be a scandal to waste me. The Treasury would never forgive either of us.”

“The Accord —”

“Is broken in that part already. I was there. I made a speech. I believe you heard it.”

“It is not done,” breathed Arian, so low that the lamps nearly had it, “save between two who have chosen one another.”

“I know. Your uncle told me so, the week after the gallery, and I sulked about it in the garden for days.” He turned his head then and looked at him, a hand’s breadth away in his own gold. “I chose. I said so to Hild with the iron in the fire, loudly enough that a clerk wrote down a word after it. I said so before the Nine in your hall. I am saying so now, on a stone floor, in my shirt, which I think counts for more than either.” He very nearly smiled. “You very seldom ask, your uncle says. Ask me.”

The frost on Arian’s lashes had stopped being grey.

“May I?”

“Yes,” said Caleb.

He took Arian’s face in his two hands, as Arian had once taken his on the glass of a gallery, and drew him down.

Nobody held his wrists. His hands were in Arian’s hair, black and cold and full of frost, and they were holding him there, and he tipped his head to the side of his own accord and felt Arian’s breath on his throat, and it was not cold at all.

There were two points of cold, like the first two stars that come out over the high passes.

He did not cry out. He laughed, under his breath, because it was so much less than he had braced for on a gallery floor, as a child laughs when the tooth he has been dreading for a week comes out in the apple. And then the cold came into him.

It came slowly, out from his throat, along the roads that the bloom had burned and the Grace had just finished mending, and it found nothing in him to put out. There was no fire in him now. There was only the warmth of a man who had been lying four nights in the light, and the cold came into it and lay down there, as snow settles on the roof of a house where the lamps are lit, and the house went on being warm under it.

He felt Arian drink.

He felt it as a pulling, deep and slow, and this time his body did not betray him. It went quiet in Arian’s arms because it wished to, and he wished it to, and the two of them were for once entirely of one mind about something. The light went with the blood. He felt it go, and he kept it low and gentle, as one breathes on cold hands, so that it should warm and not burn, and he felt it go into the cold and be taken, and not burn, and stay.

Then the bridge opened.

It came the way a sound comes through a wall, as it had come once on the floor of a gallery, and then all at once it was in him and he was in it. It was the house. He knew it. He had stood at its door once, pinned and bleeding, and found every room of it shut up and empty. He had gone into it once in a cave with a lamp in his hands, and found it on fire.

Tonight every door in it stood open. In every room a lamp had been left burning, low, as lamps are left for someone late home, and in every room there was something of his. There was a grey-bound *Lantern* with a child’s pencil line beside a column about cake. There was a chair of wheat-coloured velvet by a fire nobody could feel, and a book of recipes floured at the page for scones, and a primer stained along its lower edge by a flooded ford, and a garden that smelled of warm glass and wet earth, and a grey cloak folded on linen in a drawer. He went through the house from room to room, and he was in all of them, and had been for twenty years, and had not known that he lived here.

At the end of the house there was a room he had walked into once without knowing it was locked. Its door stood open too. There was nobody in it but a boy in a black coat lying in the roses, and the boy was not bleeding, and he was asleep.

Against his throat, so low it was hardly a sound, Arian said a word.

He said it once, quietly, as a door is opened onto weather that has turned fine by a man who has stood a long while with his hand on the latch. And Caleb knew it. He had not known it on the floor of the gallery, with the fever in him and the fear, but it was the second word in any grammar of the Coast that a child is given, after the word for night, and he had learned it at fourteen from a tutor with a cold, and it had only been waiting for him to be well enough to hear it.

*Mio.*

“Yes,” whispered Caleb, into Arian’s hair. “Yours.”

And the silver came out.

He felt it come. It rose from the hands in Arian’s lap, through the linen, in beads as bright and round as the silver comfits on a wedding cake, and the beads fell onto the iron of the sill and rang there, each with one last thin note, like a voice that has been singing in another room a long while and has at last been asked to stop. Against Caleb’s chest something in Arian’s side settled and was still.

Arian lifted his head.

He lifted it when he had had enough, and not before, and he did not have to tear himself away. The blood at the corner of his mouth was glowing, a soft gold, like a lantern seen through fog. The crimson had gone out of his eyes. They were only black, and Caleb could see himself in them, two flecks of gold, as a lit window shows in a millpond at night.

Arian laid two fingers on the place he had bitten. Caleb felt the coolness go in, shadow poured into the two marks, and the sting go out of them. Then the thumb came down, and rested on the side of his throat, over the pulse, and stayed.

“Thank you,” said Arian.

“That is seven.” His voice had gone somewhere and had not quite come back.

“I know.”

He had been carried into this fortress drugged, and kept alive under twelve suns in a garden made to smell like a day twenty years gone, and he had been fed on, and bled for, and sealed to before the Holds, and fetched back out of the dark by a man who had crossed the Marches twice in a wine cask for him, and every night of it, every single night, that man had sat down on this sill and asked for nothing, and now, now with the silver lying on the sill like spilled comfits and the frost going soft on Arian’s lashes against his cheek, now with his own heart going steady under that cold thumb and another heart going with it against his breast through the black silk, beat for beat, he found that it was not a thing that needed deciding any longer, and had not been for some time.

“I love you,” said Caleb.

Behind him, at the bottom of the garden, all twelve crystals turned on their silver chains at once, as if a hand had gone along them, and chimed. It was one note, clear and cold and gold, and it went up the throat of the old vent and out under the dome into the ash, and the ash was slow to let it go.

The thumb on Caleb’s throat stayed where it was. The frost on Arian had begun to change, all over him, on his hair and his shoulders and the dove grey of his uncle’s coat, not melting, only catching the gold that came off Caleb as dew catches the morning, until he knelt there at the edge of the light all lit with it like a tree after an ice storm when the sun gets into it.

“I have loved you,” said Arian, “since I was twelve years old, and lying in the roses, and you told me that you were not afraid of me.”

He stopped. The thumb pressed, a little, on Caleb’s pulse.

“So there,” said Arian.

Caleb laughed. He laughed with his forehead against Arian’s, on his knees on the stone of a corridor, until the gold came up out of him so bright that the violet lamps along the wall leaned in their cages to look, and somewhere far below in the lilies Vesna said something in the Wildmarch tongue that was either a prayer or the opposite of one.

“May I carry you?”

“I walked up. I can walk.”

“Yes.”

Caleb considered him, the hollows already going out of his face as if somebody had lit him from inside, and the side he no longer held his arm against.

“Carry me. I should like to know what it is like when I am awake.”

\* \* \*

The study had been put straight round the hole he had left in it. The fire was lit for nobody, as high as ever, and the gold chair stood before it with the grey cloak folded over its arm, and the scorched rug was gone and there was a new one, and the Lady Ysolde lay face down on the footstool, still waiting at the iron doors for her key.

Arian did not put him down in the chair. He carried him the length of the long room, past the green books and twenty years of the *Lantern*, to the door at the far end that Caleb had never once seen open, and opened it with his shoulder.

It was the dark of a room where nobody had ever needed a lamp. Caleb could see nothing of it but what his own skin showed him, a yard of black floor and the edge of a bedpost, and the cold of it was deep and old and still, the coldest place he had yet found in all that cold fortress.

“There has never been a lamp in here,” said Arian.

“You do not need one.”

“No.”

“I do.” Caleb let his light come up.

He did it slowly. He had spent his whole life letting it go, at a wound or a sunrise or a sister, as it pleased itself, and had only these last weeks begun to learn that it could be asked. He asked it now. It came up out of him gold and quiet and went out over the room as warmth goes over a cold floor from a hearth just lit, and the room came up out of the dark to meet it.

It was the plainest room he had seen on the Coast. There was a bed, wide and low, of black wood without a single serpent carved on it, hung with curtains of grey, and a chest, and an empty chair, and a window of smoked glass with the Coast’s violet laid against it like a pressed flower. And there was frost on everything, fine and white and undisturbed, as frost lies on a field nobody has walked across, and it lay thickest on the canopy over the bed, where the cold of a man who did not sleep a great deal had gone up on all the nights he had lain there, and settled, and never once been melted.

Caleb’s light went up into it.

It went into every grain of the frost on that canopy and lit it from within, one grain after another, gold and white and faintly blue, until the whole of it hung over the bed like the sky over the high pass on the second night of the journey north, thick with light and trembling.

“There,” said Caleb.

Arian was not looking at the canopy. He was looking at Caleb.

“Yes.”

Caleb had seen that face by violet lamps and by the white noon of a cave, across a Council table and across an anvil, and it had given nothing to any of them that he could be sure of. It gave nothing now. But he had never before seen it by a light of his own making, and in this light it seemed to him only unread, like a page written in a hand he had not yet been taught, and he had been a scholar far too long to leave a page alone.

\* \* \*

He undid the linen on Arian’s hands first, because he was a physician before he was anything else, and laid his palms over the wounds the silver had left. They were open to the tendon and clean now, and the Grace went into them as water goes down a channel it has cut before, and closed them, as it had closed a sun-wound in a cave. He laid his hand on the side that had broken and knit it, and Arian let him, standing in the starlight of his own frost with his healed hands held a little away from him, as if he did not yet know what to do with them.

Caleb knew what to do with them. He put them on the buttons of his shirt.

“May I?”

“Are you going to ask every time?”

“Yes.”

“Then I shall say yes every time,” promised Caleb, “and you may have it written down in the book of the Holds, with a word after it.”

Something went out of Arian’s face. It went at the brow first, so faintly that it might have been only the frost overhead shifting in the light, a smoothing such as comes over a field of snow when the wind that has worked at it all night drops at last. Caleb, who had looked at that face for two months and caught it moving perhaps twice, and never been sure of either, found that he had stopped breathing to watch, and set himself at once to make it happen again.

The shirt went, and the dove-grey coat, and the black silk under it, button by jet button, every one asked for and every one given. The shadows left the walls and lay over the two of them as they went down onto the bed under the frost, soft and dark and heavy, a second coverlet, and Caleb’s light went up through them and lit them from within, so that they lay in a dark that glowed, as embers glow under a skin of ash. Where his hands went on Arian the frost did not melt. It took the gold and kept it, and where Arian’s hands went on him, cold and careful and no longer shaking, the gold rose under his skin to meet them and followed them, as the flowers in the garden had followed the sound of a name.

In the glowing dark he began to learn the face, as he had learned the Coast’s grammar at fourteen, a word at a time and with his whole attention. The brows drew together when his mouth found the hollow of Arian’s throat, as if at a pain too fine to place, and the mouth went soft at its middle before anything else in it did, as a frosted pane goes clear first in the one place where somebody has breathed on it.

He had thought, when he let himself think of it at all, in the carved bed in the east wing and the cupboard bed at Dornhöhle, that it would be like being cold. It was like standing at the very edge of the light on the first morning of the world and being told that he might go further, and going.

Arian asked again, at the last, with his forehead against Caleb’s and his breath going white and warm between their two mouths.

“Yes,” Caleb breathed. “Yes. Write it down.”

And Arian came into him, slowly, as the tide comes up a long shore at evening, and the thread that a smith’s hammer had drawn through Caleb’s chest at an anvil in the Hall of the Chain went tight, and he felt it twice. He felt it in himself, and he felt it come back to him along the thread from the other end, as if he were standing on both sides of a door at once, and he did not know any longer, and did not care to know, which side was his.

Above him, in the light of the canopy, Arian’s face came open. It happened all at once, as the Coast’s violet would come open if the ash over it ever parted, and for the length of a breath nothing in it was kept back, and Caleb could read every word of it, and it said only the one he had been given against his throat.

Under his palm, on Arian’s chest, the heart went with his own.

It went beat for beat. When his quickened, it quickened. When he caught his breath, it caught. It climbed with him as if the two of them were going up the same stair in the dark with their hands on the same rail, faster, and higher, and the gold went up out of him into the frost of the canopy until every star in it blazed, and the cold of Arian went all through him and put out not one grain of him, and the warmth of him went into Arian and did not burn. And at the top of the stair there was a moment in which he could not have said where his light ended and the dark began, or whose heart was beating in that room, because there was only one.

Then there was the bed again under the frost, and breathing, and the stars over them came slowly down from white to gold.

\* \* \*

Afterwards Caleb lay with his ear on Arian’s chest, and counted.

He had promised. Every night, to three hundred, as the Vales teach. He counted now, under his breath, with the frost of the canopy going gold and quiet over them and Arian’s hand in his hair.

The heart under his ear kept his count.

At a hundred and forty, out of the scholar’s itch that would always be in him, he thought of the hearthstone and the bloom and four nights in the lilies, and his own heart went quick and hard, and under his ear the other went quick and hard with it, as if it had been there too. Then he thought of the word against his throat, and his heart steadied, and so did the other.

He lifted his head.

“It is mine.”

Arian looked down at him in the gold of the frost. His face had gone still again, as still as it went in the hall, but Caleb had learned some of its words in the night, and could see them under the stillness now, as writing shows through a page when there is a light behind it.

“Yes.”

“Since when?”

“Since a little after seven,” answered Arian. “You were counting, in the glasshouse.”

Caleb put his head back down, because he did not trust his face.

“You asked me to count,” he murmured, into Arian’s chest, “on the night you went. Every night, to three hundred. You would not tell me why.”

“Because it is not only yours,” said Arian.

Caleb lay and listened to it. It went on under his ear, slow and warm and in no hurry, in the time a boy of eight had counted out loud in a glasshouse with mud on his knees, and he counted it to three hundred, all the way, for both of them, and then, because he could, he went on.

Arian’s right hand had come to lie on Caleb’s chest, over his heart, and his thumb turned, as it had turned in a carriage on the Fault road and in a cave and at a desk, a quarter turn, at nothing.

Caleb took the hand. He looked at it in the light of the frost, long and white and healed, with its two gold rings and the third finger bare. He kissed the bare finger.

“It will be sold at the Eclipse,” said Arian. He said it quietly, as he said everything that cost him most. “To whoever bids.”

*It will not*, thought Caleb. *It is lying in my coat in Vesna’s linen room, under the good tablecloths, behind a black crescent, and it has been lying against my heart since a night in Dornhöhle, and there is nothing in the Seventh Chamber for anybody to bid on but a casket and a number.*

He could have said it. He had it ready, as he had had it ready once on a balcony over the forges. He looked at the finger, and thought of how its owner would look at him if he were told, and of the word he would say, and Caleb would be obliged to say *That is eight*, and would never in his life catch up.

Not in the dark, as a thing to be thanked for in bed, like a bottle brought up from the cellar.

“Do you mind?” he said instead.

“I minded the hearthstone.”

Caleb laid the hand back on his own chest, over his heart, and held it there.

“You have taken your hand off your heart,” said Arian, after a while.

“Have I?”

“You sleep with it there. I thought you were keeping a place.”

“I was. There is somebody else’s hand on it now. I find I need not keep it myself.”

\* \* \*

The Coast’s morning came to the window as it always came, a little lighter in its violet, as if somebody had thinned the ink, and with it, at the door of the study, a knock.

It was a very correct knock. It was the knock of a man who has stood outside a door for some time deciding how correct to make it.

Arian rose. He went out through the study with a black coat thrown round him and nothing on his feet, and Caleb heard the outer door open, and his uncle’s voice, dry and level and pitched to carry as far as the second door and no further, which meant that he meant it to be heard there.

“The Nine sit at the third bell,” said Enzo. “On the breach of the Accord. They require the Sovereign to answer it.” A paper rustled. “By card. The lord of the inland vineyards wrote it in the cellar himself.”

“And the prince?”

“They do not ask for the prince.” A pause. “Don Salvatore has sent oranges.” Another. “He sent the first basket back to the harbour. They were not sweet enough.”

Arian said nothing.

“Walker sat all night on the stair below,” said Enzo, “with his hands open.” The paper rustled again, as a man folds a thing he would sooner have burned. “The *Lantern* is in from Lutèce. The gala is on its first sheet, and the Eclipse on its last.”

Caleb had watched Paul pray at the turn of the water-stair only the evening before, with his hands cupped to his mouth and closed over his thanks. He lay and wondered what it meant in the Highland March, when a man sat all night with them open.

“Thank you, Zio,” said Arian.

The door closed. That one, Caleb decided, did not count.

He lay under the gold frost of the canopy with his hand over his heart, where somebody else’s had lain, and looked at the window. The violet stood in the smoked glass, a little thinner than it had been, and the ash drifted down past it as it had drifted on the morning his sister sailed. Down in Vesna’s press, under her key, a ring lay in a scorched coat, waiting for a new one.

And above all of it, over the ash and the Coast and the Marches and the white noon of the Citadel, where nobody on the Coast could see it, the moon had begun its long walk towards the sun.
