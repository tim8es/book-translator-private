## Chapter 9: Chapter Eight - Durnhollow

*To a crown prince of any realm, the bow of the third degree. To a prince of the blood who is not the heir, the bow of the fourth degree. To any client who has closed his account with the Chamber, the bow owed to his rank, and not one hair less.*

— From the Tariff of Courtesies of the Seventh Chamber of Dornhöhle

\* \* \*

The Holds had never let the sun decide who might call on them.

Caleb learned it within the hour. He had supposed that they would wait out the day at the waystation and cross the valley at dusk, and he had resigned himself to it with good grace and a second breakfast, but the convoy did not wait. Behind the waystation, where the ledge ran back into the mountain and the snow lay blue in the rock’s shadow, there was a door. It was iron, rimed white with frost along every rivet, broad enough for two carriages abreast and cut from edge to edge with runes, and when Mirek knocked on it with the pommel of his new sword, since nobody had told him not to, the runes woke one after another from the bottom up, gold through the frost, like the windows of a hill town lighting at dusk, and the door swung inward on a road that went down.

The Sovereign came out to the carriages along a passage of riveted iron that ran from the waystation door to the foot of the carriage step, walled and roofed and without a chink in it anywhere for the sun to find. The Holds had built it, Mirek said, for clients of the Coast who could not cross a valley by daylight, and they charged for it by the yard.

Caleb was already in the carriage with the black coat folded on his knees. He had folded it twice, and then shaken it out and folded it again more neatly, and was proud of neither.

“You left this.”

Arian looked at the coat, and then at him.

“Yes.” He took it, and laid it across his own knees instead of putting it on.

The road ran down under the valley for the better part of an hour. It was broad and smooth and as straight as a bowstring, and the runes along its walls slept dark until the carriage reached them and woke gold as it passed and dimmed again behind, so that the convoy carried a travelling dawn down the road with it, and the cold black night of the rock closed up again everywhere it had been. Once, where the road ran level and the engine quietened, Caleb heard water, far above them through the rock, the river going on down the valley over their heads, and he tipped his face up to the roof of the carriage like a child listening for rain.

And under everything, growing as they went, was the hum.

He had heard it from the ledge at sunrise. Down here it was everywhere. It came up through the iron wheels and the floor and the grey velvet into the bones of his legs, a deep, slow, patient note, like the lowest string of a Vales harp left sounding after the harper has gone home to supper. Under the floor slats the violet glow of the engine trembled with it, and the tea in Vesna’s flask shivered against its stopper. His pencil rolled off the notebook on his knee and across the seat, and Arian put out a hand without looking and stopped it, and laid it back on the page.

“The forges,” said Caleb, working it out as he said it. “Do they never go out?”

“Not in the memory of the Holds.”

Then the road turned, and climbed, and came out into the heart of the mountain, and Caleb forgot the pencil.

Durnhollow had a face, and he had seen it at dawn, burning gold on iron across the valley. It also had a heart, and this was it. The mountain was hollow. Long ago someone had looked at a mountain and seen a city inside it waiting to be let out, and had gone in after it with hammers, and the city had come out to meet them tier by tier. It rose on every side of a vast dark gulf, gallery above gallery, cut from the living rock and faced with iron, so many and so high that the uppermost were only a scattering of lamps, like a town seen across a valley at night, and the black rock between them glittered wherever the light touched it. Every lintel and stair rail and parapet was cut with runes, and every rune was lit, a deep warm gold like dark ale held up to a lamp. They ran round the doorways and along the balustrades and up the faces of the pillars, line upon line of square Holdish letters, until the whole city seemed to be written on itself, a book whose pages were also its walls.

Across the gulf, from tier to tier and at every height, went the bridges. They were iron, slender as wire at that distance and lit gold along their parapets with runes, and there were dozens of them, crossing and recrossing the dark. Caleb had been trained by the physicians of the Harp Vales, and he looked at the bridges and saw stitches, close and even, set across a long wound to hold it closed while it knit, and thought that the mountain must have been opened wide, once, and that whoever had sewn it up again had known their work.

At the bottom of the gulf, far down, further than he could see, was the light of the forges. It came up red and gold and warm out of the depths and lay along the undersides of the bridges and the lowest galleries, so that the mountain seemed to keep its sunset in the cellar.

The air smelled of hot iron and coal smoke and wet stone and, from somewhere near at hand, new bread.

\* \* \*

The carriages drew up in a broad court at the edge of the gulf, under an iron arch whose runes threw a warm gold light across the flagstones, and Caleb climbed down into the hum and the light.

Dwarves were everywhere, and all of them were busy. They went by with handcarts and mules and ledgers, their beards white with stone dust or black with soot, bundles of iron rod on their shoulders and spectacles pushed up on their foreheads to flash in the light of the runes, and every one of them, as they came level with the Sovereign’s carriages, stopped at a certain distance, bowed to a certain depth, and went on. Not one stopped a step nearer than the dwarf in front, and not one bowed a hair lower.

“They measure it,” whispered Mirek at his shoulder. He had his bandaged hand pressed to his chest, and his eyes were enormous. “My father told me. Everything. How far you stand, and how low you go. There’s a book.”

“There is always a book,” said Caleb.

There was also, it emerged, a dwarf to keep it. He came across the court towards them, upright and ancient, with a white beard trimmed as level as a ruled line, and he carried in one hand a staff of black iron notched all down its length in inches, and in the other a book bound in iron boards that hung from his belt on a chain. He stopped before the Sovereign, set the foot of his staff to the stone, sighted along it, took one pace back, and bowed.

“Sovereign. Usher of Distances to the Shapers’ Hall. The Master Shaper comes. The Sovereign will stand here.”

He touched the stone with the staff. There was a mark there, a line cut fine into the flags, and Caleb saw now that the whole court was scored with them, hundreds of fine lines, some worn almost to nothing by centuries of boots and some sharp and new, with the gold of the runes lying in them like light in the grooves of a coin. Here and there a line had been cut through with one hard stroke of the chisel, where somebody had once been given a place in Durnhollow and then, for reasons the book would know, no longer needed one.

Arian stood on his line.

The Usher turned to Caleb, and opened his book, and paused.

The book put a prince of the Solar House at five yards from the Master Shaper’s step. A prince of the Solar House betrothed to the Sovereign of the Coast was another matter, on which the book had plainly never been asked for its opinion, and the Usher turned pages for some while with a crease between his white brows. Then he measured Caleb with his eye from boots to hair, as a tailor does, and set his staff to the stone a pace behind Arian’s left shoulder, at four yards and a half, and stood back to consider it, and at last stepped in and moved Caleb forward by one inch.

“I have lived at court for twenty-eight years,” said Caleb, standing on his inch, “and in all that time nobody ever measured how near I stood to anyone. They only looked pained when I stood too near.”

“The Holds do not look pained, Highness. The Holds measure.” And he wrote it in his book.

Then the Master Shaper came.

She came out of the Shapers’ Hall at the top of a short flight of iron steps, alone and without a herald, and the court went quiet round her the way a forge goes quiet when the bellows stop. She was not tall, for no dwarf was tall, but she was as broad as a door, and she wore a robe of black wool with a smith’s leather apron over it, scorched dark and supple with age, as if she had come straight from her work and saw no reason why the Sovereign of the Coast should be met in anything else. Her beard was the grey of iron cooling on the anvil, and she wore it plaited in a single thick rope and tucked into her belt, as a smith tucks away anything the fire might catch. Her forearms were bare, and the rune light lay along them. Old burns ran over them to the elbow, white and glossy and puckered, a whole life’s work written on her in scars.

Caleb had looked at a great many faces across a great many rooms and taken his time deciding how far to trust them. He looked at hers and decided at once.

She came down the steps and stopped on her own line, and she bowed, and the bow went to the Sovereign’s right hand, where the heavy gold ring with the black stone sat on his finger. She bowed to the ring first, a full hand’s breadth lower than she then bowed to the man. The runes on the iron steps behind her threw their gold across it, and the black stone took the light in and gave none of it back, as still water takes a dropped coin.

“Hild,” the Usher announced to the court at large, “Master Shaper of the Holds.”

“Sovereign of the Coast,” said Hild. Her Alorian was old and plain, the Alorian of the treatises, every word laid square on the one before it like a course of stone. “Dornhöhle receives you.”

“Master Shaper.”

“You have grown.” She looked him up and down without hurry, as she might have looked over a blade brought back to her for grinding. “The last time you stood on that line you were nine years old, and you asked me whether the chain went all the way down. I told you to come back when you had something to hang on it.”

Arian did not answer, and the court was quiet with him. Far below, some forge flared, and its glow came up out of the gulf and lay red across the flagstones and went again. Caleb, on his inch, forgot to breathe.

“So you have.” Hild turned her head. “Usher.”

The Usher coughed. Caleb, who had been made ready for this moment at the age of seven by a dancing master with a cane, stepped forward the quarter pace required and bowed by the degree he judged correct.

“Mistress Hild.”

“Master,” murmured the Usher behind him.

“Master Hild,” said Caleb, without a pause. “Forgive me.”

“The Holds forgive anything once. Give me your hand, Highness.”

He gave her his left. Her hand was hard as horn and warm all through, with the forge warmth that goes into a smith’s skin and never comes out again, and she turned his hand over in hers and back, and tilted it to the rune light, and studied the ring for a long while, the line of runes and the closed crescent and the empty setting.

“My work. It sits well.”

“You made it?”

“I make every ring the Holds are asked to make in the old style.” She let his hand go. “There are not many. There have not been many for some while.”

She stepped back onto her line.

“Tomorrow, at the third hammer, in the Hall of the Chain. Both parties present.” She looked at Caleb, and the look went in and stayed a while. “Each consenting aloud, Highness. The Holds do not accept a nod.”

“I have never in my life,” protested Caleb, “been accused of nodding when I might have spoken.”

“Good.” Hild went back up her steps, and the hush of the court let go behind her, and the dwarves began to move again, going round the Sovereign’s party at their measured distances like water round a stone.

At the back of the court, beside a door with seven iron bars across it, a young dwarf in grey with a ledger chained at his belt had been waiting all this while in the shadow of the arch with the patience of a milestone. Now he came forward, judged his own distance with his eye, stopped on it, and bowed to the Sovereign’s ring.

“Master Brandt’s compliments, Sovereign. The Chamber is ready when the Sovereign is.”

Nothing moved in Arian’s face. Under the arch the shadows drew in off the walls and gathered round his boots, close and black, as hounds come in to heel when the horn goes.

“Go up and rest. Mirek will take you.”

“And you?”

“The Chamber.”

“The Durnhollow debt.”

“The Coast’s business,” said Arian, and went.

Caleb watched him cross the nearest bridge with the grey clerk three measured paces ahead, a tall black figure and a short grey one, with the gulf of the mountain falling away beneath them and the forge fire coming up red and gold under their feet. Halfway over, Arian rested his right hand on the parapet. The rune light caught the gold of his rings and slid along them, and lost itself in the black stone. On the galleries above, dwarves had stopped at their work to watch him go over, and Caleb saw one of them take a pencil from behind her ear and write something down.

\* \* \*

The Holds had given the Sovereign’s party a house of its own, high on the eastern tiers, in a street where every door was iron and every doorstep had been scrubbed until the runes on it shone. It had been built for the Coast, by the look of it. There was not a window in it from cellar to roof. Its rooms were low and warm and panelled in dark wood carved all over with oak leaves that the lamps turned to gold at the edges, so that walking through them was like walking through a wood at dusk in a dream, and the beds were built into the walls like cupboards, with doors that shut. Every lamp was a rune cut in a plate of iron. It lit when one knocked on it twice and went out when one knocked once more, and Mirek knocked on every lamp in the house in the first quarter of an hour, twice and once and twice again, until a dwarven housekeeper came up the stairs with a duster and looked at him, and he stopped.

Caleb’s room was at the top of the house, and it had a column of sunlight standing in the middle of the floor.

He stopped in the doorway.

It came down a shaft in the ceiling, a round well lined with polished bronze that ran up through the rock to the mountainside a long way above, and it carried the sun down with it, handing it from bronze to bronze all the way, and set it on the boards in a gold pool the width of a carriage wheel. Dust turned in it, slow and bright, gold on gold. It was the only sun in the house, and it stood in the middle of the room like a guest who had arrived early and was too well mannered to sit down before it was asked.

“The Sovereign wrote ahead for a room with a well,” said the steward who had shown him up, a dwarf with a voice like a cart going over cobbles. “The Holds had one. They are for elves, mostly. The Holds have a great many Alorian clients.” He looked at Caleb’s face, and seemed to find something in it that the Holds had not measured. “There is a shutter, Highness. For the night.”

When he had gone, Caleb went and stood in the light, and lifted his face into it, and shut his eyes. It lay warm on his eyelids. It smelled faintly of hot bronze, and of the cold clean mountain air it had come down through, and of snow.

Mirek came to the door with the baggage, found him there, and went away again without a word, which for Mirek was a feat of tact, and Caleb heard him tell someone on the stair in an anxious whisper that His Highness was having a moment.

Paul brought up the last of the bags himself. He set them inside the door, and when Caleb turned in the light and said his name, he said “Highness” to the boards between his boots, and stepped back over the threshold, and drew the door to between them with great gentleness, as one closes a door on a sickroom.

Caleb looked at the closed door for some time.

Tomorrow, he thought, or the day after. Before the road home.

He slept through the afternoon in the cupboard bed with its doors open, and woke in what the Holds called night, when the city struck its last hammer of the day and the hum went down by half a note and stayed there. The gold pool on the floor had gone, and the room was dark and smelled of cold bronze. At the top of the well there was a single star, no bigger than a grain of salt, looking down the long throat of the shaft at him as if he were at the bottom of a well, which he supposed he was.

He sat up and drew his coat onto his lap and took the paper out of the lining.

Fourteen, fifteen, twenty. Twenty-five, five, twenty.

He held it in the dark and waited to know, as one waits at a window for a carriage that is late, and the star went slowly across the mouth of the well, cold and white, and out of sight. Below him in the house a door opened and closed, softly, and footsteps came along the passage under his room, and stopped, and went on.

He folded the paper and put it back, and lay down with his hand over it.

\* \* \*

The third hammer of the morning came up through the floor of the Hall of the Chain, and Caleb felt it in his teeth.

It was a round hall, deep in the mountain and older than anything he had yet seen in Durnhollow, with walls of rough black rock that nobody had ever troubled to dress. Its dome was cut with runes that burned a dull, deep red, like the eye of a forge seen through a keyhole, and in the middle of its floor was a hole. It was round and some four paces across, rimmed with iron worn smooth as glass by the hands of everyone who had ever leaned on it to look down, and the breath of the forges came up out of it, slow and warm, and the hum came with it.

Out of the dark at the top of the dome hung a chain.

It came down through the middle of the hall and into the hole, and went on down. Each link was as long as a hand. Those at the top were bright and new, with the hammer marks still crisp on them, and the further down Caleb looked the older they grew, black, and then pitted, and then rusted to the soft brown of rose petals pressed in a book, until the chain went down out of the rune light and he could not see it any more. Now and again among the old links one hung open, sawn through, with its two cut ends a finger’s breadth apart, and the Holds had left it hanging so among the whole ones. The chain turned a little in the warm air coming up, and the links touched and chimed against one another, a faint, continuous sound, like a great many people a long way off, all remembering something at once.

Every promise ever sealed under the bond-law of the Holds was a link in that chain. Caleb had read so in the east wing and thought it a pretty piece of nonsense.

“How far down does it go?” he asked Mirek, under his breath.

“All the way, Highness,” Mirek whispered. “That’s what they say.”

Beside the hole stood an anvil, black and squat and scarred, and beside the anvil a forge, a deep stone hearth with an apprentice at the bellows, and in the heart of the coals lay a single open link of iron, white hot, throwing its light up white across the black face of the anvil. Hild stood at the anvil in her black robe and her leather apron. She held a hammer that was a good deal lighter than Caleb had expected, and three masters of the Holds stood behind her in a row as witnesses, and at a lectern by the wall a clerk sat over a great book open at a clean page, with his pen already wet.

The hall was full. The Usher of Distances had been at work since the first hammer, and every soul in the place stood on a line. There were dwarves along the walls in their good black, with the red of the dome on their beards, and gnome factors in their bright wool, and the lycans of the escort in Conti black with their hands clasped before them like guards at a shrine. Mirek stood on his line in a borrowed coat, trying not to bounce. Paul stood by the door where the Usher had put him, as far from the anvil as the hall allowed.

And on the far side of the anvil, on the line the Usher had cut for him, stood Arian, in black from his throat to his boots, with his hands clasped behind his back.

In the heat of the forge the air round everyone else had begun to shimmer. Round Arian it did not. A fine frost had come up on the black silk of his shoulders and lay there, three paces from the coals, and did not melt.

Caleb wore the ivory silk with the silver frogging. He had dressed with great care in the light of the bronze well, and at the last, without letting himself think about it, he had put on the coat with the paper in its lining. He could feel the folded edge of it now against his ribs.

“The Holds do not ask why,” said Hild.

She said it to the hall, in her plain old Alorian, and the hall listened.

“Lutèce asks why. The Citadel asks why, I am told, and writes the answer on vellum, and hangs it in a frame. The Holds ask only whether.” She took the link from the coals with her tongs and laid it on the anvil, open and glowing, and its light came up white under her chin and turned the scars along her forearms to silver. “I will ask each of you once. Each of you will answer, aloud, so that this hall hears it and the Book has it. If both answer, I close the iron, and it goes on the chain, and there it stays until the two of you come back together and ask me to open it. If either does not answer, the iron goes back in the fire, and no one in this hall will think the less of anyone.”

She did not look at Caleb while she said it. He knew it for a kindness, and could not have said how.

“Arian of House Conti, Sovereign of the Obsidian Coast. Is it your will that this promise be sealed?”

“It is,” said Arian.

He said it at once, in the voice he had used to say *the prince is right*, without lifting it, and the whole hall heard it to the back wall.

Hild struck.

The hammer came down once on the glowing iron, and the sound was not loud. It was clean and deep, a sound with no edges to it, and it went down through the floor and the soles of Caleb’s boots and down the hole after the chain, and he heard the chain take it, link after link, carrying it down into the mountain until it was too far away to hear. A spray of gold sparks leapt from the iron and died in the dark. On the anvil the link had bent half shut. Along the walls every shadow in the hall had leaned towards the anvil and the man beyond it, and stayed so.

Hild turned to Caleb.

“Caleb of the Solar House, Prince of Aloria. Is it your will that this promise be sealed?”

Caleb’s mouth was dry, and no word stood ready in it.

His hand had gone to his coat. He felt the paper through the silk and held it there. The hall waited. Somewhere along the wall a dwarf shifted her weight, and the iron of her boot heel scraped on the stone, and the scrape went round the walls and down the hole and was a long time going. The link on the anvil had begun to cool. He watched it go from white to gold, and from gold towards red, the way a face changes when the lamp is carried out of the room.

“Take what time you need, Highness. Iron can be heated twice. It has been before.”

He had written NOT YET in the east wing to keep until he knew whether it was true, and he had carried it against his ribs ever since, across the strait and under his first stars and into his first dawn and through the white mist of Old Meridian, waiting the whole way to know. And now, now with the link going red on the anvil and the chain chiming in the dark beneath his feet, now with the whole of the Holds along the walls and Mirek forgetting not to bounce, now with the Sovereign of Vesperia standing three paces off with frost on his shoulders and his hands behind his back, not looking at him, taking such care not to look at him that Caleb could feel the care across the anvil like the heat off the coals, he found that he did not have to wait any longer.

He knew.

“Yes,” said Caleb.

It came out a great deal louder than he had meant.

It rang off the rough black walls and went up into the dome and came down again, and then it went down the hole after the chain, *yes*, *yes*, *yes*, fainter and fainter along the iron into the dark of the mountain, and a dwarf on the back wall gave a bark of laughter and was elbowed by her neighbour. Heat went up Caleb’s neck and into the tips of his ears. The last time he had answered a question of this kind it had been across a candlelit study, and he had said *No* at much the same volume, and he heard that too, and the heat took his whole face.

Then he waited, as one waits on new ice after the first step, for the crack.

No crack came. The floor lay solid and warm under his boots with the warmth of the forges in it, and nothing in him was falling. He stood in the Hall of the Chain with his hand flat against his coat, over a fold of paper that one word had made the only untrue thing about him. The gold came up under his skin from his wrists to his throat, slow and sure, as it came up in the garden. On his left hand the runes of the black ring woke one after another round the band, and the iron grew warm, and then warmer, until it was warmer than the finger it was on.

Hild struck again.

The second blow closed the link in a burst of gold sparks. Its sound went down after the first, and something went through Caleb with it, a drawing tight like the moment in a healing when the light finds the wound and takes hold, as if a thread had been run from the ring on his finger through the middle of his chest and out across the anvil, and someone at the far end had taken it up.

He looked across.

Arian had not moved from his line. But his left hand had come from behind his back, and it was pressed flat to his chest, over the place where a man keeps his heart, as if something there had been struck, and he was looking at Caleb now, and did not seem to know what his hand was doing. The frost on his shoulders had thickened to white. Behind the anvil Hild stood with her hammer still lifted and looked from one of them to the other, as a smith looks at a weld that has taken where there was no heat to take it, and said nothing.

At the lectern the clerk wrote, and stopped, and looked up at Caleb over his spectacles, and bent and wrote one word more.

Hild took the link in her tongs and plunged it into a trough of water by the anvil, and the water flung up a cloud of steam that smelled of rain on hot slate, and when she lifted it out it was black and whole. She carried it to the chain herself. An apprentice at a windlass in the wall brought the chain up out of the dark by one link’s length, chiming, and Hild opened the topmost hook and set the new link on it, and closed the hook, and nodded, and the apprentice let the windlass go. The whole chain went down into the mountain by the length of one link, with a long iron sigh that ran away beneath them out of hearing. At the top of it, in the red light of the dome, the newest link hung black and plain, with the hammer marks still on it.

“It is sealed. No crown, council or court of any realm can loose it. Only the two of you, and only together.” She laid her hammer down on the anvil. “The Holds require nothing further.” She considered Caleb’s face. “Whatever they may do in Lutèce.”

The lycans along the wall laughed outright, Mirek loudest, and then the dwarves were laughing too, and the hall broke up into talk and the scrape of boots leaving their lines, and the Usher of Distances could be heard over all of it asking people to mind where they stood.

Arian came round the anvil.

He stopped an arm’s length away, where Caleb could feel the cold of him come through the forge heat like a draught under a door. Both his hands were behind his back again. The frost was still on his shoulders. He asked for nothing, and he did not bend his head, but he said, so low that the chain nearly covered it, “Thank you.”

“That is three,” said Caleb. His voice was not altogether his own. “I am keeping count, you know.”

Arian said nothing. But the frost that the forge had not touched all morning had begun to melt on his shoulders, and ran down the black silk in dark streaks.

Over his shoulder, by the door, Paul was not looking at the floor. He was looking at Caleb, and his face was wet. When Caleb’s eyes found his, he turned his head to the wall, as a man turns from a fire that has grown too hot to stand near.

The Holds fed their guests in the Long Hall on the fourth tier, at a single table of black oak so long that the far end of it was hazy with lamp smoke. A hearth stood at either end of the hall, big enough to stable a horse in, with a fire roaring gold in each, and the rune lamps hung low over the table on iron chains, and the Usher of Distances had seated every one of them.

There was mutton from the high pastures, roasted until it fell from the bone at a look, and a soup of black mushrooms from the under-farms that tasted of rain and woodland and smelled of the inside of a cellar in the best possible way. The bread was so dark it was nearly purple, and the cheeses came wrapped in nettle leaves, and the dwarven ale was the colour of peat water in cups of beaten iron. The gnomes had brought puddings, white and trembling and stuck all over with candied violet petals, and a sugar model of the Hall of the Chain with a chain of spun sugar hanging down through the middle of it, at which Mirek gazed for some time in silent reverence before he ate the dome. For the Sovereign there was a cask of the Coast’s own blood-wine, kept in the cellars of the Holds for clients who could drink nothing else, and the steward brought it up the hall in a cup of black iron held out before him in both hands, like a relic in a procession.

The masters of the Holds rose to speak in order of rank. Each spoke for as long as his rank allowed and not a breath longer, and the Usher sat at the foot of the table with a sand glass and turned it for each of them, and when the sand ran out the speaker sat down, sometimes in the middle of a word. A master of the smiths’ guild spoke for a whole turn of the glass on the tensile strength of bridge iron and its bearing on the married state. A master of the vault-masons spoke on the subject of glass, and looked down the table at Caleb as he did it, and Caleb raised his cup to him and saw him go red to the beard.

Then Arian rose to answer them.

The hall went quiet. He stood at the head of the table with the iron cup in his hand and the light of the lamps lying along his black sleeve, and he did not look at the Usher’s glass.

“The Coast thanks the Holds, who keep what is given into their keeping.”

He lifted the cup. That was all.

It was the shortest speech of the evening by half a glass, and it undid the hall.

Caleb had felt the pull of the old Umbral bloodlines on his first night on the Coast and set his heels against it, but he had never watched it go through a room. It went through this one the way warmth goes through a cold bath when the hot is poured in at one end. In the two great hearths the fires bowed low, and the gold went out of them to a dark warm red. A smith of some eminence set his cup down in the silver salt dish and did not notice. Two gnome factors halfway down the table leaned together and sighed, and went on leaning. A young dwarven woman with a hammer embroidered on her sleeve put her chin in her hand and let out a breath so long that her neighbour had to steady the lamp. Down at the foot of the table the Usher of Distances let his glass run out and did not turn it, and sat looking up the hall with his mouth a little open and his iron staff forgotten across his knees, and for the space of several breaths nobody in the Long Hall measured anything at all.

The rune lamps nearest the Sovereign had dimmed to a deep ember red, and leaned, as much as an iron lamp can lean, towards his chair.

“They’ve all gone soft,” whispered Mirek, enchanted. “Look at them, Highness. Look at the old one with the stick.”

“I had not noticed.”

“You’re staring, Highness.”

“I am looking at the mutton,” said Caleb. “It is exceptionally fine mutton, and I am considering whether to have more of it.”

The gold had come up at his wrists. He put his hands under the table.

Arian sat down. A master of the Holds came up the table to be presented, with the Usher at his elbow, and the Usher set his staff to the floor and looked, as the Holds had taught him, at the Sovereign’s right hand, to measure the hand’s breadth owed to the ring. He opened his book. He stopped. Then the master bowed to the man, and to the man only, and the Usher made a note in his book with a pen that seemed not to want to make it.

Caleb looked at the hand.

It lay on the black oak beside the iron cup, long and white and still, and there were two heavy gold rings on it. The third finger was bare. There was no mark where the ring had been, no band of skin paler than the rest, because no part of Arian was paler than any other part. On Caleb’s first night in the great hall at Castel Nerezza the black stone had looked like a place where the world ran out. He had seen it on the bridge only yesterday. Now it was gone. He thought of the anvil that morning, and of a right hand kept behind a back for the whole length of the rite.

He was still looking at the bare finger when somebody climbed onto the bench beside him.

She was a gnome, and she had to stand on the bench to look him in the face, which she did without the least embarrassment, as if benches had been invented for the purpose. She wore a lace cap starched into two stiff wings, like the sails of a Lowmere barge, and stockings striped in orange and white, and at her waist hung a ledger on a chain that was nearly as large as her skirt. Her face was round and brown and creased as a winter apple, with two black eyes in it as bright as a wren’s, and she was holding out her hand.

“Maartje Bosboom, Your Royal Highness. Of Van Houten and Daughters, of Lowmere, second factor to the third daughter. May I call you Highness? The whole of it takes me a breath and a half, and I’ve a great deal to say.”

“You may call me anything you like,” said Caleb, and shook the hand, which was dry and quick and warm as a sparrow, “so long as you go on talking. I have been sealed under the bond-law of the Holds this morning, and I find I am in no condition to carry my own end of a conversation.”

“Oh, I know. I heard you.” She sat down on the bench, crossed her striped ankles, and beamed at him. “Half the city heard you. You’re on the board.”

“The board?”

“At the Undergate. Every sealing goes up on the board by the gate before supper, for anyone to read who likes, and everyone likes. The Sovereign’s line says *Consented*. Yours says *Consented*, and then a word after it in Holdish that the clerk put in afterwards, which isn’t usual, Highness. The clerks of the Chain don’t add words. They aren’t paid by the word. I asked.” She leaned in, glowing. “*Nachdrücklich.*”

“Which means?”

“Emphatically,” said Maartje, with relish.

Caleb put his face in his hands. When he took it out again she was laughing, a quick high gnomish laugh like a handful of beads let fall on a table, and he found that he was laughing too, helplessly, with all the breath the anvil had left him.

“Van Houten carried your glass, you know,” she said, when they had both recovered. “For the dome. Forty crates from the vault-masons’ yard to the Coast in the dead of winter, every pane in wool and straw, and not one cracked. I packed the top tier myself.”

He looked at her, the bright black eyes and the absurd starched cap, and thought of a pale disc of twilight through iron ribs, seen from among the roses far below, and of the chalk marks on the glass that nobody had wiped away.

“Then I owe you my life, Madame Bosboom, and I am very glad to be able to say so to your face.”

Maartje went pink to the edge of her cap and was, for a moment, quite silent.

“Highness,” she said then, and lowered her voice, and the brightness went down in her face to something more careful. “May I ask you a thing? An odd thing. You’ll think me cracked.”

“I have been a guest of the Coast for eight weeks. My notion of cracked has grown very generous.”

“We carried twelve crates for the Citadel. Ten days since, from your own wharves, by air, to the Marches. Refinery machinery, the bill said.” Her quick brown fingers went to the ledger at her waist and rested on it, as if to make sure of it. “Weight found somewhat greater than declared. No remark entered. Van Houten never enters a remark, Highness, it’s bad for custom. But I was in the hold with them over the high Marches, and it was so cold the frost came up on the inside of the hull like fur on a cat, and the crates were warm. You could put your hand on a lid and warm it. And at night, in the dark of the hold, there was a light along the seams of one lid, no brighter than a glow-worm in a hedge, and —” She stopped. “And one of them sang.”

Caleb set down his cup.

“Sang.”

“Not hummed. Hum I’d understand. A note. High and thin, on and off, all night long, so that our lycan deckhand wouldn’t sleep down there, and I didn’t blame him.” She looked up at him. “So I wondered, Highness, you being from the Citadel. Does Citadel machinery sing?”

He thought of the refineries of the lower city, the great engines that ground and thudded behind their brick walls and filled the Underglow with dust and second-yield glow, and none of them had ever sung. He thought, without knowing why, of Cecily, and put the thought down at once, as one puts down a cup that is too hot to hold.

“Not that I ever heard,” he answered slowly. “Where did they go?”

“To the Kessel Pass. The Chamber’s couriers took them on from there, and where they went after that I couldn’t tell you, and the Chamber wouldn’t. They were paid for out of an account at the Seventh Chamber, so I’m not to ask, and I haven’t.” She smiled, a little crookedly. “Except you. And you don’t count, Highness. You’re family now. Of the Holds, I mean. Once you’re on the chain.”

*The Kessel Pass.* At that name, before the Council, Don Salvatore had gone still in his chair. Caleb put the crates away with the Kessel Pass, where he kept the things he could not yet afford to look at, and found that it was getting crowded in there.

Maartje’s eyes had gone up the table. His went with them, to the Sovereign’s bare hand on the black oak.

“Brave,” she murmured. “That’s what they’re saying in our counting house, with the door shut. Brave, or mad, and the dwarves won’t say which, being dwarves.”

“What is brave?”

“The ring, Highness.” She said it the way one mentions the weather to someone who has just come in out of it. “Pledged to the Seventh Chamber yesterday afternoon against the whole of the Coast’s paper. Every note the Ashward held, bought back this morning while you were at the anvil. The Don’s grandson stood for the Ashward, young Ruggero Caruso, and he asked for a day to send to his grandfather, and the grey clerk gave him a note from the Sovereign instead. One line, it was. Nobody saw what it said. He read it, and put it in the bowl of his pipe and lit it, and swallowed the smoke the wrong way and had to be patted on the back, and then he signed before the clerk had blotted the page. I saw it. Van Houten’s carrying the notes home to Castel Nerezza on the Sovereign’s own word, so we had to know what stood behind them, you see, for the insurance.” She turned the iron cup in her hands. “Redeemed by the Long Eclipse, or forfeit, and up for auction under Holds law, and my mistress would sell her canal house to bid on it, and so would half Lowmere. And meanwhile —” she dropped her voice still lower “— meanwhile they say on the Coast that a Sovereign without his ring can’t seal a thing that sticks. That any of his Nine may stand up and contest whatever he seals, and call for a Leaning on it, and let the shadows decide. Is that so? I’ve always wondered. You’d know, Highness, you’d —”

She stopped. She had looked at his face.

Caleb did not know what was in it. He could not feel it. He could feel the cold iron of the bench under him and the hum coming up through it, and the warm light of the lamps on his face, and the noise of the hall going on all round him, mutton and laughter and the scrape of knives, as if from the far side of a wall.

“Oh,” breathed Maartje. “Oh, Highness. You didn’t —”

Her round face went through dismay and calculation and a deep professional horror, all in a moment, like a ledger riffled under a thumb.

“I’ve said a thing,” she whispered. “I never say a thing. Van Houten never enters a remark.” She slid off the bench and stood on the floor in her striped stockings, twisting the chain of her ledger round her fingers. “I didn’t say it. You didn’t hear it. I’ll go.”

“Madame Bosboom,” said Caleb. His voice came out quite ordinary, which surprised him. “Thank you for my glass.”

She looked at him for a breath, and something in her face went soft. She bobbed him a curtsey. Then she was gone among the benches like a mouse into a wainscot.

Caleb sat with his cold hands under the table.

The hall went on as it had been. Up the table Arian sat listening to a master of the smiths with his face turned a little to one side and nothing in it to read, and the lamps near him still leaned and glowed like embers, and every dwarf within ten yards was still leaning with them. His right hand lay by the iron cup it had lifted to the Holds, who keep what is given into their keeping.

Caleb thought of the Nine in their high-backed chairs under the violet lamps of Castel Nerezza, and the lord in the mulberry coat, and Don Salvatore turning his garnet ring, all of them waiting for a Sovereign with a bare hand to seal something they did not like, so that they might stand up one by one and ask the shadows of the Coast whether he was Sovereign still.

He ate the rest of his mutton. He did not taste one mouthful of it.

\* \* \*

He found the balcony by following the heat.

It was at the end of a passage off the Long Hall, where an arch opened onto the dark, and beyond the arch a balcony of black iron hung out over the gulf of the forges. The warm air came up past it in a long steady breath that lifted the hair from his forehead and smelled of hot metal and charcoal and, faintly, of the sea, though the sea was a hundred leagues away, and he never found out why. Far below him the forge fires burned. He could see them now, ring below ring of open hearths going down into the mountain, each a mouth of red and gold, with figures moving in front of them, black against the glow, and the sound of hammers coming up on the hot air, many hammers, out of time with each other and yet somehow, all together, keeping time with the hum. Sparks rose past the balcony. They rose slowly from far down and drifted past him and on up into the dark of the gulf, red and gold and dying to grey, as unhurried as thistledown over a meadow at the end of summer.

He put his hands on the rail. The iron was warm, and the hum came up through it into his palms.

He knew Arian had come when the sparks changed their minds. They had been going straight up past the rail. Now they went sideways, all of them together, drifting towards the arch at his back, and the warm air on the nape of his neck turned cold, and the shadow of the balcony rail on the iron floor lengthened towards the arch as if someone had carried a lamp out of the passage behind him, though no one had.

Arian came and stood at the rail beside him, an arm’s length off, and put his right hand on the iron.

Neither of them spoke for a while. The hammers went on below, and the sparks went up past them, red and gold, into the dark.

*Why did you not tell me?* Caleb thought. He could see the hand on the rail without turning his head, the two gold rings and the bare finger beside them, and the forge light lying red along the white bones. *Were you going to let me read it in the* Lantern *over breakfast, like everything else?*

He thought of Enzo on the great stair. *Ask him. Do not tell him.*

“May I ask you something?”

Arian turned his head.

Caleb lifted his left hand into the forge light, which came up through the empty setting of the black ring and lay in it like a coal.

“This. It has been empty for eight weeks. Master Hild says it sits well. The *Lantern* informs me that by the old custom it waits for the wedding day, and for a stone of the bridegroom’s choosing. I have tried every stone I know against it, in my head, every jewel I ever wore to a ball and a good many I only coveted, and none of them will do.” He lowered his hand. “What is it to be?”

Arian looked at the ring for a long moment, and the sparks between them went slowly up.

“It is not finished.”

“That is not an answer, Sovereign.”

“No.”

“Then it has that in common with my reputation, which the Holds are still writing.” Caleb turned his back to the gulf and leaned on the rail on his elbows. “Did you know they have put us on a board at the Undergate, for the whole city to read? Your entry says *Consented*. So does mine. And then there is a word after mine that the clerk added, when he had had a moment to think it over.”

Arian waited.

“Emphatically,” said Caleb, when it was clear that he would not be asked.

And Arian laughed.

It began somewhere low, and it took him by surprise, Caleb could see that, for he put his hand to the rail as if the balcony had moved. It was a rough sound, and it came out of him unevenly, in pieces, the way water comes out of a pump that has stood all winter in a frozen yard, coughing first and then, all at once, running clear. Then it ran clear. He bowed his head over the rail and laughed, and went on laughing, with his shoulders shaking and his black hair falling forward over his brow, and below them the forge light flared up red and gold all down the gulf as if every bellows in the mountain had been worked at once, and a great bright swarm of sparks came up past the balcony and whirled round the two of them, and the shadows on the iron floor scattered like starlings off a field and came wheeling back.

Caleb held the rail behind him.

He had heard Arian say a great many things in eight weeks, *no* and *yes* and *stay in the light* and *the prince is right*, and *thank you* three times, and he had never once heard him laugh.

Arian straightened at last, and his face was already its own again. His eyes were not. For eight weeks they had been as black and as closed as the stone of his ring. Now the forge fire was in them, red and gold, and it stayed there. The sparks settled, and the fires below sank back to their ordinary glow.

“You laughed,” breathed Caleb, when he could.

“Yes.”

“I had begun to think you did not know how.”

Arian looked out over the gulf, at the bridges going over in the dark with their runes lit gold, like stitches.

“So had I.”

His right hand lay on the rail between them. Caleb looked at it, at the bare finger, and felt the knowledge of it come back into him from wherever it had gone while Arian laughed, cold and whole, the way the ache comes back into a mended bone when the weather turns.

He could have said it then. It was there on his tongue, *I know about the ring*, and *how could you*, and under both of those, plainer, *why*. He had a fair idea of what Arian would answer, if he answered at all. *The Coast’s business.* And then whatever had opened in his eyes would close again, and it would be Caleb who had closed it.

“Good night, Sovereign,” he said instead.

Arian was quiet a little longer.

“Good night.” He went in under the arch, and the sparks straightened behind him and went on up into the dark the way they had been going before, as if nothing had happened to them at all.

Caleb stayed at the rail.

The hammers rang below, and the rail hummed under his hands, and he thought of the money.

Every child of the Solar House had a portion, settled at birth, so that a prince or a princess of Aloria should never have to go cap in hand to the Treasury for the price of a coat. His had been settled on the twelfth day of his life by his mother’s own clerk, and laid away in a vault of the Holds where nobody could spend it on his behalf. He had never touched it. He had planned at fifteen to spend it on a ship to Nauplion, and at twenty on a house in Lutèce with a balcony over the river, and at twenty-five on nothing whatever, which was the most expensive plan of all. In the east wing, in the weeks of the Blight, he had lain awake in the carved bed and reckoned it over. It was the one thing in the world that was his, that did not come to him from his father’s hand, and it was lying, every crown and every penny of it, in the cold vaults of the Seventh Chamber of Dornhöhle.

Somewhere beneath his feet.

Paul was sitting on the top stair outside Caleb’s door when he came out in the morning with two cups of Vesna’s Wildmarch.

The morning had come down the bronze well before him. It stood in his open doorway and spilled out across the landing and down the first stairs, a long warm tongue of gold on the dark oak, and Paul sat at the edge of it with his back to the wall and his boots in the shadow, as if he had taken care to sit where it would not reach him.

He had made the second cup for Paul out of sheer obstinacy, and now he held it out.

Paul got up. He looked at the cup, and at Caleb.

“Sit. Please. It is very bad tea, and it is getting cold, and Vesna will know if either of us wastes a drop. She knows everything. I suspect her of a crystal ball.”

Paul took the cup. After a moment he sat down again on the top stair, and Caleb sat down beside him in the warm light, which a prince of the Solar House did not do on stairs, in company, before breakfast, and they drank Vesna’s tea side by side, and both of them made the face.

It was quiet. The steam went up white from both cups into the gold of the doorway. The house was carved round them in its oak leaves, and the hum came up through the stair, and from the street below came the sound of a dwarf sweeping a doorstep, slow and thorough, as if the doorstep had done something to deserve it.

“I used to tell you stories,” Caleb murmured, “when you first came. You never said anything, and I thought that if I talked long enough you would have to answer out of politeness. You had the whole history of the Solar kings in one week, with the dull ones left out and several invented.”

“I remember, Highness.” Something moved in Paul’s face, and was put away. “You gave King Aurel a dragon.”

“He deserved one. He had a very dull reign.” Caleb turned the cup in his hands. “Paul. What have I done?”

Paul was silent.

“Whatever it is, I would rather know it. If I have hurt you, or shamed you, or if there is something I ought to have seen and did not, I would rather you told me to my face than sat outside every door I sleep behind and looked at the floor. You used to look at me. You looked at me yesterday.” He heard his voice beginning to go, and steadied it. “In the Hall of the Chain. You were weeping.”

“You looked happy, Highness,” said Paul.

He said it to the cup, so low that the hum nearly had it.

“Yesterday. At the anvil. When you said it.” He was turning the cup round and round between his big hands. “I haven’t seen you look like that since you were a boy. Since before —” He stopped.

“Before what?”

“Before you were grown, Highness. That’s all.”

“Paul.”

“You’ve done nothing.” Paul looked up then, and met his eyes, and his own were tired and kind, and there was something behind the kindness that Caleb could not read, like a letter seen through an envelope held up to a lamp. “You’ve never done anything, Highness. Not to me. Not to anyone. That’s the truth, and I’ll swear to it on whatever you like.”

“Then talk to me.”

“Not here.” Paul set his cup down on the stair as gently as if it might break. “When we’re home. I’ll tell you everything when we’re home. I promise you that.”

“Home. Do you mean the Coast, or the Citadel?”

But Paul only got up, and took Caleb’s empty cup out of his hands with his own, as he had taken a hundred cups out of Caleb’s hands in fourteen years, and stood a moment on the stair above him with a cup in each fist.

“The Sovereign’s chosen the Fault road home. Mirek told me. Shorter than the way we came, and quieter. Away from the market towns.” He looked down the stair. “Go and rest, Highness. It’s a long road.”

He went down. Caleb heard him in the kitchen below, rinsing the cups, slowly, the way a man does who is in no hurry to go back to anywhere. Caleb sat on at the top of the stair, and the warm light crept down the steps behind him one stair at a time, to the place where Paul had been sitting, and lay there.

Arian was with the masters of the Holds all that day, and Caleb did not go looking for him. He sat in the bronze well with his notebook on his knee and wrote nothing in it, and in the afternoon, when the gold pool had crept off the boards and up the oak leaves of the wall and gone, he asked the steward, in the most idle voice he possessed, at what hour the Seventh Chamber closed its doors.

“The Seventh Chamber does not close, Highness. It keeps the Coast’s hours. Its clients come by night.”

\* \* \*

The door with the seven iron bars stood at the back of the court of the Undergate, where Caleb had first seen the grey clerk waiting. At the last hammer of the day the court was dark and empty but for a lamplighter knocking on the runes along the parapet, twice and twice and twice, and the Usher’s fine lines across the flagstones, lit gold now from the side and every one of them throwing its own fine shadow. Caleb crossed them without standing on any.

He had come alone. He had told Mirek that he meant to sleep early and had then sat in the dark in his cupboard bed, fully dressed, until he heard the boy’s breathing go slow and even through the wall. Paul was not at the door. He had gone down to the kitchens after supper, and had not come up again.

The night clerk behind the door was a different dwarf, older, with a lamp and a book of his own. He looked at the ring on Caleb’s left hand, and rose, and bowed to the fourth degree, and said nothing at all, and led him in.

The corridor beyond was cold-iron.

Caleb knew it within three steps. The walls were black iron from the floor to the vaulting, plate on riveted plate, cut with runes that lay dull under the lamps, and the moment his boot came down on the iron floor the light in him folded itself away, as a bird puts its head beneath its wing. The gold went out of his wrists. The faint warmth that had lived in his palms since the Harp Vales, waiting always for a wound, went quiet. It had been with him every waking hour since the nursery. He went down the corridor from lamp to lamp, each a long way from the last, with his hands closed at his sides, and felt a little less like a prince of the Solar House at every one.

Arian had come down a corridor like this one yesterday afternoon. Caleb thought of the dark that leaned after him through every hall of the Coast, and of how it must have stopped where the iron began, and stayed there on the threshold, and let him go on alone from lamp to lamp with nothing at his back, to take the ring off his hand.

The room at the end of the corridor was a counting room. The Seventh Chamber was said to build all its counting rooms to a single pattern, wherever in the world it kept them, so that no client should ever be distracted by the furniture, and this one had nothing in it to distract anybody. There was an oak table polished nearly black, a ledger, an inkwell, and pens in a row. One lamp hung over the table on a chain, and its light made an island on the oak and left the iron walls to the dark. The air was cold and smelled of oil and metal and old paper.

At the head of the table a dwarf rose from his chair into the light of the lamp. He wore his beard in three flat plaits laid on his chest, with his spectacles on a ribbon below them, and he bowed to Caleb to the fourth degree, which was the bow owed by the Seventh Chamber to a prince of the blood who was not the heir. It was the tariff’s bow to the inch, and wanted nothing, and Caleb found it restful.

“Your Highness is unexpected,” said Master Brandt. “The Chamber is grateful.”

“I am told the Chamber keeps the Coast’s hours.”

“The Chamber keeps every client’s hours, Highness. The Coast’s are only the longest.” Master Brandt drew out a chair at the side of the table, with his own hand. “Your Highness will sit.”

Caleb sat. The oak was cold under his wrists. He laid his hands on it, folded, and found that they wanted to shake, and let them want.

“I hold a portion with the Chamber. Settled on me at birth. I should like to know what stands in it.”

Master Brandt did not consult the ledger. He took his spectacles from their ribbon and looked at Caleb for a moment without them.

“The Chamber knows what stands in it, Highness. The Chamber has known on every day of the last twenty-eight years.” He put on the spectacles. “Your Highness did not come down an iron corridor at the last hammer to be told his balance.”

“No,” said Caleb. “I came to redeem a pledge.”

The flame stood straight up in its glass.

“It is the law of the Holds, I believe,” Caleb went on, “that a pledge held by a Chamber may be redeemed by any party who can pay its price. I read a treatise on it once. I was very bored.”

“Any party. Yes. The Chamber is not sentimental about whose money it is.” He laid his hands flat on the table, one either side of the ledger, and looked at Caleb for some time. “And which pledge does Your Highness wish to redeem?”

“The Sovereign’s ring.”

“The Chamber does not discuss the business of its other clients.”

“I am not asking you to discuss it,” insisted Caleb. “I know the terms, the Long Eclipse and the auction, and I know what it means on the Coast for a Sovereign to seal without it. I did not learn them from the Chamber. I ask the Chamber nothing but its price.”

Master Brandt was silent for a while.

“The Chamber has sold a crown under that law, Highness,” he said then. “In my time. The king came to the sale. He stood on the line the Usher gave him, and watched it go, and bowed to the buyer, and went home. The Chamber did not hear of him again.”

He took up a pen, dipped it, and wrote on a slip of grey paper torn from the back of the ledger. He wrote two figures, one beneath the other, in his square printed hand, and turned the slip round on the oak with one finger, and slid it across.

Caleb read it.

The first figure was the price of the ring. The second was his portion. The second was larger than the first, and not by much. He looked at the two of them for a long time under the lamp, the one sitting on top of the other, and thought that Eleanor would have admired the neatness of it.

“Your Highness will understand that if Your Highness pays the first figure out of the second, there will remain in the Chamber in Your Highness’s name the difference, which is not large. And if Your Highness then closes the account, as is usual when a portion is spent, there will remain nothing. Your Highness will hold nothing with the Chamber. Nor, so far as the Chamber knows, anywhere else.”

“I understand.”

“Not the price of a coat.”

“I have a great many coats.”

“The Chamber does not lend against coats,” said Master Brandt.

“No. I did not suppose it would.”

Master Brandt took off his spectacles and folded them and let them hang on their ribbon.

“Nor the price of a passage, Highness,” he said, in the same voice. “From here to the Citadel by air is not cheap. From the Coast it is less cheap again. Your Highness is a hostage under the Accord, and a hostage does not go home. But a man who holds a portion in the Holds has a door in his wall, whether he means to use it or not. Your Highness will have no door.”

It was the first thing he had said that was not about money, and Caleb answered it as it deserved.

“I know. I have counted the steps to it every night for eight weeks. I know where it is.” He put his hand flat on the slip. “Pay the first figure out of the second, Master Brandt, if you please. And put the difference in with it. I would rather close the account.”

“The difference will be entered as the Chamber’s fee.”

“Then the Chamber will be very handsomely paid for its silence.”

“The Chamber’s silence is not for sale, Highness. It is included.” He drew the ledger towards him and opened it at a clean page, and dipped his pen. “Your Highness’s portion is recorded in the books of the Solar Treasury. When it moves, the Treasury will be told that it has moved. That is the law, and the Chamber keeps it. The Treasury will not be told where it has gone, or why.”

“My sister keeps those books.”

“Then your sister will know that it has moved.”

Caleb looked at the clean page.

Eleanor would see it. Eleanor, who saw everything that moved in the Treasury’s books, as a heron sees every fish in a pool. She would sit at her desk in the Chancellery with the white noon in the windows and her sleeves pinned back and see his portion go out of the books in one night, all of it, into the silence of the Holds, and she would not know where, or why, and she would think —

He did not know what she would think. He knew what she had asked him.

“Master Brandt. Must the payment be entered as one sum?”

“It may be entered as Your Highness pleases. The total is the same, however Your Highness cuts it.”

“I know. I should like it cut into seven.”

Master Brandt looked at him over the top of the ledger.

“Seven drafts, each for a sum I shall give you, the last to take up whatever is left. I shall want the pence of each to be what I say. The crowns may be whatever they must.”

There was a pause, in which a bead of ink gathered on the nib of Master Brandt’s pen and hung there, black and shining under the lamp, and did not fall.

“The Chamber,” said Master Brandt, “has no objection to arithmetic.”

Caleb put his hand inside his coat.

The paper was where it had been for eleven days, in the lining against his ribs, folded tight. He drew it out and unfolded it on the oak, and the light of the lamp fell on his own column in his own hand, NOT YET in Eleanor’s pence, gone soft at the folds from being carried. He turned it over, to the clean side, and held out his hand for a pen.

Master Brandt gave him one.

The lamp burned on its chain above him, the one light in all that black iron, and he reckoned it as he had reckoned the price of lemons and harpsichord strings in the east wing, slowly, counting on his fingers under the table where no one could see. In the cold-iron there was no gold to come up in his skin and give him away. His hands were his own, and quite steady, and only cold.

Twenty-three. Nine. Twelve. Twelve again. Nine. Fourteen. Seven.

He looked at the column for a while. It said WILLING. He had known it would, and it still took him by the throat, so that he had to sit with the pen lifted until he could see the page clearly again.

He turned the paper round and slid it across the table, as Master Brandt had slid the slip.

Master Brandt read it. If he saw anything in it but seven numbers, he gave no sign. He copied them one by one into the ledger in his square hand, with a crown sum before each that he worked out in his head as fast as he wrote, and he did not ask a single question.

“There is one more thing,” said Caleb.

The pen waited.

“Twelve crates of refinery machinery went out of the Citadel not a fortnight ago by Van Houten and Daughters, to the Kessel Pass. I am told the money for them came out of this Chamber. Whose account?”

“The Chamber does not discuss the business of its other clients,” said Master Brandt.

He said it as he might have said that it was raining, and did not look up from the page, and the pen did not pause.

Caleb had expected nothing else. All the same he had gone cold all down his back, and the iron had nothing to do with it. Somewhere in this mountain, at an oak table built to this same pattern, a dwarf had bowed to whoever paid for those crates, to the depth the tariff owed and not one hair less, and would bow to him again tomorrow.

“And mine?” he asked. “Will the Chamber discuss mine?”

“It will not.”

“With anyone?”

“With anyone.”

“With the Sovereign of the Coast, if he should come here and ask after his ring?”

“Least of all.”

“Write that down.”

“It is the custom of the Chamber, Highness. It would be done whether it were written or not.” Master Brandt dipped his pen. “But the Chamber will write it, if Your Highness wishes. The Chamber makes no charge for writing down what it would do in any case.”

He wrote it. Then he blotted the page, and turned the ledger round so that it faced Caleb, and laid the pen beside it, and read the whole aloud, every clause, in his flat clear voice without any expression in it at all. The portion, and its sum. The seven drafts, with their crowns and their pence. The pledge, named in the ledger only as *one ring of gold, set with a black stone, received of a client of the Chamber*, released into the redeemer’s hand and the client’s debt discharged. The account closed. The name of the redeemer withheld from every party, the pledgor included, by the custom of the Chamber and at the redeemer’s wish.

Then he folded his hands and looked up across the lamp.

“Is it Your Highness’s will, spoken aloud, that this be done?”

Caleb thought of Hild at the anvil under the burning runes of the dome, with the link going from white to gold.

He did not have to wait to know, this time.

“Yes.”

It was hardly louder than the pen, and the cold-iron walls took it in and did not ring.

He signed, and the ink went down black and shining in the light of the lamp, and the ship to Nauplion went down with it, with all hands, and the house in Lutèce with the balcony over the river. There was no seal to set beside his name, since his seal lay in a cold grey puddle on the floor of a hall on the Coast. Master Brandt took his thumb in ink instead, and wrote beneath it in his own hand that the Chamber had seen the client’s face and knew it. In the Holds, Caleb thought, that was probably worth more than any seal.

Master Brandt rang a little iron bell. The grey clerk came in with a casket of black wood bound in seven bars of iron, and set it on the table, and went out again, and Master Brandt unlocked it with a key from his belt and lifted the lid.

The ring lay on grey velvet.

It was heavier than he had thought. He knew that before he touched it, from the way it sat in the velvet. It was old gold, worn soft at the edges by a great many hands, with the stone sitting deep in its claws. Here in the cold-iron it was only a stone, black and without lustre, like a drop of ink that had dried before anyone could write with it. He picked it up. It was cold, and his hand did not warm it, and for a while he only sat and felt the weight of it in his fist.

Then he folded the paper with WILLING on one side and NOT YET on the other and held its corner to the lamp. Master Brandt lifted the glass for him without being asked. The paper caught slowly. Caleb watched the flame go along the edge and turn his column black, and the other column on the far side, and held it until it nearly reached his fingers, and then laid the last of it in the iron dish under the lamp and watched it go to grey.

“The Chamber keeps no papers,” said Master Brandt, “but its own.”

Caleb rose. Master Brandt rose with him, and came round the table, and bowed.

It was the bow of the fourth degree, and the light of the lamp slid down his three plaits as he bent. It was as deep as the bow with which he had been received, to the hair. Caleb, who had been raised at court and had watched the depth of other men’s bows change with his fortunes all his life, stood still and received it.

“The Chamber has held Your Highness’s portion since the twelfth day of Your Highness’s life. The Chamber regrets the loss of the client.” He considered. “It does not, as a rule.”

Caleb found that he could not answer that. He bowed instead, to the degree owed by a prince of the Solar House to a master of the Holds, which he had learned at seven from the dancing master with the cane, and had used only once before, to a smith in a scorched apron.

The night clerk took him back through the dark iron and opened the door with the seven bars, and he stepped out into the court of the Undergate.

His light woke all at once.

It came up from his heels to his crown in one long warm rush, like a breath he had been holding, and the gold rose in his wrists, and the warmth came back into his palms. And in his left fist, where he held the ring, something answered it. All along the empty court, the fine shadows of the Usher’s lines in the stone, hundreds of them, leaned the same way, towards his closed hand, and stayed leaning, as filings lean towards a lodestone, until he put his fist inside his coat and held it there against his chest.

\* \* \*

He went back up through the city by the long stairs, so as not to cross a bridge where anyone might see him. It was late, and the runes along the rails burned low and gold, as the Holds kept them at night.

At the turn of the passage below his room, in the house with no windows, a single rune lamp was lit on the wall, and Paul was standing under it.

He had his back to the passage and his head bowed, and his big hands cupped at his mouth as if he were sheltering a match from the wind. The lamp laid its gold along his knuckles, and something between them, where the fingers did not quite meet, caught it and gave back a colder light. Caleb stopped in the dark at the head of the stair. He could not see Paul’s face. He could hear his voice, low and close, a murmur that came and went under the hum, and for a moment he could make out none of it.

Then he heard *Highness*.

And then, after a while, *the Fault*. And *dusk*. And then *Highness* again.

After that there was nothing for a long time, as if he were listening. Then, so low that it was hardly breath, *Keep them well.*

Caleb stood still in the dark.

Lycans of the Highland March prayed into their hands. In his first year at the Citadel Paul had knelt at the shuttered guardroom window in the Citadel’s made night, homesick, with his hands cupped to his mouth so that the wind could not take the words before they reached a moon the Citadel never showed. There was no moon in the mountain either, and no wind. But Paul stood under the only lamp in the passage with his head bowed, speaking low of the road home, and of the dusk, and of his Highness, and asking whatever listened in the March to keep his mother and his sisters well.

Caleb’s throat closed.

He went back down three stairs, without a sound, and waited there in the dark with his fist against his coat until the murmur stopped and he heard Paul’s tread go away down the passage, slow and heavy. Then he went up to his room, and shut the door, and knocked twice on the lamp.

Vesna had packed a needle case.

He found it at the bottom of the basket, under the last of the Wildmarch, wrapped in a scrap of flannel and tied with kitchen string, with a note pinned to the flannel in a hand like a fence that had been mended many times. *For when His Highness tears something. Which he will.* There were needles in it of every size, and three cards of thread, black and white and a grey that matched nothing he owned.

He sat on the edge of the cupboard bed with his coat across his knees and opened the lining where the paper had been. The letter pocket a Citadel tailor had cut there was too shallow, and the ring pulled the silk out of true, so he unpicked three inches of the seam at the breast of the coat and made a place between the lining and the cloth, and laid the ring in that, where it lay flat and heavy and did not move.

He had been taught to close a wound with silk thread by the temple physicians of the Harp Vales, at fourteen, on the skins of oranges and then on the arms of the novices who had drawn the short straw. A coat lining was more forgiving than either. He threaded a needle with the black silk, and knotted it, and took his stitches close and even, one after another, with the gold of his own hands for a lamp, until the seam was shut and the ring was a hard round weight in the breast of the coat that no one would find unless they knew where to put their hand.

It lay where the paper had lain, beside his heart. The paper had weighed nothing at all.

He bit off the thread and put the case back under the tea. Then he knocked the lamp dark and lay down in the cupboard bed in his clothes, with the coat over him and his hand flat on the place, and felt the ring under his palm, cold through the silk, and his own heart going steadily beneath it.

He had nothing now. There was no door in his wall. He had bricked it up himself tonight, in seven courses, and written WILLING in the mortar.

He waited to be frightened. It did not come.

Far above him, at the top of the bronze well, the star had come back. It looked down the shaft at him with its one cold point of light, and he looked back up at it, and beneath him the forges of Dornhöhle went on with the work they had never once stopped, and the hum of it came up through the floor and the wall and the bed and into the ring against his heart, and into him.

Tomorrow at dusk they would take the Fault road home.
