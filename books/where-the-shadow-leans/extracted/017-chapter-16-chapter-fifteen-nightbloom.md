## Chapter 16: Chapter Fifteen - Nightbloom

*Of the nightbloom of the Obsidian Coast this house knows only what it has been told, for no physician of the Vales has had occasion to see it. The lords of the Coast wear it at table for its scent, and it is harmless to the Umbral houses and to the lycan. To the Solar blood it is a seed. In the sun it dies unopened. Out of the sun it lies quiet for a day, and then it opens, and the Grace, rising against it, does the rest. No remedy is known to this house.*

— From *Of the Poisons Peculiar to the Solar Blood*, a commentary of the physicians of the Harp Vales

\* \* \*

Eleanor’s yacht went up at the first bell, into an ash that had begun to fall in the night and showed no sign of stopping.

It had come down in the yacht court on the mountain’s flank, where Caleb’s own had come down ten weeks before with Caleb asleep in it, and it stood on the black flagstones with its step let down, white ashwood and gold with the Chancellery’s sunburst on its stern, and its lamps lay across the wet stone in long gold smears, like yolk broken on a slate. The ash sifted down through them, soft and grey. It settled on the white rails and the shoulders of the lycans at the arch, and on the spectacles of Eleanor’s clerk, who took them off and wiped them on his sleeve and put them back on and found them grey again at once, and gave it up.

Caleb had not slept a great deal. Every time he shut his eyes a pale flower had opened in a mulberry buttonhole, taking its time about it, and at some hour without a name he had woken with a sweetness at the back of his throat, faint and cold, like snow that has fallen through a lime tree in flower. He had put it down to his own foolishness, and dressed in the Coast’s black, with Eleanor’s violets in his right-hand pocket, and come down to see his sister off.

She stood at the foot of the step in a travelling cloak of the Chancellery’s blue, with the hood back and the ash settling on her hair, which bore it as it bore everything, without a strand out of place, drawing on her gloves.

“You will write.”

“I always write.”

“You write to Tilda, in a hand she can read on horseback. You write to me in the pence, and only ever when I have asked you something first.” She smoothed the first glove down over her knuckles, one at a time. “Write to me in the main figures.”

“The main figures are dreadfully dull.”

“Then make them interesting. You have never once found it difficult.” She took up the second glove. “I am taking the Throne’s warrant home with me. I shall give it back to the Crown Prince myself, in his own office, with my compliments.” Her voice was perfectly level. “I should like to see his face while he reads it.”

Eleanor held out her gloved hand to him, as the Arch-Chancellor gives her hand to a foreign prince before witnesses, and he bowed over it. When he straightened she did not let it go.

“You are cold to the fingertips.”

He had known it since he woke, the thin cold that comes into the hands of a clerk who has copied too long in an unheated room. He had been a physician long enough to know what he would tell a patient who complained of it, and a younger brother a great deal longer, and he knew what Eleanor would do if he told her. She would send the yacht up empty and sit down on the step in the ash with her despatch case open on her knees until she had found out what was the matter with him to the penny, and the warrant would go back to Andrew by some other hand, and Cecily would have nobody at her door but Matilda and a man with a pie cart.

*Stay*, he thought. *Stay a day. Sit in the garden and price the lemons.*

“Everything on the Coast is cold,” he said, “until it has made up its mind about you.”

“And has it?”

“I believe it has decided to keep me.”

Eleanor went on looking at him, at his face and his collar, as she looked at a column she had added twice and did not quite trust to stay added once her back was turned. Then she let go of his hand and turned to the arch.

Arian stood in it, at the edge of the yacht’s lamplight, in black, with the ash falling past him and none of it settling. Eleanor gave him her curtsey on the wet stone, the full depth owed to the head of a house, and rose from it with her chin up.

“Sovereign. I have kept my brother’s books since he was sixteen. He has never once balanced them.”

“I have seen his arithmetic.”

“Then you know what you have taken on.” She went up the step and turned at the top of it, with the gold of the cabin behind her and the ash going down before her face like a veil let fall. “I shall audit,” she warned.

Arian inclined his head to her, as far as the Coast inclines it to a creditor, and no further.

The step came up. The lamps along the yacht’s rails brightened as she passed them going in, as lamps always did for Eleanor, and the white hull lifted off the black stone with a sigh that set the ash whirling, and went up, turning its gold stern to the mountain. Caleb watched it go into the ash until the ash had it. Somewhere above all that grey, two days to the north, the noon was waiting for her at Chalmont.

He felt Arian come to his side. A cold hand touched the small of his back and was gone.

“Come in.”

At the arch Caleb coughed, once, and tasted that cold sweetness again, and put his hand to his coat. Behind the crescent the ring lay against his ribs, and under it, under the bone, something lay folded and cold and perfectly still, and he took it for the ring.

\* \* \*

By the Coast’s evening the gold chair had been his for six nights, and he had stopped pretending that he came up the Sovereign’s stair for any reason but to sit in it.

He sat in it now with his boots on the footstool and the fourth volume of the corsair novel open on the rug over his knees, and the grey cloak lying along the chair’s arm, where it had lived since the night it was put into his hands. The fire threw its gold down the room as far as the desk, where Arian sat among the Council’s papers with the frost creeping out from his hands across the black wood as rime creeps over a slate.

The papers were all about the breach, and every one wanted the Sovereign’s answer under his seal. Every so often, without looking up, Arian set his thumb to his eyetooth and laid a bead of blood on the fold and drew the shadow down into it out of the dark under the desk, and it hardened there, black and blank. Caleb had stopped watching him do it. Each time another blank seal set on the desk, he found something else to look at.

He looked at the page.

“‘*You keep books,*’ said the Lady Ysolde,” he read aloud, “‘*and yet you are a corsair.*’ ‘*A corsair,*’ said the corsair, ‘*has a great deal of time between ships.*’”

“That is not what it says,” said Arian, without lifting his pen.

“It is what it ought to say. The author has given him a speech about the loneliness of the open sea that goes on for a page and a half, and I have spared you it.” Caleb turned the leaf. “She is about to discover that he has had the library built for her. She faints. I have read ahead.”

He did the corsair low, and the Lady Ysolde in a voice of faintly bored correctness so like Eleanor reading out the Treasury rolls that on the third line a candle on the mantel guttered as if somebody had laughed near it, and Arian’s pen stopped on the paper and stayed stopped. The lady swept down a gallery of black glass with her skirts in her hand, and the corsair followed at a respectful distance with a lamp, and she came to a pair of doors bound with iron, and he gave her the key.

Caleb stopped reading.

The fire had stopped reaching him. He sat with his finger on the line and felt it go, as one feels a draught come in at a door somebody has opened at the far end of a house. The heat of the hearth came as far as his skin and lay there, on the backs of his hands and on his face, and waited, as a dog waits on a step it has been told not to cross.

He looked down at his hands on the page.

The gold was coming up in them, and he had not called it. It came in threads, quick and thin and hard, running along under the skin and stopping and turning and running again, as the lines run across a pane of glass when the frost begins on it, only these were gold, and they were moving, and they were looking for something. And between them, under them, where the threads had not yet run, there was something else. It was darker than the skin. It branched as the gold branched, but slowly, finely, pale at its edges, like the veins in a white petal held up before a lamp.

He laid two fingers on his left wrist, as the Vales had taught him at six, and counted.

His heart went on for nine beats, steady. On the tenth it was not there. Then it came, too hard, as a man who has missed a stair comes down hard on the next one, and went on.

“You have stopped.”

“I have lost my place.”

“Your finger is on it.”

Caleb looked at his finger on the page. “So it is.”

Arian had lifted his head and moved nothing else. He held it tilted, as Caleb had seen it held in a dark carriage, listening.

“Your heart.”

“Yes. I was counting it.”

“It missed.”

“Twice. Three times, now. I make it three.” His voice came out level. It was the voice the Vales had given him for telling a woman that her son would not walk again, low and plain and unhurried, so that there should be nothing in the room louder than the news. “Do you remember the flower? In the buttonhole of the lord in the mulberry coat. At the end of the song.”

Arian did not answer. The frost under his hands went out a full span across the black wood in one breath, and stopped.

“It opened while I watched it,” said Caleb. “I thought it prettily timed. It smelled of snow in a lime tree. It has been in my breath all day. I thought it was the wine.”

“You did not drink the wine.”

“No. I am not a very good physician tonight.” He looked at his wrist. The pale lines had gone a little further under the gold. “There was a man at the long table with gold at his nostrils, who watched me eat a scone and did not blink. I thought he was a little in love with me, which is the usual explanation. I think now that somebody wished to be sure where I was sitting.”

Arian put down his pen.

He rose and crossed to the shelf of beech-leaf green, and his hand went to what he wanted without looking. He drew a thick book from the middle of the row and stood holding it shut in both hands, as a man holds a letter he has already read and would give a great deal not to read again.

“You have read it.”

“Yes.”

“What does it say?”

Arian stood with his back to Caleb and the book in his hands, and the candles in the branched stand before the shelf leaned away from him one by one down the row.

“Tell me,” said Caleb. “I would rather hear it in your voice than in theirs.”

“That the sun kills it in the seed,” answered Arian. He turned round. “Your sister sailed at the first bell.”

“And I stayed.”

He said it lightly. He had meant it to be light, at any rate.

It was the last light thing he said for some time.

The bloom opened.

He felt it open. It opened under his breastbone, behind the ring, in the place where something had lain folded and cold all day, and it opened as the flower had opened in the lowered lamps, slowly, one petal and then the next, unhurried, as a thing opens that has never once in its life been made to hurry. Each petal was cold. Each was colder than the last, and with every one that unfolded the cold went out from it along the roads of his blood, into his arms and his throat and the backs of his eyes, and the Grace came up to meet it.

He had felt the Grace come up a hundred times, to a torn side in the lower yard and to a sun coming undone in a man’s chest, quick and willing, as water runs to the low place in a field. Now it rose as a fire rises in a house where the shutters have been left open in a gale, all at once and every way, and it had nowhere to go, because the thing it hunted was everywhere, in every road it ran along, and so it burned them all. He felt it go through him looking for the cold, and wherever it found the pale lines it tore through them to get at them, and through him with them, and the light came out of him at the wrists.

It came through the skin as light comes through the rents in an old awning when the noon is high behind it, bright and torn, and the skin went with it. It split along the gold threads at his wrists as the skin of a plum splits in a stewing pan, and the blood that welled into the splits boiled there and went off the backs of his hands in a fine red smoke that smelled of a smithy, and the light came on through the wounds as if they were only doors it had been waiting to have opened. At his throat it came out above the high black collar and scorched the jet buttons, and down every seam of the Coast’s silk where his gold had shone so prettily the night before it ran now in lines of white fire, and the silk browned along every one of them, and smoked. The book slid off the rug. The rug smouldered where his hands had lain on it.

All down the long room the candles stood up tall and white, every one, with a hiss like fat thrown on a fire, and then they guttered. The fire in the hearth went white to the back of the chimney. The shadows came to him off the walls and out from under the shelves, as they had come to lie at his feet in the great hall the night before, and his light caught them as they came and burned them, and they went up off him in black curls like the ash of a burnt letter, and still they came.

He reached for his light gently, to call it home, as one reaches for a bird that has got into the house, and it would not come. It was not his to call. It was hunting.

He was on the floor.

The gold chair was above him and the fire beyond it was white, and he was lying on the hearthstone with the light tearing out of his wrists, and two hands held him under the shoulders, and they were cold, and he could smell them burning. He turned his head and saw the frost on Arian’s sleeves going up into steam where his light touched it, and the white hands under his shoulders reddening, blistering, as they had blistered on a doorframe on the first day. The blisters rose and broke and rose again, and the skin of Arian’s palms came away on the scorched silk in wet white flakes, like the skin lifted off scalded milk, and the hands did not let go.

“Let go,” Caleb gasped. “You will burn.”

“Yes.”

He drew Caleb up against him, into the black silk, into the cold, and held him there with the light going into him at every point it touched and the steam going up off both of them together, and his face, an inch from Caleb’s own, was white and quite still, and his eyes were not still at all.

The pale thing turned over behind Caleb’s breastbone and opened another petal, and the Grace went after it and tore. He bit through the inside of his cheek and tasted blood and snow in a lime tree together, and he heard himself make a sound he did not recognise.

His hand went to his breast of itself, as it had gone every night of the road home in a dark carriage, and closed on the black crescent and held on, hard, because it was the one place on him that was still and cold and his own, and everything else in him was coming apart.

“Arian,” he said.

He had never said it. He had said *Sovereign*, and *Don Conti*, and before the Nine in the great hall the night before he had said *I am his* without once saying whose. He said it now on the hearthstone, through his teeth, with his light tearing out of him in rags, and every shadow in that long room that he had not yet burned stopped where it was.

Then they came to him all together, the whole dark of the room, and lay down over him, and burned, and did not move away.

“I am here.”

“The garden.” Caleb had to find each word and pick it up, as a child picks up pebbles one at a time from the bottom of a beck. “The light. The stones. It keeps it shut. The flower kept shut all night under the lamps. It only opened when they turned the lamps down.”

Arian understood him. He had always understood him faster than was reasonable.

“Yes.” He stood up with Caleb in his arms.

\* \* \*

The galleries went by him in pieces.

A lycan housemaid flattened herself against a wall with her brush held to her breast, her face lit white by him as he went past. Overhead the violet lamps went by on their long chains, and each one leaned down to him as he passed under it and flinched back up, as a hand flinches from a hot griddle. Someone ran ahead of them, calling down the stairwells in the Wildmarch tongue, and against his cheek, through the black silk, he felt Arian’s heart going as his own was going, missing, catching, missing, beat for beat, as if the two of them had missed the same stair, and he could not think about it. He thought that Arian ought to have his hands seen to, and that there was nobody in the fortress who could do it but Caleb, and that Caleb was being carried.

“Your hands,” he told the black silk. “I shall want to see them. Afterwards. Do not let anybody put butter on them.”

Arian took the next flight in two strides and said nothing at all.

The air grew warm. It was the giants’ stair, going down into the breath of the mountain’s heart, and Arian went down it so fast that Caleb felt the steps only as a long falling, and at the bottom the corridor, and the iron doors.

Arian did not set him down. He shifted Caleb in his arms and took his ring hand in his own blistered one, and pressed the iron ring into the hollow in the doors.

The runes woke red across the iron and went to gold, and the ring on Caleb’s finger woke with them. It went warm, warmer than his finger, as it had at the second stroke of the hammer in the Hall of the Chain, and the thread the hammer had drawn that day between his chest and another pulled taut, so tight that it hurt, and through it, for one heartbeat, he felt the burns on the hands that held him as if they were his own.

The doors parted.

The gold came out over them both. It came down the corridor in a warm wave, the clean roaring gold of twelve suns that were not suns, and it lay on Caleb’s face, and he felt the thing behind his breastbone feel it too. It stopped opening. It lay half-folded, as the flower had lain in the buttonhole all evening under the lamps, and the Grace, finding the light pouring into it from outside, faltered in its hunting and came a little way back towards him, like a child who hears its name called across a fair and cannot tell from where.

Arian stood at the threshold, with Caleb in his arms and the light on his face and his blistered hands, and his frost going up off him in a thin bright smoke. He did not step back out of it, and he could not go in.

“Tomas.”

Somebody came up the iron stairs from the garden.

He was the largest lycan Caleb had ever seen, a man as broad as the doorway and grey at the temples, in a leather apron with black earth to the elbows, with Mirek’s ears and a face that looked as if it had been left out in a great deal of weather and had not minded, and he did not say a word. He held out his arms.

So that was Tomas, thought Caleb, from a long way off. He was not at all good at not being seen. He was only very good at being somewhere else.

Arian put Caleb into his arms.

He did it carefully, across the sill, as a man hands something over a wall he cannot climb, and when his hands came back out of the light the blisters on them had broken, and the palms were raw to the meat and weeping, and there was frost forming over them again already, white over red. He looked past them at Caleb.

“He is to stay in the light. Nobody carries him out of it.”

\* \* \*

Tomas carried him down the iron stairs under the slow wheel of the crystals, and their light lay on him like a palm laid on a fevered brow, and held him. Under it the pale thing in his chest lay half open and opened no further, and the Grace ran up and down in him like a hound on a lost scent, and every so often, when it found the pale lines in his wrists, the light tore out of him in a tatter and scorched the leather of Tomas’s sleeve.

Tomas did not seem to mind that either.

They laid him on the lowest terrace, on rugs and a mattress of sacking stuffed with straw in the long bed of golden lilies by the water-stair, where the light fell thickest and no part of him would lie in shadow. The lilies bent under him and gave up their warm green smell. The water went past an arm’s length away over its stone lip.

Vesna came down the iron stairs at a run.

He had never seen her run. She had flour on her forearms and her skirts gathered in one fist, and Paul behind her, and she dropped to her knees in the lilies beside him without seeing what she knelt in, and put her hand on his forehead, as she had on the morning after the gallery, and took it away again quickly. Her palm was red.

“His Highness is burning.”

“His Highness is aware,” said Caleb.

“Paul. Water. As cold as it comes, from the top of the stair, where it comes in. Tomas, the linen from the press in the east wing, all of it, and the big ewer. Go.”

Paul did not go at once. He stood over the mattress with his big hands open at his sides, and his face did something Caleb had seen it do only once before, on the stones of the lower yard, torn open.

“Walker,” said Caleb, because the face would not stop doing it. “You carried me up a stair once with a turned ankle, and into the sun once with a great deal worse, and you have never dropped me yet. Go on. She will only shout.”

Then Paul turned and went up the terraces towards the head of the water-stair at a stumbling run, and Tomas went the other way, up the iron stairs, without a sound.

Vesna began on the buttons.

The jet buttons were hot. She undid them anyway, one after another, from the collar down, with her mouth set, and blew on her fingers between them, and the silk fell open over his shirt along seams that were brown and brittle and still smoking where the light had come through them. She slid her hand in under the silk at his shoulder to draw it off him.

Caleb’s hand was still on the crescent. He had not let go of it since the hearthstone, and as Vesna drew the silk back from his shoulder he felt it begin to go out from under his fingers, and held it harder.

“Let go, *malyshka*. It’s burning you.”

“No.”

“It’s half burnt through already. I’ll have them make you another —”

“Vesna.”

Her hands stopped.

Her fingers lay flat on the black silk under his own, on the crescent, and under the stitching, inside the lining, there was something round and hard and heavy, and cold in the middle of all that heat. He felt her feel it. Her fingers went still on it, and then pressed, lightly, once, as a woman presses the crust of a loaf to know whether it is done.

She looked at him.

“He is not to know,” Caleb whispered. “Not from you. Not from anyone. Not until I give it to him.”

Vesna was silent so long that he thought she had not understood him. Then her thumb moved a little along the black stitches beside the crescent, close and square and neat, the stitches of a boy who had learned to suture on orange skins in the Harp Vales and had sewn nothing since but people.

“That’s my thread. I’d know it anywhere.”

“You gave me the case.”

“I did.” Her voice had gone rough. “I said His Highness would tear something.”

“I have,” Caleb admitted. “I have torn nearly everything.”

She took her hand away. She drew the coat off him, carefully, his left arm and then his right, keeping the breast of it folded under her palm the whole while so that nothing in it should knock against anything, and watching his face. She folded it. She folded it with the crescent turned in, against the lining, so that what lay outermost was only the scorched black back of a coat that had been worn one night in the great hall and one morning in the ash, and she lifted his head with one hand and slid it under him, and laid his head back down on it.

“There. You keep your own pillow. Nobody’ll touch it but me, and I’m an old woman, and I can’t see a thing on a black coat without my spectacles.”

“You do not wear spectacles.”

“Then I can’t see a thing at all,” said Vesna, and put her red palm back on his forehead, and held it there.

He lay with the ring under his head and her hand on his brow, and for a little while the Grace in him only paced, back and forth, as a sentry walks a wall, and he could think.

“Listen. You must be my hands. There is no physician in this fortress who can stand in this light but me, and I cannot sit up. Keep me where it falls thickest. If the Grace comes out of me in the wrists, put my hands in the water, not on the linen. Count my heart here.” He moved her fingers to his throat. “At the turn of every crystal. If it misses more than four in a hundred, send to the Sovereign. If it misses more than ten —” He stopped, because her face had changed. “If it misses more than ten, send to him anyway. It will make no difference, but he will want to have been sent for.”

Vesna’s mouth worked, and closed.

“And the Sovereign?” she asked. “He’ll want to come in.”

Caleb turned his head on the folded coat.

From the lowest terrace a man could look up the whole throat of the garden, past the roses and the crystals turning in their spiral, to the doors at the top. They stood open. In the doorway, framed in black stone and iron where the light gave out, a tall figure stood with his hand on the jamb, as he had stood there on the first day, and looked down into the garden, with the frost coming up white again on his hands.

“He is not to come in,” Caleb insisted. “Whatever he says. Whatever I say. If I ask for him, you are to tell me I am a fool and he would burn.”

“He would burn,” said Vesna.

“Yes. So you see I am not entirely a fool.”

A man who could hear a heart miss across the width of a cave could hear a prince speak across a garden. Caleb saw the hand on the doorframe close on the iron and stay closed, and knew that he had been heard.

The Grace turned over in him and found the cold, and tore.

Before the light went white he saw the figure in the doorway at the top of the garden. It had not moved. It stood at the very edge of the gold, and came not one inch further, because Caleb had asked it not to.

\* \* \*

From the threshold, the garden was a well of light with the prince at the bottom of it.

Arian could see him as plainly as if he lay at his feet, in his shirtsleeves among the lilies, with Vesna on her knees beside him and his two hands in the water of the stair to the wrist. Every so often the light broke from him there. It came up through the clear running water in a white flare that lit the undersides of the roses on the terrace above and threw the shadows of the lemon leaves up the black walls of the vent, and the water hissed over his wrists and went on down the stair stained pink, and then the flare went down, and the garden was only gold again, and the prince lay still in it.

And Arian could hear his heart, under the hum of the crystals and the talk of the water and Vesna’s voice counting, *odin, dva, tri*, at the turn of every crystal as she had been told. It was missing more than four in a hundred. Arian had counted every beat since the hearthstone, and he could not have stopped if he had wished to.

Under his own ribs, his own heart missed with it.

It had kept the prince’s time for twenty years, since a glasshouse and a boy’s voice counting, and it had never once kept any other. He had thought it the only thing he had ever been given and not taken. It went with the prince’s now. When the prince’s heart missed, his own missed, and there was a space under his ribs where nothing moved, as there had been nothing there for the first twelve years of his life, and then the prince’s came back too hard, and so did his.

If the prince’s stopped, he had no notion what his own would do. He found he had no wish to know, and very little care.

The broken blisters on his palms had begun to weep under the frost and freeze where they wept. He did not look at them. He stood with them on the iron and made a list, because there was nothing else his hands could do, of whoever had grown the flower and whoever had cut it, and the lord who had pinned it in his mulberry buttonhole, and the tongue that had told the harbour at what bell the lamps would go down. He went down it as he went down the prince’s heart, beat by beat, and name by name where he had names, with no more feeling than a clerk carrying a figure to the foot of a page.

The light lay across the iron sill a hand’s breadth from his boots. He had sat on that sill on more nights than he could count with his back to the frame, and he had not wished himself across it. He stood on it now with his hand on the iron and the frost growing back white over his broken blisters, and wished it so hard that the iron under his palm had begun to rime over, and he did not move.

*He is not to come in. Whatever he says. Whatever I say.*

The household physician had said everything that could be said by a vampire who could not go within six paces of his patient, and the yacht for the Harp Vales had gone at the third bell, and would be four days there and four back.

Arian had read the green book at nineteen, by a fire he could not feel. He had read the page on the nightbloom twice, as he read everything about that blood twice, and come to *No remedy is known to this house*, and turned the page. No Alorian had sat at a table on the Coast in a thousand years. It had seemed to him, at nineteen, a thing that need never be thought of again.

He had made a garden out of the throat of a dead volcano. He had hung twelve suns in it, and filled a wall with green books, and lit a fire every night since the first for hands that were cold, and it had not occurred to him to take a flower out of his own hall.

On the water-stair the light went up through the water again in a white flare, and the prince’s heart missed, and caught.

Arian’s missed, and caught.

\* \* \*

Walker came up the iron stairs to him at the ninth bell, with a ewer in his hands that he had forgotten he was carrying.

He stopped on the mezzanine a few paces short of the doors, in the full light, with the water slopping over the lip of the ewer onto his boots. His heart was going like a hare’s in a snare, fast and light and high.

“Sovereign,” said Walker.

“Walker.”

“Let me take him home to the sun.” He said it in a rush, as a man says a thing he has been saying over to himself on a stair. “There’s the Chancellery’s yacht, it can’t have got far, it could be turned. Two days, Sovereign, and he’d be in the noon. The noon kills it. He told Vesna so himself. He could be well by the week’s end, he could —”

“It has opened.”

“Then the physicians at the Citadel —”

“The Citadel gave him a cup of wine.”

Walker’s face went grey. The ewer tipped in his hands, and the last of the water ran out through the lattice of the mezzanine floor and fell, glittering, a long way, into the light.

“I know,” said Walker. It came out of him so low that a man with a mortal’s ears would not have heard it. “I know it did.”

His heart stopped.

It did not beat for the space of three of the prince’s, and then it came down hard, and went on, and Walker stood on the iron with his eyes on the floor and his shoulders shaking, without a sound, as a man weeps who has been taught that it is not done in front of officers.

Arian had meant for some weeks to find out what was wrong with that heart. He did not ask now. Walker had carried the prince into the sun once, on Arian’s order, and he could kneel beside him in that light and hold a cup to his mouth, which Arian could not. Arian had a use for him yet.

“Go down to him. Do not take him anywhere.”

Halfway down the iron stairs Walker stopped, on the turn above the second terrace, and put his two hands together before his mouth, cupped, and stood so with his head bowed, as the Highland March prays, into the hands, where the wind cannot get at the words.

There was no wind in the garden either. Arian listened to his heart, which did not slow.

\* \* \*

His uncle came along the violet dark of the corridor at the tenth bell, and stopped six paces short of the light, where his blood obliged him to stop.

“Arian,” said Enzo.

“Zio.”

Enzo did not ask how the prince did. He had been listening to the same heart all the way down the giants’ stair. He stood in the dark beyond the light with a sheet of paper in his hand, and his face did nothing at all.

“The harbour guildmaster sat four hours at your table with his eyes open. He remembers none of it.”

“He was looking at the prince.”

“His eyes were. I am not certain that he was.”

He turned the paper to the edge of the light, and Arian read it from the sill. It was written in silver in the same square, hurried hand as the half-sheet from the harbour, and it was as short.

*The brick is still there. The quarter says the dust-wife keeps a cellar behind it, and grows flowers in it, in the dark, and feeds them on sunshine.*

Arian had sat at that table. He had seen the pale flower in the mulberry buttonhole, shut tight under every lamp in the hall for four hours together, and he had seen the lamps turned down for the song, because the Coast had turned them down for that song for six hundred years and every soul at the table had known that it would. He had not watched the flower open. He had been watching the prince.

The frost on the iron under Arian’s hand went out across the face of the doors in a white sheet, over the sleeping runes, as far as the hollow, and stopped there.

“And the Arch-Chancellor.”

“She breathed it too, and sailed into the noon,” said Enzo. “An envoy who would go home, and a prince who would not.”

*And I stayed*, the prince had said by the fire, lightly, as if it were a pleasantry.

Arian took his hand off the iron.

He looked once more at the man in the lilies, whose head lay on a scorched black coat folded so that the crescent of House Conti was turned inward, under his cheek. Vesna’s doing, he supposed. Something of the house for him to lie on. The shadows of the doorframe had gone out across the iron sill towards it as far as the light allowed, and lay there pointing, as they pointed to Arian himself across every room of his life, and he let them.

“Summon the Nine. And the household. All of it.”

Enzo drew a second paper out of the breast of his coat, from behind the broken chain he kept there. It was worn furry at the folds and closely written in Enzo’s own neat hand, in several inks, the oldest gone brown.

“In case.”

\* \* \*

The long tables of the gala were gone from the great hall, but nobody had been willing to be the one who threw away a gift of the Ashward, and the oranges had been carried to a sideboard against the wall, pyramid and silver stand and card and all. They stood there with their dark red hearts open to the violet lamps, beginning faintly to smell of the end of something.

The Nine came in their night silks, with their hair unbound and their faces composed. The household came in its aprons and lined the walls as far as the great doors, grooms and housemaids and clerks with their pens behind their ears, and the sergeant of the escort at the end of the line with his fist already on his heart. Don Salvatore came in garnet, as if he had dressed for the summons some time ago, with his granddaughter at his side. The lord of the inland vineyards came last of the Nine in his mulberry velvet, and took his chair.

There was a nightbloom in his buttonhole. It was fresh, white and long-throated and as large as the first, and it was shut tight under the lamps.

Arian did not take the high seat. He stood at the foot of the dais on the black glass, and at his right hand the glass chair stood empty. The thread of gold that slept in it had gone out. He had seen it catch like a wick under the prince’s palms on the night of the warrant, and tonight the glass was only black.

He did not look at it again.

“Consigliere.”

Enzo stood at the end of the Nine with his soft old paper, and read from it as he read the Council’s minutes.

“The master of the cellar beneath this floor,” he read. “Ninety-one years at the foot of the old vent.”

A man in a leather apron near the doors made a sound and stopped making it. He was white to the eyebrows, and he had carried the blood-wine up that stair for Arian’s father’s table, and for his grandfather’s.

“Off this mountain,” said Arian.

The sergeant of the escort took his fist from his heart. “By the road, Sovereign?”

“No.”

Two of the lycans along the wall took the master of the cellar by the arms, not roughly, and he went out between them by the great doors with his apron still on. He did not struggle. He had listened under that floor for ninety-one years, and he knew the house he had sold.

“Two clerks of this Council’s secretariat,” read Enzo, “in the Ashward’s pay since the last Long Eclipse. The under-steward of this hall, who sent to the harbour to say at what bell the lamps would be lowered.”

Nobody had to point to them. The candle flames along the walls had begun to lean, every one, towards three people in the line of the household, as flames lean towards a door left open, and the household had stepped away from them on either side without being told. There were two young men with ink on their cuffs and their pens behind their ears, and a grey woman with the hall’s keys at her belt, who had turned the lamps down for the song with her own hand.

Arian closed his hand.

Their shadows stood up off the black glass. They rose as smoke stands up off a wick pinched out, all three, each as tall as the one who had cast it, and closed on them from the feet up, as a fist closes on a handful of straw. Arian heard three hearts go high and fast as wrens’ hearts, and then the bones went, all together, with a sound like a sack of walnuts trodden underfoot. When the shadows lay down again there was nobody left standing in that part of the line. What lay on the glass lay folded in ways that nobody is made to fold, in livery and inky cuffs, and the blood came out from under it slowly into the violet light and froze as it came, as wine spilled on a winter step sets before anyone can fetch a cloth. One of the pens rolled a little way across the floor towards the Nine, and stopped.

A housemaid by the wall put her apron to her mouth. Nobody else moved. Don Salvatore looked at the pen with mild interest, as a man looks at a card somebody else has played.

Enzo turned the paper over. “There are eleven more. They know who they are.”

“By the road,” said Arian. “Before the bell.”

As he spoke the candle flames along the walls drew down into their wicks, all together, from the high seat to the great doors, as a field of barley goes down under a wind that nobody can see, and stayed there, blue, no bigger than seeds.

“The household needs no seal,” said Enzo, to the Nine.

The eleven went in a long white silence down the walls and out by the great doors, stepping wide of the red on the glass, and the potboy went last of all, a lycan of fourteen with a gold shimmer at his nostrils, who looked back once from the doors at the Sovereign without blinking, as if somebody else were looking out of him and wished to see this to the end.

Arian let him look.

Somewhere beyond the doors, out on the flank of the mountain, a heart that had listened at the foot of a vent for ninety-one years went over the lip of the yacht court, where the black flagstones ended and there was nothing under them but the night and the falling ash. Arian heard it all the way down. It went faster and faster as it fell. Then it stopped against the rocks at the mountain’s foot, a long way below, and the ash went on coming down on the place where it had stopped.

Arian turned to the Nine.

A clerk came down from the secretariat, shaking, with a sheet of the Coast’s black paper, and Arian wrote on it in silver where it lay across the clerk’s forearm. Every vampire in that hall could read silver on black at forty paces, and every one of them read it as he wrote.

*The house of Caruso is put out of Castel Nerezza, and its creatures with it, before the next bell. The lord of the inland vineyards is stripped of his chair among the Nine, and is to be held, for the poisoning of the Sovereign’s consort, until the Sovereign has done with him.*

He set his thumb to his tooth and laid the blood on the fold, and called the shadow up into it out of the floor, and it set like jet, and blank.

He held it out.

The lord of the inland vineyards rose from his chair without haste, as an old man rises who has sat a long while by a fire, though his face was as smooth as Arian’s own, and his voice, which had kept the centuries his face had not, carried to the doors.

“I ask to see the device.”

Nobody moved.

“I asked ten days ago, when the Sovereign closed the harbour quarter from a cave in the Fault, and I was not shown it. The Consigliere will tell us what the law says.”

Arian watched his uncle’s face, and saw what it would cost him, and saw him pay it.

“Any decree sealed without the Sovereign’s ring,” said Enzo, “may be contested by any of the Nine. Being contested, it lies upon the table of the Council, and is not obeyed.” He folded the paper.

“Then I contest it,” said the lord. “I wore a flower to his table.” He touched the white bud in his buttonhole with one finger, lightly, as one touches the cheek of a sleeping child. “Half the Nine have worn it. The Sovereign’s own father wore it to his wedding. If the Sovereign means to hang every lord of the Coast who has worn a nightbloom to supper, he will want a longer rope than the Coast can —”

The oranges went first.

The frost came over them on their silver stand from the base of the pyramid to its point in one white breath, and they froze to the heart, all of them, so that for an instant the whole tall heap stood there like garnets in a jeweller’s window. Then they split, all together, with a sound like a wet log in a hot grate, and went across the black glass floor in a red frozen hail.

The floor was cracking. It went out from under Arian’s boots in white lines, as ice goes on a pond under somebody who has walked out too far, in a star, under the chairs of the Nine, and the frost came up out of every crack as it ran. The violet lamps were going out down the length of the vaulting, drowned one after another, as a lamp drowns that is let down into a well. And the shadows of the hall rose up.

They came as they had come in the gorge of the Fault, with a sound like wind going through a loft of drying sheets, every shadow in that hall, past the Sovereign and up the mulberry velvet from the hem like water rising in a cellar, cold and black and soundless, to the lord’s knees and his folded hands, and held him there, and went on rising, to the white flower in his buttonhole, to his throat.

The lord stood in them and could not move, and his smooth face looked at the Sovereign through the dark with the patience of a man who has been told by his house what will happen next and is waiting for it.

There was a red rim at the edge of Arian’s sight. He had seen the world through it in a gorge with a sun-blade in his side, and on the glass of a black gallery, and first of all at twelve, kneeling in the roses. It was coming in from the edges and narrowing the hall, and in the middle of it there was a smooth face above a white shut flower, and nothing else in the world.

He could close his hand.

The shadows were his. They were round a lord’s throat, waiting for the hand to close, and when it closed the lord would lie on the glass as the three by the wall lay, folded over his own flower where nobody would ever find it, and he would be the fifth that night.

And the Nine would stand up before the next bell, all of them that were left, because the law of this Council was very old and very plain, and a Sovereign who would not be bound by it was a Sovereign who might be asked about. They would stand in this hall, in the dark he had made of it, and ask the shadows. And the shadows of this hall had leaned two ways the night before, half to the high seat and half to a prince in black with his gold running in his seams, and Arian did not know which way they would lean tonight. If they did not lean to him he would be a Sovereign of nothing. He could keep nothing, not a seat or a seal or a voice in this Council, and not a consort, whom the law kept only for as long as he was Sovereign, and not a garden under the mountain with a door that only one ring opened, and not the one who lay at the bottom of it in the light with his hands in the water, and his heart missing, and his head on a scorched coat, waiting to be fetched home by whoever had a warrant.

The red came in from the edges of the hall.

He opened his hand.

He did it slowly, one finger at a time, as a hand opens that has been closed a long while, and there was nobody standing beside him to lay a hand over it. The shadows went down off the mulberry lord as a tide goes down off a rock, and lay down on the black glass, and the red went out of the edges of the hall.

The lamps stayed drowned. The hall stood in the dark, lit only by the frost in the cracks of the floor and the blue seeds of the candles along the walls.

The mulberry lord straightened his cuffs.

“I thank the Sovereign.”

In his buttonhole, in the dark, the nightbloom had begun to open.

“The Sovereign is distressed,” said Don Salvatore. He stayed in his chair, and his voice came out of the dark as gentle as a voice at a sickbed. “Nobody here would think of asking the shadows anything at such an hour, cousin. Sit down.”

The mulberry lord sat down.

Arian kept his eyes off Salvatore. He did not trust what the shadows would do if he looked at him, and he had used up all the opening of hands he had in him for one night. He looked along the Nine instead, and so he saw the granddaughter.

Valentina Caruso was not looking at the Sovereign. She was looking at the white flower opening in the mulberry buttonhole, petal by petal in the dark, and her hand had gone up to the black thorn at her throat and closed on it so hard that her glove had split across the knuckle. Then she looked at her grandfather, at the side of his smooth pleasant face, for a long time, as a woman looks at a ledger she has kept all her life in her own hand and has found a line in it that she did not write.

She did not touch his arm.

“The Council will rise,” said Arian, to the dark hall.

It went out by the great doors in its night silks, quickly, over the frozen red of the oranges and wide of the other red by the wall, and the mulberry lord went among them with the nightbloom wide on his breast, and did not hurry.

When the doors had closed Enzo came down the cracked floor and stopped two paces off from his nephew in the dark.

“Any of the Nine may walk behind another,” he murmured. “The law has never once troubled to say otherwise.” He put the soft old paper away behind the chain.

Arian understood him. The lord would go to whoever had grown his flower before the night was out, because the Ashward was a tidy house, and he would not look behind him, because Don Salvatore had just told him before the whole of the Nine that he had nothing on the Coast to fear.

“I will walk with you.”

Enzo looked at him, and then away down the hall, towards the stair that went down to the garden.

“He has forbidden me the door.”

Whoever had grown the thing might know how it was undone, if anybody did. Enzo inclined his head a quarter of an inch, as he did whenever the Sovereign said a thing he would not have said himself and could find no argument against.

\* \* \*

They went down the mountain on foot by the switchback road, as the shadow of a cloud goes down a hillside, faster than a horse and quieter than the cloud. Below them the harbour lay in its long black bay with the lamps of the quays strung round it like the beads of a broken necklace, and a long way ahead a man in mulberry velvet walked alone with a lantern, a little gold spark going down into the dark, and never once looked behind him.

The road went down past the walls of the Ashward’s harbour gardens. Over the coping the nightbloom stood open in long white rows, every throat of it turned up into the falling ash, and the slope smelled of snow in a lime tree. A lantern went along between the rows, and the flowers shut as it came to them and opened again behind it. Under it a woman in long gardening gloves was cutting the blooms one at a time into a basket, as another woman might cut roses for a supper table, and she never lifted her head to the road.

“Donna Ottavia,” said Enzo, without slowing.

Arian knew her, the Don’s daughter, who kept the Ashward’s gardens, and he took no breath until they were past the wall. He looked at her gloves as he went by, the long pale leather stained green to the elbow, so that he would know them again.

The Caruso quarter climbed the hill behind the harbour in steps and alleys too narrow for a carriage, between rope-walks and net lofts and tall black houses leaning together over the lanes as if they had a great deal to say to one another and wished the street would mind its own business. The ash lay on everything, and under the ash there was gold. It lay along the doorsteps and in the cracks of the cobbles and in the folds of the nets hung out to dry, sifted down off a great many sleeves over a great many years, and it glittered faintly in the light of the lord’s lantern going on ahead, like a frost under new snow.

And in the doorways there were sleepers.

They sat on the net-loft steps and the boarding-house sills, propped against the jambs with their hands loose in their laps, lycans and sailors and harbour folk of a dozen kinds, with gold rimming their nostrils and their eyes wide. Nothing about them moved as two vampires went by in the dark but their eyes, and every pair on that hill turned, slowly, all together, to follow the two of them up the street, as the heads of cattle at a gate turn after a passing cart.

One pair did not turn. A man sat on the bottom step of a boarding house with his chin on his breast and his hands palm up in his lap, as if he had been given something and forgotten what, and the ash lay on him unbrushed, as thick on his open eyes as on his shoulders. The gold at his nostrils had gone dark, crusted over with the blood that had come out after it. Nobody on that street had moved him. Nobody on that street could.

And their hearts were wrong.

Every heart on that street beat slow, much slower than a sleeper’s ought, and every one beat at the same moment as every other, the lycans’ and the sailors’ and a child’s on a step at the turn of the lane, one beat all together and then a long wait and then another, as if there were only one heart on that whole hill, beating in all of them at once.

“Zio,” breathed Arian.

“I hear it.”

The lord’s lantern turned into an alley at the top of the hill, under the overhang of a net loft, and stopped. There was a door there. It was a low door, of old planks painted a blue that the ash and the years had made grey, and over its lintel a single nightbloom had been hung up by the stalk, head down, to dry, gone the brown of old paper. Arian could have sworn there had been no door in that wall a moment before.

The lord knocked once, went in, and drew the door to behind him.

Enzo was at it before it closed. He set the flat of his hand against the grey planks, as a man sets his hand flat on a page to stop it turning, and the door, which had been about to stop being there, stayed.

They went in.

\* \* \*

It was warm inside, and close, and it smelled of honey.

The room ran long and low under the net loft, with the nets hung from the beams overhead like the grey sails of a ship that has been brought indoors to sleep, and the gold lay on everything in it, on the floorboards thick enough to take the print of a boot, and on the hundred jars along the walls with their mouths tied in paper, and on the long board under the window, where a knife lay beside a little heap of it. It glittered in the light of one tallow candle and hung in the air, so that the whole room was faintly bright with it, like a kitchen where somebody has just shaken out a floured cloth.

Along the walls, on stools and on a long bench under the shelves, nine sleepers sat in a row with their hands in their laps and their eyes open, an old lycan woman in a fishwife’s shawl and a young sailor with rings in his ears and a man in a clerk’s black coat, and others, and all of them had turned their open eyes to the door, and were looking at Arian.

A cat sat on the long board in the gold beside the knife, a harbour cat, enormous and ragged and of no colour at all under the dust in its fur, and its yellow eyes did not blink either.

And in the chair by the board, with her back to the window, sat the dust-wife.

She was old as nobody on the Coast was old. The lycans grow old grey and broad and slowing, and she had grown old as a thing does that has been left a long while in a cupboard, and gone the colour of the cupboard. She sat wrapped in shawl over shawl of what might once have been fishing net, knotted and patched and grey, and her two hands lay out of them on the arms of the chair gold to the wrist with the dust, as if she wore gloves of it. Her face under her cap was brown and folded and kind, the face of anybody’s grandmother at the end of a long street, waiting on a step for a child who has been sent for bread.

Her eyes were white.

They were white all through, without a mark in them, the white of the inside of an eggshell, and they were open, and they looked at nothing at all.

“There. The little Conti. And the widower. I saw you come up the hill out of eleven pair of eyes, my lords, and you were handsome in all of ’em. And I saw your hall tonight, little Conti, out of my potboy. You’ve made a terrible mess of your floor.” Her voice was thin and light and pleasant, with the harbour in it, and it went up and down like a woman singing to herself over a sink. “Shut the door, my duck. There’s a draught, and it gets in the jars.”

The lord of the inland vineyards stood beside her chair with his lantern in his hand and his flower open on his breast.

Enzo shut the door.

“Stand, then,” said the dust-wife comfortably. “Your kind always stand.” She turned her white eyes towards the cat on the board, and the cat turned its yellow ones on Arian and looked him up and down. “Oh, you’re thinner than you were. I said so at the gala, out of the guildmaster. Nan Corrow, I said, that one’s not eating. I can’t see into you, of course. Your kind don’t breathe enough to be any use to me. But I can see you very nice out of a cat.”

“You brewed it,” said Arian.

“I grew it,” corrected Nan Corrow. “Brewing’s for ale. I grew it in my cellar in the dark three months, on sunshine and nothing else, so it’d know the taste of light and go looking for it. A harbour nightbloom’d give an elf a headache. Mine’s a deal more choosy.” She smiled, with very few teeth. “I sat in the guildmaster at the long table and watched the bright one watch it open, like a little one watching the lanterns lit on a feast night, the dear. He had a scone in his hand. He ate the whole of the first one.”

She was quiet a moment, with her gold hands folded in the nets, and the cat on the board looked at nothing.

“I’ve grown for the Ashward since I could hold a knife, and my mother before me. I never once wished it was a different flower.” The white eyes did not move. “I wished it at that table, watching him. I don’t know why. He never looked at me but the once.”

The frost came out from Arian’s boots across the gold on the floorboards, and the gold under it went white, and dull, and the cat on the board laid its ears back flat.

“The cure.”

“Now there you’ve come to the wrong house, little Conti. I never have the cure to anything I make. The Don don’t allow it. If I had the bottle and the cork both, I could sell the bottle to the Don and the cork to whoever he’d poured it for, and be paid twice, and there’s nothing a Caruso hates worse than a body being paid twice. So the Don keeps the corks. Every cure to every bottle I’ve made in twenty year, and every one my mother made before me. He keeps ’em under the iron, where no hand on the Coast can reach.”

“Where?”

Nan Corrow smiled, and did not answer, and the nine along the wall drew one breath together, and let it go.

“You watched the Grand Alchemist’s door,” said Enzo, in his ledger voice.

“That I did,” answered Nan Corrow, and on the bench the old fishwife turned her open eyes to him. “Out of a girl that sits on a step across from the foundry, down in the Underglow. She takes my cut of a rest-day. The Underglow’s full of my windows, top to bottom.” She folded her gold hands. “That’s what I am, widower, if you want to know, and nobody ever does want to know, they only want to buy. I cut the sunshine for the Don, and I cut a bit of myself in with it. Salt.” She touched one white eye, gently, with a gold fingertip. “There’s a grain of my salt in every paper I’ve ever cut. And anybody that’s breathed it has a grain of me in ’em, behind the eyes, and I can sit in my chair and go and look out. Twenty year I’ve cut. I’ve none left at home.” She laughed, thin and light. “A thousand windows and not a door I go out of.”

“And the Crown Prince of Aloria,” said Enzo.

A fold of her face went still that had been moving, as a pond goes still when something large passes underneath.

“I can’t see him. Nor never could. He takes the Treasury’s own. The pure stuff, out of the refining before it’s ever cut. There’s none of me in it. I’ve looked at him out of his own guards, out of the girl on the step when he goes by in his grey. I’ve never once seen what he sees. The Don don’t like a man he can’t watch. That’s why he’s so careful of him. And so am I.”

“Nor at the Sanctuary,” pressed Enzo.

“I saw the Sanctuary,” murmured Nan Corrow.

Enzo did not move.

“I had a pruner in the vines. The vineyards of the Marches buy my cut in the spring, to keep the pruners going through the night, and there was a lad among the vines that night who’d had his paper at supper. I saw a man in grey come up between the rows with a light in his hand like a little sun. I saw the vines go up. And I saw her, widower.” Her voice went lower, and kinder, like a grandmother’s at a bedside. “Your lady. On the cellar stair, at the iron door. I’ll tell you what her face did, if you like. Every bit of it. It’s no use to me.”

Arian looked at his uncle.

Enzo stood by the grey door with his hand on his breast, over the pocket where he now kept a broken chain, and his face had not changed. Only his hand moved, a little, and pressed flat.

“No,” said Enzo.

“It’s no trouble. I’ve kept it.”

“I knew her face for two hundred years.”

Nan Corrow put her gold hand out blindly and found the cat’s head on the board beside her and rested it there.

“You’re the first that ever said no to me. A son out of a husband’s eyes. A wife out of a maid’s. They all say yes.” The cat closed its eyes under her hand, and opened them, and looked at Enzo. “Well. There’s a thing.”

Arian waited. The frost had stopped at the foot of her chair. It went on now, a white inch, up the first of the shawls.

“Now why should I tell you where, little Conti?”

“Because the Don has sent his cousin to tidy you,” said Enzo, from the door.

The mulberry lord did not deny it. He only looked down at the old woman, pleasantly, as a man looks at a dog he is fond of and has been told to drown.

She could not see him out of any eyes in that room. He did not breathe enough. But she turned her white eyes up towards where his voice had last been, and the sleepers along the wall breathed in all at once and did not breathe out.

“Has he, cousin?” she asked.

“The Don sends his regards, Nan,” said the lord.

“Does he?” She sat back in her shawls. “Does he now? Twenty year.” She laughed, low in her throat, and there was nothing pleasant in it any longer. “Then I’ll give the little Conti his cork for nothing, for the Don’s regards. And for the bright one. Listen, my duck. The Don don’t keep ’em on the Coast. He keeps ’em under the Holds, in Durnhollow, where the dwarves keep everything they’re paid never to write down, down under the Seventh, in the —”

Arian had been listening to hearts since they came in. He had listened to the slow single beat of the hill and the cat’s quick one and the old woman’s, which went once in the time the cat’s went twenty, and under all of them, a long way up the mountain, the prince’s, missing, and his own missing with it.

There was no heart in the lord of the inland vineyards to hear.

He moved as Arian’s own kind move, without warning, faster than a lamp flame can lean. His white hand closed on the old woman’s throat above the shawls and turned it, as a man turns the stem of a glass to see the wine go round, and went on turning after it ought to have stopped. Something gave in her neck with a crack like a knuckle, and then something else gave, wetter, and her head came round on her shoulder further than it had ever turned in all her long life, so that her white eyes looked back over it at the dark window behind her chair.

Then he let go, and her head went down on her breast.

The blood came up out of her mouth over her few teeth, dark and slow, with the gold in it, and ran down into the nets of her shawls. Her gold hands lifted off the arms of the chair, both together, a little way, as if she meant to catch something that had been thrown to her, and fell back. Out of the corner of one white eye a single drop came, and went down into the folds of her cheek, and stopped there.

She had told them she had none left.

Along the wall, the nine sleepers shut their eyes. They shut them all together, as the shutters go down along a market street at dusk, and their heads went down, and they slept, truly, with their mouths open, and one of them began to snore. The gold on the floorboards went dull. The cat blinked for the first time and was only a cat, ragged and indignant, and it jumped down off the board and went under the bench and would not come out.

And outside, all down the hill, the single slow heart that had been beating in every doorway stopped, and there were a hundred hearts instead, each in its own time, quick and uneven, as hearts beat in sleepers who wake in a cold doorway in the ash with no notion how they came there. Somebody in the street began to cry.

Enzo had the lord against the wall.

Arian had not seen him cross the room. Nobody alive had seen the Consigliere cross a room at that pace in two hundred years. The lord stood pinned against the shelves with his lantern rolling on the floor and Enzo’s long white hand flat on his breast, over the open nightbloom, and the jars rocked behind him and a little gold sifted down out of them and settled in his hair.

Enzo’s face was as composed as it had been at the door. His hand did not close.

“Any of the Nine may hold another to answer before the Council. It needs no seal. Nobody has wanted to in eleven hundred years.” The hand pressed, and the white petals crushed under it, and their scent came up into the warm gold air, cold and sweet. “I want to.”

Something came into the lord’s smooth face that was not patience.

“The Consigliere has always been a lawyer.”

“The Consigliere has always been a great many things,” said Enzo.

Arian looked at the old woman in her chair.

Her head hung over on her breast at its wrong angle, and the blood had stopped coming. Her white eyes were still open. There was nobody behind them now, and nobody behind any of the eyes she had looked out of, on that hill or in the vines of the Marches or on a step across from a foundry door in the Underglow. Every window she had had in the world had gone dark at once, and somewhere in Durnhollow, under the Seventh, in a mountain of a thousand galleries and as many doors as there were dwarves to pay, a cork lay in its bottle, and Arian did not know where.

He put out his hand and closed her eyes, with his thumb, as the women of his house close the eyes of the dead, and the lids came down over the white without any trouble at all, as if they had only been waiting for somebody to do it.

The prince did not wake that night, nor all the next day, and the gold of the garden went on falling on him without end.

Arian stood at the threshold. He had a chair brought, and did not sit in it. The prince’s heart was missing now more than six in a hundred, and his own with it, and Vesna had sent up to him four times as she had been told and once more besides, because she wished to. Walker sat on the water-stair with a cup in his hands and did not drink from it. The light came out of the prince’s wrists in the water every hour or so, less brightly than it had, and that was worse, and they all knew it.

The lord of the inland vineyards sat in the dark of the cellar under the great hall, among the casks of blood-wine, with the old vent above his head carrying down to him whatever the Nine said of him in the room above, which was a great deal. Enzo had seen to that himself. He thought it would be instructive.

At the eleventh bell of the second night, the fae came.

Arian heard the coin before he heard her step. It had sung in every room Oriane de Chardon had ever walked into, bright and copper and pleased with itself, and it was not singing now. He heard it not sing all the way down the long stair, until she stood at the edge of the light.

Her people walked where they liked, in any light, and she came up to the very sill in her coat of feathers, some paces along it from him, and every feather had gone a sober bronze from collar to hem. She looked past him at what lay among the lilies, and her face, which had never in his hearing been told that it was not young, looked every day of its age.

“I did not stop to print. I have printed every Council of this Coast in Lutèce within two days of its sitting for seventy years, Sovereign, and never once missed.” She drew a letter out of her coat. “I was given this at the posting house at Chalmont yesterday at noon, by a courier of my own people, with my fee in a purse and the purse in a glove. She did not know who had given it to her. I did not know her. My instructions were to put it into the Sovereign’s hand, and to read it aloud to him if he would not take it.”

He took it.

It was a single sheet of plain white paper that might have come from any stationer’s in the Dominion, folded in four and sealed in grey wax. There was no device on the seal, only the faint mark of a thumb that had pressed it down and lifted and been careful to leave nothing behind. He broke it.

The hand inside was neat and square and upright, a clerk’s hand, a hand that had been taught to look like no hand at all, and there were five lines.

*The Ashward keeps its cure for the nightbloom in the Eighth Chamber of Dornhöhle, beneath the Seventh. It lies under the iron with the others, in a phial of white glass stoppered in lead, under the Caruso mark. It will not be given. It must be taken. In the light he has nine nights, or ten.*

There was no name at the foot.

Arian read it again, and held it up to the dark of the corridor behind him, where his uncle could read it as easily at six paces as at one.

“There is no Eighth Chamber,” said Enzo, after a while.

“No,” said Oriane. “There is not. I have never in seventy years heard a dwarf say so without looking at the floor.”

“Who?” said Arian.

“Who wrote it, I do not know.”

The coin in her pocket stayed silent, and Arian believed her. The bronze in her feathers did not stir.

“Who sent it,” she said, “I decline to tell you, Sovereign, as is my right. I know where the glove came from, which is not the same thing as knowing whose hand was in it, and I cannot say a thing I might be wrong about.” She turned her green eyes up to him. “And I do not think, tonight, that it matters to you in the least.”

It did not. Whoever it was knew what lay under the iron of the Holds and wished the prince to live, and Arian would think about who on another night.

Arian bowed to her. The Sovereign of the Coast did not bow to brokers, and the bronze in her feathers stirred, all down her coat.

“You may tell him,” said Oriane, “that I did not stop to print. He will understand what it cost.”

She went back up the corridor with her coin silent in her pocket, and the dark took her before she reached the stair.

“Durnhollow is five nights by the road,” said Enzo.

“I will not take the road.”

“There is no other.” His uncle stood six paces back in the corridor, and kept his voice as level as he had kept it at every Council for two hundred years, and nearly managed it. “Send the sergeant. Send forty. If you go, the fortress is open.”

The rest he left unsaid, because they both knew it. A Sovereign without a ring had better be in his own hall, in his own chair, with the Nine sitting on a contested decree and a lord of the Coast in his cellar and the Ashward on the hill.

“Then hold it.”

“With what?”

“The Consigliere has always been a great many things.”

Enzo said nothing.

“The Holds forgive anything once. And only of one.”

Master Hild had said so on the Undergate, and he had never yet given them occasion. If somebody was to walk into a chamber of Dornhöhle that did not exist and take what lay in it from under the Caruso mark, Arian would rather it were he they forgave. He looked down into the garden. He could not stand at this door for nine nights and listen.

His uncle looked at him a long while in the dark.

“Your aunt,” said Enzo, “would have gone already.”

“Yes.”

Something crossed his uncle’s face and did not stay. “I will hold it.”

At the bottom of the garden, in the gold of the lilies, the prince turned his head.

Vesna bent over him, and straightened, and looked up the whole height of the garden to the doorway. “He’s awake, Sovereign,” she said, not loudly, because she knew as well as the prince did what he could hear. “He’s asking what the bell is.”

“Tell him the eleventh.”

Vesna had no need to pass it on. The prince answered for himself, up the garden, in a voice that was thin and dry and his own.

“The eleventh. Then I have missed supper, and you will have been standing in that doorway since the ninth at least, and the floor of that corridor is stone. I am told you were asked to sit.”

“I was asked.”

“Sit.”

He did not sit. The prince lay with his head turned on the scorched black coat and his face lit gold from above, and his eyes were tired past anything Arian had seen in them, even in the east wing in the last week of the Blight.

“The shadows are leaning up the corridor. They only do that when you are going somewhere.”

“I am going to Durnhollow.”

“Without me. The Usher will be distraught. He will not know where to stand you.” The prince’s hand moved in the water, and a little light came out of it there, and went down. “What is in Durnhollow?”

“Something that is owed you.”

The prince was quiet. Arian listened to his heart go on for eleven beats, and miss, and catch.

“You are not going to tell me what.”

“No.”

“Will you come back?”

“Yes,” said Arian.

He had a great deal else to say. He had twenty years of it, and had never spent a word, and he stood on the sill with all of it behind his teeth, and looked down the garden at the only one he had ever wished to say it to, lying in the lilies with a poison opening in him a petal at a time, and kept it there. It was not a thing to say to a man who might take it for a farewell.

“Will you do a thing for me?” he said instead.

“If it can be done lying down.”

“Count.”

“Count what?”

“Your heart. As the Vales teach. Every night, to three hundred.”

Arian saw the prince’s face do what it had done in a cave when he understood that Arian had read the primer, and saw him decide, as he had then, to let it go.

“It is a remedy for sleeplessness,” protested the prince. “I am in no danger of that. I have slept for a day and a night, I am told. Vesna has been counting it for me, in Wildmarch, which I think is cheating.”

Arian said nothing.

“Why?”

*Because it is not only yours.*

“Because I asked you to,” said Arian.

The prince looked at him up the long gold throat of the garden, a long while.

“You very seldom ask. Your uncle told me so. He said he could count the times on one hand.” His mouth moved, as it moved when he was going to be impossible. “I believe that makes two hands, now. He will have to borrow one of mine.”

“Caleb.”

Every flower on the lowest terrace turned its face up to the door.

The golden lilies did it first, all down the long bed, and then the roses on the terrace walls above them, white heads turning one after another up the spiral of the garden as if a wind had gone up it that touched nothing else, and the lemon blossom, and the starflowers in their urns, until every flower in the garden stood with its face turned up to the threshold where the Sovereign stood, and did not turn back.

The prince lay among the lilies with their faces all turned away from him to the door, and laughed. It was hardly a laugh. It hurt him, and Arian heard it hurt him, and he laughed anyway.

“That is new.”

“Yes.”

“I shall count,” promised Caleb. “To three hundred. Every night. Go on, then, if you are going. Go quickly. I should like you back before I am out of numbers.”

Arian took his hand from the doorframe.

The frost had grown thick on the iron under it while he stood there, white and deep, the whole print of a hand. He left it there. He went back along the corridor towards the giants’ stair, and the shadows of the doorway went with him as they always went, off the frame and out of the corners and along the black floor at his heels, all of them.

All but one.

It stayed on the iron sill at the edge of the light, where he had sat on so many nights, long and dark and quiet, lying out across the threshold towards the lilies as far as the light would let it go, and it did not follow him.

Under his ribs his heart went down the stair with him, in the dark, beat for beat, missing when the other missed, catching when it caught, keeping time.
