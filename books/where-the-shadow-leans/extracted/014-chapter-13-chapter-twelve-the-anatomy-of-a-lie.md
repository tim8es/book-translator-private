## Chapter 13: Chapter Twelve - The Anatomy of a Lie

*Before the hostage passes, his house may require of the wronged an inquiry into the blood, to be held within the season before witnesses of the Holds, and the hostage shall not pass until it is done. Should his house require none, the debt shall stand as it has been named.*

— The Twilight Accord, Article Nine, the clause following (translated from the Lyssois)

\* \* \*

They came up the mountain at the hour the Coast called evening, which was the same violet as the hour it called morning, and the fortress saw them a long way off.

The road wound up out of the harbour in loops, back and forth across the dark face of the mountain like a lace drawn through the eyelets of a boot, and at every turn of it the ash cloud came a little lower and the valleys fell a little further away beneath the carriage window. Caleb watched them go with his forehead against the smoked glass. He found that he knew them. There was the village with the three bridges, hung on its ravine side with every lamp lit, like a string of beads somebody had dropped and not troubled to pick up. Further up, the red river ran so near beneath the road in its gorge that the carriage floor grew warm through his boots, and a turn below the gate the mountain let out its breath through a crack in a long sigh of steam that smelled of struck flint and bad eggs, and the lycans on the roof cursed it in the Wildmarch tongue as they passed, all together, by long tradition and with great affection, as one curses an old dog that has been sick on the same rug for years. Where the road crossed back over the gorge on an iron span, a row of gibbet cages hung from its rail above the red light, rusted open, with nothing in them now but ash, and the same lycans went by under them without a word.

He had looked down on all of it once through a porthole with ash in his mouth, black stone pricked with violet fire, and thought he had come to the bottom of the world. He looked down on it now and thought, before he could stop himself, that the bridges wanted mending.

Above the last turn the mountain opened its black gate, and Castel Nerezza had lit every lamp it owned.

The courtyard was the one the yacht had come down into on the first night, cut deep into the mountain’s side, with the walls going up sheer and dark on three sides and the violet lamps hanging from their iron arms. On that night it had been empty but for a sentinel and a silence. Tonight it was full. The household had come out of every door of the fortress and stood along the walls and on the steps and in the arches, lycan grooms and housemaids with their sleeves still turned back, clerks in black with pens tucked behind their ears, the vampire physician with the voice like a hinge, a pair of fae couriers perched on the rim of the well with their feathers fluffed against the cold. A fine ash was falling, as it always fell on the Coast when the wind came off the summit, soft and grey as the down from a burned letter, and it lay on their shoulders and in their hair and nobody brushed it off.

The carriages came in under the arch one after another, the violet light of their engines running across the black stone, and stopped. The escort came down off the roofs and the running-boards carrying their hurt between them, their bandages gone brown and stiff on the long road home, and the lycan with the cut scalp still wore the blood of the Fault road dried black down one side of his face, like half a mask. A housemaid by the well went along the line of them with her lips moving, counting, and counted to the end, and sat down on the well’s rim with her apron over her face.

Caleb stepped down after them, and the nearest of the violet lamps leaned in their iron arms towards him and went, at the hearts of their flames, a little gold. And he understood, from the way the yard turned, that the story had come up the mountain ahead of them.

It had come, he supposed, with the rider from the rim, or with the night ferry’s crew, or on the ash itself. He saw it go round the courtyard in the faces under the violet lamps, the way a draught goes round a room and sets every candle leaning. The lycans by the stable door put their fists to their hearts. That was not new. They had done it since the morning after the betrothal. But tonight the housemaids did it too, and a clerk did it with his pen still in his hand, and an old lycan groom with no teeth at all took the cap off his head and held it to his chest and said something to the woman beside him in the Wildmarch tongue, and she nodded, and Caleb caught one word of it, which was the word for *walked*.

He had no notion what to do with his face. He did his best with it, and went to help the sergeant with the rug.

Vesna came through the crowd as a laden barge comes through a flock of ducks.

She had no eyes for Caleb, nor for the Sovereign. She had seen Paul come round the end of the second carriage with Mirek in his arms, and she came straight across the courtyard at him with her skirts bunched in her hands and her face gone as pale as her own flour, and stopped before them in the violet light, and looked at her son. She looked at the leg a long while, at the hazel rods and the lace of the Solar House that bound them, gone grey with three days of road and a sheep cave.

“Mirek Tomasovich.”

“Mama.” Mirek put his chin up from Paul’s shoulder. “The Paladin said it was a good splint.”

“What Paladin?”

“Of the Radiant Guard. The High one. She tried the knee herself.” He swallowed. “And she knew what the ninth blade was for.”

Vesna put out her hand and touched the lace at his knee with one finger, as one touches the lace on a bride’s veil laid away in a chest.

“Whose lace is this?”

“His Highness’s, Mama.”

Then Vesna turned her head and looked at Caleb.

He had braced himself for a scolding, and for tears, and had nothing ready for being looked at in that way, as if he had been weighed on her kitchen scales and come out heavier than she expected. She came to him. She put her two hands up and took his face between them, which she had never once done, and her hands smelled of woodsmoke and onions and the bread she had left rising somewhere to come out here, and they were warm from the kitchen fire, and he leaned into them before he knew he had.

Then her thumbs moved, and her face changed. She took her hands from his face and caught up his hands instead, both of them, and turned them palm upward in her own, and held them, and he watched her feel what Matilda had felt in a cave in the Fault.

“Cold.”

“A little.”

“Cold as a well bucket.” She looked up at him with her mouth gone thin. “His Highness has been spending himself again.”

“His Highness had very good reasons.”

“His Highness always has very good reasons. His Highness had good reasons in the yard, and good reasons in the black gallery, and he lay in that garden for a week afterwards like a sack of flour with good reasons.” She closed his hands together between her own and held them there, hard, as if she could put the heat back into them by force, and for all he knew she could. “Garden. Tonight. Before anything.”

“I was going.”

“You were standing in a yard being looked at, *malyshka*.” Her voice cracked on the last word and she mended it by scowling. “Go and be looked at by your lemons.”

Behind her, the Sovereign of the Obsidian Coast had come to a halt some paces off from Paul and the boy, with ash settling among the frost on his shoulders.

“Vesna,” said Arian.

She let go of Caleb and turned and gave the Sovereign a curtsey so short it was hardly worth the bending of the knee.

“Sovereign.”

“Your son had the prince heal me first. He is to have double wages.”

Vesna looked at her son, lying in Paul’s arms with his chin up and his ears going red, and then back at her Sovereign.

“He is to have his ears boxed first, Sovereign. And then we will talk about wages.”

“The wages stand.”

“*Prächtig*,” said Mirek, faintly, to nobody, and Paul carried him away towards the kitchens with his mother walking beside him with her hand on his good ankle, and the courtyard, which had held its breath, let it go in a sound that was nearly a laugh.

\* \* \*

Enzo was waiting on the steps.

He had come down no further than the third. He stood there in his dove grey, hands folded, as still as the black stone of the step, with the violet of the lamps lying along his sleeves and the ash on his shoulders like everybody else’s, and his face was perfectly composed. He had plainly been standing there some time, and did not wish it known. Caleb saw his eyes go to Arian before they went anywhere else. They went to the face, and to the cheekbones, which had been burned on the Fault road and mended in a carriage and were white again now and showed nothing, and then down, to the right hand hanging at the Sovereign’s side.

They stayed on the hand. On its third finger there was nothing.

Then Enzo came down the last three steps, and bowed.

“Sovereign.”

“Consigliere.”

Enzo straightened, and turned, and bowed to Caleb a little lower than he had ever bowed to him before.

“Your Highness is unharmed. I read that first.”

“Then you will have read the rest,” answered Caleb.

“So have the Nine.” Enzo fell into step as they went up, on Arian’s left and two paces back, where Caleb had seen him walk in every gallery of the fortress. He kept his voice where it was. The noise of the yard was enough.

Arian did not break stride. “Go on.”

“I read your order to them the morning it came, as the law requires.” Enzo’s face did not change. “The lord of the inland vineyards asked to see the device.”

Caleb saw the mulberry coat in his mind’s eye, and the ancient voice that went with it, which had once made the same point from the second bell until the secretaries quietly stopped writing it down.

He knew the rest without hearing it. Maartje had told him in Durnhollow what a seal was worth with no ring behind it. Whatever else Arian had sealed into that letter in the cave beneath the word that Caleb was unharmed, any one of the Nine could contest it, and the lord had contested it in the gentlest way there was. He had not asked for a Leaning. He had asked only to see a ring, and there had been no ring to show him.

“It lies upon the table of the Nine,” Enzo went on. “The harbour quarter has been open these two days. A good many have left it.”

“Write down who.”

“I have, Sovereign.”

They had reached the top of the steps. Arian stopped there, under the great black lintel, and looked out over the courtyard, the lamps and the falling ash and the carriages ticking as their engines cooled, and Caleb saw his right thumb find the bare root of the third finger and give it a quarter turn, at nothing.

Enzo saw it too. He said nothing at all.

“And Salvatore?” asked Arian.

“Silent, and gracious. He has sent a basket of blood oranges to the physicians’ hall for the wounded of the escort.” His mouth moved by a hair. “The physicians have not eaten them.”

“Tonight. Your rooms. Bring everything.”

Enzo glanced at Caleb, and something in his face was careful in a way Caleb had not seen before. “Not all of it is about the Carusos, Sovereign.”

Caleb looked back down into the courtyard, and let the look go by him.

The lycans were handing down the boxes from the roof of the third carriage, and the writing case, and Caleb’s own trunk, which had been to Dornhöhle and back without his once opening it. And beside the near wheel, his back to all of it and the light of the lamps falling past him, Paul stood by himself, head bent, his two big hands cupped before his mouth.

Caleb had seen him do it under a lamp in Dornhöhle, on the road, and on the step of a carriage in the Fault at dusk. Paul had prayed so every night since, as far as Caleb could tell, and Caleb did not like to think what about.

Arian had followed his look.

“Does he pray often,” he asked, “your Walker?”

“Every night, lately. They do it in the Highland March, into their hands, so that the wind cannot carry the words away before they reach the moon.” Caleb watched the bowed head, the broad shoulders, the ash settling on them. “His mother taught him, I expect.”

Arian looked at Paul for a while.

“There is no wind in this courtyard.”

The ash came down between them and the third carriage, straight and slow, every flake of it falling plumb, as snow falls in a valley on a still night.

“Then it is habit.”

“Yes,” said Arian, and said nothing more. But his head stayed turned a little towards the carriage, as it had turned in the lambing pens, and he did not look away from Paul until Paul had lowered his hands and wiped them on his coat and taken up the nearest box and carried it in at the kitchen door.

Then he turned to the sergeant, who waited below the steps with the grey velvet bundle held well away from him.

“Sergeant. That goes to the garden.”

“Sovereign.”

“I will take it,” offered Caleb.

Arian looked at him.

“It is the one room in this fortress where nobody of your blood can follow it. And it is my garden.” He held out his arms. “And they are my sister’s.”

The sergeant looked at his Sovereign, and his Sovereign nodded, and the sergeant put the rug into Caleb’s arms as carefully as a midwife hands over a child.

It was heavier than it looked. It was warm through the velvet, as a loaf is warm through its cloth, and the gold leaked through the pile in threads and lay along the inside of his arms, and his own light rose to meet it all down his wrists, as cream rises in a pan left on a cool sill. He carried it in through the great door and down the long black galleries, where the clerks stepped back against the walls and the lamps leaned after him as he passed. He carried it down the giants’ stair, where the air grew warm and smelled of the mountain’s heart, to the iron doors at the end of the corridor, and set his ring to the hollow, and watched the runes wake gold from the centre out.

The garden took him in.

It had missed him, and did not trouble to hide it. The white roses along the first terrace turned their heads after him as he came down the iron stairs, every one, with the faintly injured air of great-aunts who have been kept waiting at a window, and the lemon leaves lifted and showed him their pale undersides, and the water at the foot of the water-stair, which had run all this while for nobody, broke into a little run of notes like a laugh let slip at a coronation. Caleb laughed back at it. He could not help it, with the blades in his arms and the ash still in his hair.

He went down into the light with his sister’s blades in his arms, and laid them under the nearest lemon tree, on the black earth among the fallen leaves, and knelt there beside them with his palms turned up on his knees. Far overhead the twelve crystals swung slowly in their spiral. The light came down the spiral of the terraces and over the white roses and onto him, warm, unhurried, and found his palms, and went into them.

It did not sting this time. It went in as rain goes into a dry pot in a window box, all at once and without asking, and he felt the bottom of each palm warm, and then the sides, and then the whole of the hollow fill up to the brim and stand there, gold, and stay.

He looked at the rug.

The crystals’ light and the light seeping through the velvet were the same light, and on the black earth under the lemon tree they could not be told apart. A rose had dropped its petals on the end of the bundle. Somewhere below, out of sight, the water was running over its lip. It looked, to Caleb, as if the blades had been made for this place, and he thought of his sister bent over a bench in her foundry, humming a little off the note, as she always did when a thing was going well, making them, and believing every hour of it that she was making a door for him to come home through.

“Her name is not to be written anywhere,” he murmured, to the lemon tree.

The lemon tree, which had heard a great deal from him over the weeks, made no answer.

He sat until his hands were full and the warmth had gone up into his wrists. Then he got up, and brushed the earth from his knees, and went to find the people who would want to write it.

\* \* \*

The Consigliere kept his office as a squirrel keeps a tree.

It lay in the west of the fortress, two galleries along from the lamp room, up a stair so narrow that Caleb’s shoulders brushed the rock on either side, and it was taller than it was wide, like the inside of a clock case. Every wall of it was pigeonholes. They went up from the floor into the dark of the vault further than the lamp could follow, hundreds upon hundreds of them, and every one was stuffed with black paper tied in grey tape, so that the whole room was lined with the ends of rolled documents like the ends of logs stacked for a winter that had never come. A ladder of black oak ran round the walls on an iron rail. On the one stretch of wall that had no pigeonholes, the whole Obsidian Coast had been painted in silver on the black stone, every ravine and harbour and village, so that it glimmered in the lamplight, with pins of garnet glass stuck into it here and there like drops of blood-wine on a tablecloth. Tucked into the frame of it, at one corner, was a sprig of dried vine. Its leaves had gone thin and brown as old paper, and curled, and nobody had dusted them.

There was one lamp. It stood on the corner of the desk and burned gold, an Alorian lamp with an Alorian wick and a chimney of clear glass, and its warm light lay across the black leather of the desk and up the grey tapes of the nearest pigeonholes, and stopped a yard into the dark, as if it had been told how far it might go. Caleb understood when he saw it that it had been lit for him, since neither of the other two people in the room had the least use for it.

Enzo had Eleanor’s papers already. He had them laid out across the desk in rows, sheet beside sheet, the way a patient player lays out the cards for a long game of patience on a winter night, and he was reading them standing, with his hands behind his back, bent a little from the waist. Arian sat in a hard chair against the pigeonholes with one long leg crossed over the other, as if chairs were a concession he made to other people’s comfort. He was rimed with frost at the shoulders, and the black silk of his coat drank whatever light came near it. Caleb took the stool by the lamp. It had been set there for him too.

From the stool his eye went to the ladder on its iron rail and stayed there, because he had never yet met a ladder that went about on wheels, and he could think at once of four uses for it, none of them archival.

“No,” said Enzo, without looking up.

“I have not said a word, Consigliere.”

“Your Highness was about to.”

Against the pigeonholes the frost on Arian’s shoulder shifted, as rime shifts along a branch when a bird has come down on it and thought better of the idea, and Caleb could not have sworn before any court that it was not a laugh.

After that, for some time nobody spoke. Enzo read. He read the way Eleanor read, which Caleb had not expected, with one finger running down the columns faster than the eye could follow and halting, every so often, on one figure, as a heron halts in the shallows. The light of the lamp lay along the dove grey of his sleeve and went down the columns with his finger. Once he made a sound under his breath that from anyone else would have been a laugh.

“Your sister keeps beautiful books, Highness.”

“She would be so pleased to hear you say so,” said Caleb, “that she would refuse to believe it.”

Enzo straightened. He looked at the rows of paper a moment longer, and then at his nephew, and then at Caleb, and the carefulness Caleb had seen on the steps came back into his face and settled there.

“The blades, Sovereign.”

Arian did not answer him. He looked at Caleb.

“Cecily,” said Caleb. He had said it to the lemon tree, and it came out no easier for the practice. “My third sister. She is the Grand Alchemist of the Solar Throne. When she was thirteen she cut the runes on Matilda’s first sword, with a chisel she was not allowed, and she put a curl on the tail of every long stroke, so that they would look glad. They are on every hilt in that rug.”

Enzo said nothing for a moment. Then he inclined his head, as a man does who has been handed a coin he expected and is sorry to see, and laid one long white finger on the first of Eleanor’s sheets.

Caleb had read it once already, in the Fault.

*An account with no name, at the Seventh Chamber of Durnhollow. Fed every week from the Underglow, through three houses in Lowmere and a broker in Lutèce. Paid out of it, to Van Houten and Daughters, the carriage of twelve crates from the Citadel wharves to the Kessel Pass. To the couriers of the Chamber, the carriage of the same into the Marches.*

In the margin Eleanor had written a line of square Holdish figures, with a query beside them.

Enzo opened a drawer of the desk and took out a single sheet of thick yellowish paper, folded in four, and laid it beside Eleanor’s, where the lamp turned it the colour of butter.

“Three days ago Van Houten’s factor brought every note of the Coast’s debt to this desk, and counted them onto it twice.” He unfolded the sheet. “This was under the last.”

Caleb leaned to read it. It was printed in a Lowmere hand, round and upright as a row of cheeses.

*Twelve crates, machine parts (refinery), sealed at the consignor’s request and not to be opened in transit. Weight declared. Weight found somewhat greater. No remark entered.*

“Maartje.”

“Van Houten never enters a remark,” said Enzo gravely.

He laid his finger at the foot of the bill, where a line of square Holdish figures stood black under the word *Paid*. Caleb knew how the Seventh Chamber kept its clients, by number and never by name. He looked from Eleanor’s quick black figures in the margin to the Lowmere printer’s round ones and back, and the number did not change between them.

“The Chamber keeps half the money in Aloria,” said Enzo, in the voice of a man making conversation while he turns a page. “I imagine it keeps yours.”

“I have nothing at the Seventh Chamber,” said Caleb.

It was perfectly true. It had been true since the last hammer of a night in Dornhöhle, and it went out of his mouth as smoothly as any true thing, and neither of them looked up. Against his ribs the ring lay cold. He kept his hand on his knee.

Enzo turned up the next sheet without a word.

*From the Treasury, to the household of the Crown Prince, three hundred crowns, for the expenses of the Crown Prince’s journey to the Eclipse Marches, to inspect the positions of the Radiant Guard.* Under it Eleanor had written *He did not come*, and ruled two lines beneath.

The date at its head stood four nights before the crates left the wharves. Caleb knew that night.

“The yacht,” he said slowly. “A Solar yacht that flew no flag of any kind came down at the Kessel Pass that night, and was gone again before dawn. Madame de Chardon put it in her report herself. It was not in her commission. She told me so afterwards, in the garden. Her client had not liked it in the least.”

“Her client,” said Enzo, “has never cared for anyone mentioning the Kessel Pass.”

“It is a station of the Holds. Cold-iron. Inside it nobody can do anything to anybody.”

“And so it is where those who cannot touch hands go to shake them.” Enzo laid both his palms flat on the desk, either side of the yellow bill. “Don Salvatore went up to the Kessel Pass that night. I would stake my seat among the Nine on it.”

Caleb could lay out the rest for himself now, sheet beside sheet, as Eleanor would have. His brother had sat down at a table in the iron across from a Caruso, on a night that cost the Treasury three hundred crowns, and twelve crates of the Grand Alchemist’s work had followed him there and gone on into the Marches with the Chamber’s couriers, and a fortnight later twelve blades with Cecily’s curls on their hilts had been drawn in the Fault by men who called their master the Don, and who had been told, as Caleb had heard for himself on the ledge, not to touch a hair of his head.

The lamp on the desk leaned its gold a little towards Caleb, as Alorian lamps did in his company, and nobody said anything.

In the silence Caleb heard a faint dry ticking, like the sound of a pen nib drawn slowly across a page, and looked for it, and found it in the inkwell on Enzo’s desk. A skin of frost had come across the ink. It was creeping out from the rim of the well towards the middle in fine white ferns, and as he watched, it closed.

He looked at Arian.

Arian was where he had been, in the hard chair with his leg crossed over his knee and his hands at rest, and his face gave away nothing. Only the frost had grown thicker along his shoulders, white on the black silk, as hoar thickens on a hedge in the hour before dawn, and every shadow in the narrow room had come down off the pigeonholes and lay along the floor at his feet, still, and pointing all one way, towards the desk.

“He bought my safety,” said Caleb, “from the Carusos.”

“He bought a clause,” said Arian. He had not spoken in that room until now, and his voice was quite level. “He paid for it in suns.”

“They nearly kept it,” said Caleb.

Caleb looked down at his own hands on his knees. They were warm. He had filled them in the garden not an hour ago, and the warmth lay in them still, steady and gold, and he had no use for it.

“He was at the Sanctuary too.”

He said it to the Consigliere. He made himself say it looking at him, at the face that had gone white across the black hall on the first night while Caleb made jokes about the chandler.

“I lent him my signet in the spring. At supper, in my rooms. He asked, and I gave it to him, and I never once asked it back. I said it aloud two days ago, to my sister, by a river in the Fault, and to nobody since. I did not think I should be able to say it to you.”

Enzo did not answer.

He looked at Caleb a while. Then he opened the narrow drawer at the top of the desk, the one nearest his hand, and took something out, and laid it on the black leather between Eleanor’s papers and the yellow bill.

It was a chain. It was gold, and very fine, the kind a jeweller in the Citadel would sell for a lady’s locket, and it lay on the black leather in the lamplight in a loose bright curl, like a hair fallen from a brush. It had been broken. One end of it was whole, with its clasp still closed. The other ended in a link pulled open and twisted out of true, as if someone had taken hold of it hard and someone else had pulled harder.

“She had this in her hand. The ring was threaded on it.” He looked at the chain. “The ring went to your father. I kept this.”

Across the room, Arian went still. His eyes were on the chain, and Caleb understood that his uncle had never shown it to him. He did not ask, and his uncle did not look round.

Enzo was looking at Caleb. “Highness. Did you ever wear your signet on a chain?”

“I wear my rings on my hands,” said Caleb. His voice had wandered a long way off, and he had to fetch it. He held up his right hand into the light of the lamp, bare, with the gold of the garden still faint along the fingers. “My signet sat on my third finger from the day it was made. It would not go over Andrew’s knuckle.” He found that he was looking at the broken link and could not look anywhere else. “He tried it, at supper. On his little finger. It stopped at the joint, and he laughed, and said that he would keep it safe in his pocket.”

The lamp burned, and its light ran along the broken chain and stood in the twisted link like a drop of honey in a spoon. Somewhere far up in the dark of the pigeonholes a roll of paper settled against its neighbour with a dry whisper.

“Then I know whose hand she held,” said Enzo.

He said it quite gently. He reached out and turned the broken end of the chain over with one fingertip, so that the twisted link lay uppermost in the lamplight, and looked at it. Then he took his finger away.

Caleb found that he had stood up. He did not remember doing it. He was on his feet by the stool with his hands at his sides, and the words he had on his tongue were the ones he had said on his knees on the lamp room’s stone, princely and formal and wholly useless, and he opened his mouth to say them again.

“No,” said Enzo.

Caleb stopped.

“You have said it to her once already, Highness, on your knees.” Enzo put the chain back in its drawer and closed the drawer, and laid his hand flat on the wood of it. “She had very firm views on young men who knelt on stone. Sit down.”

Caleb sat down.

“It was not your hand. I have known that for some weeks.”

Arian had stayed in his chair. But the shadows at his feet had left the floor while Enzo spoke and gone across the narrow room, slowly, without anyone asking them, and Caleb found them lying round the stool and over his boots and on the hem of his coat, cool and heavy and quite still, the way the hounds of a household will come and lie across the feet of whoever is grieving in it, without being told who that is.

The lamp had burned a finger’s width lower. Somewhere far below, a door shut, and a warm draught came up the narrow stair from the deep places and stirred the grey tapes of the pigeonholes, and went away again.

After a while Enzo turned over another of Eleanor’s sheets and laid it face up without a word, which Caleb understood was a mercy. He knew what was on it before he read it, since nothing else could be.

*Issued from the reserve of the Treasury, deep-trench, eight stones, to the works of the Grand Alchemist. By warrant of the Crown Prince.*

Eight stones from the reserve. Cecily had needed deep-trench, and the Coast had been holding it back for months, because twelve of the finest stones in the world were hanging in gold over white roses under a mountain so that her brother should not die. So Andrew had found it for her, out of the Treasury’s own reserve, under his own name, where any clerk might see it and no clerk would dare to ask.

“An old foundry. In the lowest city, where the refineries begin. She took the whole of it when she was made Grand Alchemist and filled it with glass. There is a north window as tall as this room, and the light comes down through all her glass and lies on the floor in colours. She will not let anyone sweep in there in case they move something.”

“Then every line of it comes to one door,” said Enzo.

“She did not know what they were for.”

He had not meant to say it so quickly. It was out of him before Enzo had finished, as a hand comes up in front of a face.

“She did not know, Consigliere. She thought she was making a way to bring me home. Andrew will have told her that I was kept against my will, and that he could not tell her where, and that she must not tell our sisters, and she will have believed every word of it because she has believed every word he has said to her since she was four years old and stood at the edge of the Throne Hall watching him carry me.” He heard his voice climbing, and let it. “She cut glad tails on the runes. She would not have cut them glad if she had known.”

“Highness —”

“Her name is not to be written anywhere.” He looked from Enzo to Arian. “Not in your books. Not in the Council’s. Not in any letter that goes out of this fortress with a seal on it, or without one. The Coast may write that the blades were Solar, and Solar-made, and leave it there. If the Nine ask whose hand, they may ask.”

“The Nine will ask, Highness,” said Enzo quietly.

“Then let the Council ask.”

He heard it as it left him, and knew where it came from. He had heard it said on his twenty-first day at the foot of a black stair, in a cold voice, to a man at a lectern under the last of the violet lamps. Enzo heard it too. His brows lifted a hair’s breadth, and he looked across the room at his nephew.

Arian was looking at Caleb. His eyes had not left Caleb’s face since Cecily’s name, and they did not go to his uncle now.

“It will not be written.”

Enzo opened his mouth.

“There is nothing to seal, Consigliere,” said Arian. “It is a thing not done.” He uncrossed his legs and set both feet on the floor.

Caleb saw it a moment after Enzo did. What was never written could never be read to the Nine, and nobody in mulberry velvet could ask to see the device on a thing that had not been done.

Enzo looked at him a moment longer. Then he gathered up the sheet with the eight stones on it, and folded it once, and laid it under the others, face down.

“Thank you,” murmured Caleb.

“That is not one of mine,” said Arian. “Do not count it.”

Something was still wrong. Caleb could feel it leaning in him, all one way, as the hidden suns in the rock had leaned in him on the Fault road before he knew what they were. He sat on the stool with the shadows across his boots and looked at the face-down sheet and made himself follow it.

“How did they know?” he asked.

Enzo looked up.

“The Carusos,” said Caleb. “Don Salvatore did not fly to the Kessel Pass to haggle for twelve crates of something he had never heard of. He knew what to ask for. And Andrew would not have told him.” He was quite sure of it, and he heard how sure he was, and did not like it. “Andrew keeps things. He tells a man what that man needs to do as he is asked, and not one word more. He would have kept Cecily out of it if the whole Citadel were burning round his ears. So they had her name already.”

Enzo said nothing for a moment. He looked down at the rows of Eleanor’s figures, and then up at the painted Coast on the wall, silver on black, and at the garnet pins in it, and Caleb watched his look settle on one of them, low on the coast, at the mouth of the long harbour below the mountain.

“I can tell you how far I have followed it.”

He came round the desk, and stood before the map.

“Your sister writes that this account is fed from the Underglow.” He did not turn round. “What do your refineries make, Highness?”

“Stone. For the lamps. The Treasury sells it, and enters it in its books, and Eleanor would have —”

He stopped. The refineries made one other thing. It came off the stone in the grinding and lay on everything down there like a gold frost, and got into the mouth, and nobody had ever entered it in any book. He had learned its name at fifteen from a stable boy in the Harp Vales, who had said it with a grin and been beaten for it.

“Sunshine,” he breathed.

“Our harbour calls it the same.” Enzo touched the garnet pin at the mouth of the harbour, red in its silver bay, lightly, as one touches a sore tooth with the tongue. “It comes south in Lowmere holds, under the Citadel’s glass, and it is landed here, in the Caruso quarter. The Ashward takes its share of everything landed there, and has this cut.”

Caleb had never seen that harbour, and he saw it now, sailors and lycans along a black quay buying an hour of noon and a little gold in the skin, in a place where the sun had never once come through the ash, and sitting down after on the cold stone with their eyes open to wait for it to come round again.

“What the quay does not buy goes north again,” said Enzo, “and is sold back into your Underglow at half your brother’s price.”

“My brother,” echoed Caleb.

On the desk the lamp’s flame drew down a little into its wick, as a candle shrinks when a door opens on a cold passage, and he looked at it without knowing why. Enzo did not turn from the map.

Caleb sat on his stool and thought of the Underglow.

He had walked the lamp grid at his father’s side every year of his boyhood, and only once had they gone all the way down, when he was eleven, to look at a new pylon on the lowest terrace. His father had kept a hand on his shoulder there and back and walked faster than he usually walked. Caleb had gone down once more at nineteen, alone, in a hooded cloak, because nobody would take him. He remembered the yellow half-noon of the second-yield lamps, and the thud and sigh of the refineries coming up through his boot soles, and the glitter on everything, the cobbles and the doorsteps and the children’s hair, gold as pollen and sweet on the tongue. He remembered the people in the doorways.

They sat there in rows on the steps of the tanneries and the boarding houses, their backs to the doorframes and their faces tipped up at nothing, with their eyes wide open, smiling. There had been one, a girl no older than Mirek, with the dust gone bright about her nostrils, who had sat on a step with her eyes open and watched him go by, all the length of the street, without once turning her head. He had thought, at nineteen, that she must be looking at something. At the change of the shift a handcart had come down the street behind him with two men in leather aprons, and stopped at a step where a man sat with his eyes open like all the rest and the dust gone dark and wet about his mouth, and they had lifted him onto it by the shoulders and the knees, and he had come up off the step all of a piece, still sitting, as a chair is lifted, and nobody in any doorway had turned a head to watch the cart go.

His father had walked him past them quickly.

Enzo turned from the map, and the silver harbour glinted behind his shoulder like a fish turning in black water. He took a half-sheet from under the yellow bill and laid it by the lamp. It was written in silver, in a square, hurried hand Caleb did not know, and headed only *On the Consigliere’s word. No warrant.*

*Followed the dust from the quay to the cutting, from the cutting to a street, and from the street to a door. The door is not there. New brick, the mortar still soft.*

*Nobody will put a name to her. The quarter calls her the dust-wife. They say she has cut the sunshine for the Ashward these twenty years, and never goes out of her house, and knows a great deal about what passes in a great many doorways for a woman nobody has seen leave her own.*

Two days’ warning. The quarter had stood open two days by the courtesy of a lord in mulberry velvet, and somebody had used them to lay new brick across a door.

“And the sleepers?” asked Arian.

Caleb looked round at him.

Enzo laid one finger on the last lines of the half-sheet, which Caleb had not reached.

*Sleepers on the steps all through the quarter, gold at the nostril, eyes open. Three on the step before the new brick, sitting with their faces to it. One is a child. They do not go home. No one will talk business in front of one. They walk to the end of the street first.*

Enzo lifted one shoulder, a quarter of an inch. “The quarter has its superstitions. The lycans on the quay will not whistle on the turn of the tide.”

Caleb sat with that.

He thought of the yellow lamps and the glitter, and of his sister’s foundry at the foot of the same city, with a door she never locked, because she never heard anyone knock. He could see no harm in it. It was a superstition of the harbour, like the whistling, and somewhere in a quarter he had never seen there was a woman nobody would name, who did not go out.

The fear stayed all the same, cold at the back of his neck, like a hand that has been laid there and taken away.

“They know her door. Cecily’s. The Ashward know where she is, and what she can make. And Matilda cannot be at the Citadel before tomorrow, at the soonest.”

“Your sister the Paladin,” said Enzo, “rides to stand behind it.”

“Matilda can stand behind a door against my brother. I do not know whether she can stand behind it against the Ashward.” Caleb turned to Arian. “Salvatore has lost twelve blades in the Fault and broken a clause. He will want her, or he will want her silent. And she opens her door to anybody who knocks politely.”

Arian looked at him for a while.

“Consigliere. Have you anyone in the Citadel?”

Enzo did not blink. “A laundress in the Chancellery. A lamp-trimmer on the third terrace. And a man who sells pies from a cart outside the Treasury gate, whose mutton is said to be the best in Aloria, which I am told is not difficult.”

“The pie man. Tonight. He is to watch her door, and the step across from it. If anyone tries to take her, he is to kill them in the street, and leave them where the Ashward will find them.”

Enzo inclined his head, as he might have to an order for candles, and the light of the lamp slid along his dove grey shoulder and away.

Caleb looked at Arian. He looked at the white face and the frost, and at the black silk over the last rib on the left side, where one of Cecily’s blades had gone in and broken, and where Caleb had drawn out the singing sliver with his own light three days and a lifetime ago, like an arrowhead from a bad angle.

“She made the blade that was in you.”

“She made it for her brother,” Arian answered. “I have some sympathy.”

There was nothing to say to that. Caleb found he was on his feet again, and that this time he knew what for.

“I want to write to Matilda. To the Citadel. Tonight, with the pie man’s letter, if it can go with it. She ought to know that it is not only Andrew she is standing in front of.”

Enzo drew a sheet of black paper from a pigeonhole without looking for it, and a pen, and broke the frost on the inkwell with its end, and set them on the corner of the desk by the lamp. Caleb sat and wrote. He wrote in the plainest Alorian he had, the kind Matilda read for orders of the day, without a single joke in it, which cost him more than he would have thought. *The Ashward know her door. They have eyes in the Underglow. Do not leave her alone with anyone. Stand closer. C.* The silver ink went down on the black and stood up off it in the light of the lamp like chalk on a slate.

Then he folded it, and found he had nothing to close it with.

He had not had a seal for nine weeks. His own had melted in a stranger’s fist on the first night. He sat with the folded letter in his hands and his thumb on the edge of it, and did not know why it should be this, of everything tonight, that made his eyes sting.

A hand came over his shoulder and took the letter from him.

Arian had crossed the room without a sound. He laid the letter under the lamp, where the Alorian gold lay across the black paper and up the white of his wrist and made the frost on his knuckles faintly glisten, and pricked his thumb on his own tooth and let a drop fall on the fold. Then he drew the dark down into it, off the pigeonholes and the painted Coast and the vault overhead, and the drop rose and hardened as it had in the cave, black and blind, with Caleb’s own face drawn tiny in its gloss and the lamp’s flame beside it like a seed.

“Neither of us has a device.”

“No. She will know whose it is.”

“She will think you have sent her a threat.”

“She will read it twice,” said Arian, “and then go and stand somewhere.” He straightened, and the frost on his knuckles went dull again as he took his hand out of the light.

Enzo took the letter, and turned the blind black seal to the lamp a moment, and put it inside his coat.

“It goes within the hour. With the fae in the brown feathers. He will be at the Citadel the day after tomorrow.” He went back round his desk and gathered the rest of Eleanor’s sheets into a pile, and squared their edges, and turned the pile over to begin again from the bottom, and stopped.

\* \* \*

The sheet at the bottom of the pile was shorter than the others.

It had been cut from the end of a longer page, as Matilda’s strip had been cut from her report, and it was Eleanor’s hand like the rest, only closer and tighter, as if she had copied it in a hurry by a poor light and wished to get it all onto one slip. Enzo had stopped with it in his fingers. He did not lay it down. He stood bent over the desk with the lamp under his chin, its gold lying on his face from below and the dark of the vault going up above him, and read it once, and then a second time, and on the second reading something went out of his shoulders, as the air goes out of a room when a door is shut on the far side of the house.

“Consigliere,” said Arian.

Enzo did not answer him. He laid the slip on the leather by the lamp, very straight, squared to the edge of the desk, and turned it round with one finger so that it faced Caleb.

*From the household purse, at the second bell of the forty-first night of the spring quarter. To the wardens of the Radiant Guard at the Chalmont bridge, eleven crowns, for the register of crossings of the spring quarter, to be delivered up into His Majesty’s own hand, and the copy kept at the post struck through. The High Paladin is not to be troubled with it.*

Beneath it, in the same hand, *Sealed with the sun and the lily.*

And under that, by itself, *The night before. E.*

Caleb read it by the lamp. He read it as he read everything Eleanor had ever written, from the top, and then the pence, because he had read her pence since he was six. There was nothing in the pence. Eleven crowns, no pence at all. It was the plainest sum on the whole desk.

“The night before what?” he asked.

But he had begun to know. He could feel it coming up in him from somewhere a long way down, cold, as the cold had come up through him in a white street at eight years old, from the feet.

“The forty-first night of your spring quarter,” said Enzo, “was the night our courier gave your father the ring.” He did not look at Caleb. He was looking at the slip. “You were put aboard a yacht the next.”

He turned from the desk. He went to the ladder on its iron rail, and drew it along the wall with a long low rumble of iron wheels, past the painted Coast with its silver ravines and the dried vine, to a place where the pigeonholes were older and their tapes had gone from grey to the brown of cold tea, and climbed it. He climbed as Caleb had never seen him do anything, quickly, without his usual care, and stopped six rungs up and put his hand into a pigeonhole without hunting for it, as a man puts his hand on the place in a book where he left off.

He came down with a roll of black paper tied in faded tape, and the dust of the pigeonhole came off it into the light of the lamp and hung there, gold, like the motes in a shaft of sun through a barn door. He untied it on the desk, and spread it.

There were three sheets in it. The first was the Coast’s demand under the Accord, in a clerk’s Vesperian, with the Consigliere’s seal at its foot, the crescent of House Conti pressed in grey wax. The second was the Solar Throne’s answer to it, on the heavy white of the Citadel, and at its foot, in gold wax browned at the edges with the weeks, was the sunburst of his father’s great seal, which he had seen pressed into a thousand documents and had once, at nine, pressed into a slice of cheese. The third was neither. It was a long narrow sheet of grey fae paper, written close in Lyssois in a hand that went across the page in little rushes and stops, like a bird crossing a lawn.

“I read it the night it came,” said Enzo. He laid one long white finger on the grey sheet and kept it there, as if the sheet might lift and go of its own accord. “I thought it told me nothing at all.”

Caleb took it.

It was the courier in the moth-brown feathers. He knew it before he had read three lines, because of the way the hand rushed and stopped, and because of a fae on a gallery stair on the day of the lamp room, who had seen him come round a corner and walked on backwards some way to keep staring. The Coast would send fae to the Citadel, he supposed, since the white noon would burn any vampire on the steps and a lycan in Conti black would be insulted in every street. And a fae could not lie, which made him a good courier and not much else, because nobody trusted a fae with anything interesting, and nobody looked twice at one sitting in a window.

*The Throne Hall, at the fourth bell of the day. I gave the Coast’s demand into the King’s hand before the court, and the ring with it, on its cushion. The King looked at the ring for the space of eleven breaths. I was told to wait for the Throne’s answer in the gallery outside the King’s study, where I sat in a window with the shutter open a hand’s width and the white noon lying across my knees, and nobody looked at me, as nobody does.*

*At the first bell of the night the Crown Prince came along the gallery and went in to the King without being announced. He was within for the space of a quarter of a bell. When he came out his hands were clasped behind his back. As he passed my window he unclasped them, and there was blood in the palm of the right hand, a little, in four places, as when a man has closed his fist too hard on nothing. His face in the light of the window was very white. He did not see me.*

*Before the second bell a clerk of the household purse went in, and came out again with a paper under seal, and went away down the gallery towards the offices of the Guard, running.*

*At the third bell I was sent for. The study was full of light. The King stood at the window with the light behind him, so that I could not see his face. The Queen sat, in white. The King said, The ring has named him. The Solar Throne requires no inquiry. He will be delivered at the yacht field at the first bell of tomorrow night. The Queen asked me whether her son would be harmed. I told her what the Accord says, that hostages are kept and not harmed. She said that he was to be given something to make him sleep, and that her own chamberlain would see him aboard, and that no one of the Coast was to lay a hand on him in her house. The King said nothing more. I was not offered a chair.*

*Set down by me in the Coast’s lodging in the Citadel on the same night, and true in every word, as I cannot help. Corentin, courier.*

Caleb read it to the end, and again, as Eleanor had taught him to read a column, from the bottom and then from the top and then down the right-hand side for the figures, only there were no figures in it, only the bells, the fourth of the day and the first of the night, the second, the third.

He laid it on the desk beside Eleanor’s slip, with the bells side by side.

*At the first bell of the night the Crown Prince went in.*

*At the second bell of the night, the household purse. The register of the Chalmont bridge, into His Majesty’s own hand.*

*At the third bell. The Solar Throne requires no inquiry.*

“Every house asks for an inquiry,” said Enzo quietly. “It costs nothing, and it buys a season.” He touched the white sheet with the brown sunburst at its foot. “I had never read an answer that did not ask.”

A house that believed its son guilty would ask all the same, Caleb understood, for the season alone, so that he might have one more summer in the light before he went. He remembered a crack in the rock under the Council’s chamber, and Arian’s voice coming down through it, *He did not kill her*, and nobody answering but Don Salvatore. Salvatore had needed nothing more than this sheet, laid on the table. His own father had not troubled to ask.

“My father asks for a season on everything.”

He heard his own voice. It was perfectly steady, the voice he had used across a hundred tables to a hundred envoys while the candles burned down and the soup went cold, and it seemed to belong to somebody else, a little to his left.

“He asked the Lowmere envoy for a season to consider the colour of a carpet. He asked the Harp Vales for a season before he would let me go to them, and then for another. He has never in his life said yes to anything in the same quarter in which it was asked of him.” He looked at the bells, black on the white and the grey and the cream, and the lamp’s light lying over them all alike. “He did not ask for a season for me.”

“An inquiry,” said Arian, “would have asked for the registers.”

Caleb turned his head.

Arian had not left his place by the desk. He stood where he had sealed the letter, with the lamp at his back and his hands at his sides, and where the lamp caught the frost along his shoulders it had gone gold at the edges. He had no eyes for the papers. He was looking at Caleb.

The registers. Caleb saw them as the Coast would have asked for them, every crossing into the Marches in the spring quarter, and whose seal, and on what day, at what hour, and with how many, opened before witnesses of the Holds in the cold-iron, where no light could be bent and no shadow called. His father had known the Accord by heart for longer than Caleb had been alive.

“And he had the register in his own hand since the second bell.”

“Yes.”

Caleb looked at the slip. *The copy kept at the post struck through.* He could see the Chalmont bridge. He had crossed it at sixteen on his way to the Vales, a long stone bridge over a green river in a gorge, with snow on the peaks above it and the Guard’s post at the far end, and the wardens in their white coming out to look at his seal, and an old warden with a pencil behind his ear and a book on a string who wrote down everything, everything, the seal and the hour and the weather and the number in the party and the colour of every horse, because Matilda had been made a captain that year, and Matilda’s wardens wrote down the colour of every horse.

“I do not know what it said. I know what it would have said.” He found that his finger had gone to the slip and was resting on the words *Chalmont bridge*, and he left it there. “*His Royal Highness Prince Caleb, by his signet.* And then the horse, and the hour, and the coat. Andrew rides greys. He has ridden greys since he was fifteen. He likes a horse that shows the dust, so that people can see he has travelled. And he travels in grey, he has since the Vales, he says it is the only colour a man can be seen in on a road without being looked at.” He heard himself, and went on. “I have never once in my life worn grey to travel. I look like a puddle in it. Anybody who had ever stood at a window in the Citadel and watched Andrew ride out would know whose crossing that was.”

“Your father has watched him ride out,” said Enzo.

“Every time.” Caleb took his finger from the slip. “From the window of his study. With his back to the room.”

Enzo turned the Coast’s demand round on the leather so that its first lines lay under the lamp, where the clerk had copied out the Accord in a fair Vesperian hand.

*Where the blood of an Umbral house is shed upon Umbral ground by a hand of the Solar House, the wronged may claim the debt in blood, or in a hostage of equal rank.*

An inquiry would have found the hand that held the ring, and the debt fell on the hand, and on its rank.

“There is no hostage of equal rank to the heir,” said Enzo, “except the heir. Or the King.” He said it gently, the way a physician names a thing to a man who has already guessed it, so that it need not be guessed any longer, and his long white hand lay on the desk beside the King’s sunburst and did not touch it.

“The Crown Prince cannot be given,” said Caleb.

Enzo looked at him.

“They teach it to the second son first. Before letters. Before his own name. The Crown Prince is the Throne’s, and cannot be given to anybody, and the second son is there in case.” He looked down at his hands. They lay in his lap, still faintly gold from the garden, and he could not feel the warmth in them at all. “I was a very good second son. I learned it before anyone had finished teaching it.”

He sat and looked at the four sheets laid side by side on the black leather in the gold of the lamp, the grey and the white and the cream and the Coast’s black, with the bells running across them like a road.

He had thought, all the way across the Coast with the taste of ash in his mouth, and night after night in the east wing under the plum velvet, and on his back among the white roses with the light pouring over him, that his father had not been able to finish the sentence. He had thought it was grief. He had thought that his father, who never came to his rooms, had come to them himself because he could not bear to send a clerk, and had stood at the window with his back to the room because he could not bear to be seen, and had said that the signet was found at a vineyard among the dead and then stopped, because the rest of it, the *were you there* and the *how could you* and the *tell me it is not true*, was more than any father could make himself ask a son. He had forgiven him for it on the yacht. He had forgiven him again every night since, a little more each time, as one darns the same hole in the same stocking, and been quietly proud of how neat the darning was. And now, now that a clerk of the household purse had gone running down a gallery at the second bell with a paper under seal, now that the register of the Chalmont bridge had been carried up into the King’s own hand and the copy at the post struck through, now that a fae who could not lie had sat in a window and watched his brother come out of that study with blood in his palm, he understood why his father had stood at the window and asked him nothing at all.

His father had already been told.

The gold went out of him.

It went from his wrists and his hands and the line of his jaw all at once, the way the tide goes out across a long flat shore, far and fast and leaving everything wet and grey behind it. He felt it go. He did not try to stop it. It went out of the warmth he had carried up from the garden, which had lain in his palms all evening as honey lies in the comb, and out of the faint shine at his collar and at the roots of his hair that had been there in every mirror he had ever looked into, and when it was gone, the hands in his lap were only hands. On the corner of the desk, Enzo’s lamp, which had been leaning towards him all evening, a little, as lamps did, stood up straight in its glass with nothing to lean to, and burned there, tall and thin and gold and useless.

Nobody spoke.

He was aware of Enzo, motionless across the desk. He was aware of Arian beside him, and of the frost. It had come out along the edge of the desk where Arian’s hand rested, white on the black leather, in ferns, and as he watched it crept towards the Throne’s answer, and reached the edge of it, and went over, and lay across his father’s seal in a fine cold rime, until the gold of the sunburst could hardly be seen under it at all.

“And my mother,” said Caleb.

Enzo said nothing.

Caleb put out his hand and turned Eleanor’s slip round on the leather so that it faced him, and laid his finger under the line that said *Sealed with the sun and the lily*.

“She keeps the household purse. She has kept it since before I was born. My father says he would not know a household from a hole in the ground, and he is quite right, and the purse has her seal on every warrant beside his. And she reads them. She reads the laundry book, Consigliere. She corrects the spelling in it. She has never in her life pressed her lily into white wax on a thing she had not read twice and found a fault in.”

“Then she read it,” said Arian.

“Then she read it.” Caleb looked at the courier’s grey sheet, and the line near its end that he had already read twice and would, he knew, read a great many more times in the dark. *She said that he was to be given something to make him sleep.* “And then she sent her chamberlain to me with the wine. So that nobody of the Coast should lay a hand on me in her house.” He laid the slip down. “The chamberlain smiled at me over the glass. I remember thinking he looked tired.”

He straightened his cuffs.

He did it without deciding to, as he had done it on an iron mezzanine with a garden opening beneath him, and in the great hall, and at a hundred tables, when he wished his hands to be doing something princely while the rest of him caught up. The lace at his wrists was not there to straighten. It had gone round a lycan boy’s shin in a sheep cave. He straightened the white silk cuffs of his shirt instead, one and then the other, and laid his hands in his lap, and looked up.

“You said it in the hall,” he said to Arian. “On the first night. When I asked you what in the Sun’s name I was doing here, if you knew I had not been at the vineyard. You said, *They sent you.* As if that were the whole of it.”

“Yes.”

“Did you know? Then?”

“I knew that they had asked for no inquiry.” Arian’s voice was low and level, as it always was, and under it something had been laid down on its side with great care, so as not to break. “I thought they believed it of you.”

Caleb looked at him for a long moment.

“That would have been kinder.”

Arian made no answer. There was none to make, and he did not insult either of them by looking for one. His hand lay where the frost had spread from it, and the frost lay over the brown sunburst and did not melt, and while Caleb watched, his fingers closed slowly on the leather, as a man’s fingers close on the rail of a ship in a sea that has come up suddenly from nowhere.

Under the frost, the King’s gold wax cracked. It went with a sound no louder than a knuckle, from the rim of the sunburst to its heart and then across it, and a flake of the gold lifted on the rime and fell over on the leather, face down.

The shadows went off Caleb’s boots. They rose from every corner of that narrow room together, as hounds come up off a hearth when the horn goes, and gathered along the wall behind the painted Coast, and leaned there, all of them, the same way. They leaned north, through the black rock and the ash and the whole width of the Marches, towards a white city where the sun did not go down, and the cold that came off them stopped the breath in Caleb’s throat. Arian had not moved. He was looking at the broken seal as he had looked at the rogues of the Ashward on the Fault road, a moment before they were not there.

“Please,” whispered Caleb.

He could not have said whether he asked it for his father and mother, or for the man who would have to walk into their noon to reach them.

The shadows came down off the wall. They came back across the floor slowly and unwillingly, and lay down over his boots again, and the cold went out of the room by degrees. Arian’s hand did not open.

Caleb stood, and the shadows slid off his boots onto the black floor.

He did it carefully, because he was not certain of his knees, and they held. He found that he knew what to do next, which was a mercy. One bowed to the Consigliere, a little lower than one bowed to a minister, because the Consigliere had been kind and had lit a gold lamp for him and kept a chain in a drawer. One bowed to the Sovereign. One said something graceful about the hour, and went out, and shut the door, and did not hurry on the stair.

He bowed to Enzo.

“Thank you, Consigliere. You keep beautiful books also.”

Enzo rose from behind his desk. He did not say anything at all. He bowed, and it was not the bow he gave a minister, or the bow he had given Caleb on the steps. It was lower than either, and slower, and he held it a long time, with his hand flat on his breast, over the place where the men of his blood keep no heart, and when he straightened, his face was no older than thirty, and his eyes were the oldest things in the room.

Caleb turned to Arian.

“Caleb,” said Arian.

The lamp did nothing. Caleb noticed that it did nothing. Every lamp on the Fault road had leaned at that name, and tonight the flame on the corner of the Consigliere’s desk stood upright in its chimney and did not stir, because whatever light in the room might have answered had gone out somewhere between the first bell and the third, and there was nothing left in Caleb for the name to find.

He thought, distantly, that he would have liked it to brighten.

“What do you want?” said Arian.

He asked it without any weight on it, as he had asked *why* on a ledge of the Fault, as if he truly wished to know, and would do it, whatever it was.

Caleb looked at him.

“I want to go to bed, and not be anybody’s son until the morning.”

“Then go.”

Arian did not move. His hand stayed shut on the desk’s edge under the frost, and Caleb saw the knuckles of it stand up white against the black leather, and the frost come out thicker along them, grain on grain. He kept to his own side of the desk and the lamp, and the width of it stayed between them.

“I do not sleep a great deal. If you should want anything.”

“I know where your rooms are,” said Caleb.

He did not know why he said it. It was true, and it meant nothing, and he heard it go out of him into the narrow room like a glove let fall on a stair, for somebody to find later.

He went out, and shut the door on the gold lamp and the frost and the two of them, and did not hurry on the stair.

\* \* \*

He went back to the east wing by the long galleries. The violet lamps hung in their iron arms the whole length of them, as they had hung when he carried his sister’s blades down to the garden and every one had leaned after him, and tonight not one of them stirred. A clerk coming the other way with a candle stood back against the wall for the ring on his hand, and bowed to it, and went on. In the black glass of the floor Caleb walked beside himself all the way, a dark shape among the dark shapes of the pillars, no brighter than the clerk.

Paul was asleep across the threshold of the east wing, as he had slept across every threshold of Caleb’s since Caleb was fourteen, and he was not asleep at all.

He was up before Caleb had reached the last lamp of the corridor. He had been lying on his blanket on the black stone with his back to the door and his face to the dark, and he came up off it all at once, the way a sentry comes up off a bench at a step on the stair, and stood with his blanket still caught round one boot, and looked.

“Highness.”

“Go to bed, Paul.”

“This is my bed, Highness.”

Caleb stopped. It had never once crossed his mind, and it crossed it now, standing in the corridor’s violet with his hand on the door, that a man had laid his blanket across a doorway every night for fourteen years so that nothing could come at Caleb without first stepping over him, and that Caleb had never asked him whether the stone was cold.

“Is the stone cold?”

Paul looked at him as if he had asked whether the moon was round.

“It’s stone, Highness.”

“Yes. I suppose it is.”

He put out his hand to the latch. Paul’s eyes went to it, and stayed there, and his mouth worked once, and closed.

“Highness. Your hands.”

Caleb looked down at them. There was no gold in them at all. They lay on the black iron of the latch as plain and brown as a gardener’s, and in the violet of the corridor lamps they looked almost grey, like the hands of somebody who has been a long time in cold water.

“It will come back. It always comes back.”

“Yes, Highness.”

Paul stayed where he was, by his blanket in the dark, and his face in the violet had gone the grey it had gone in the sheep cave, and he looked at Caleb’s cold hands on the latch as if they were something he had dropped and broken and did not know how to tell anybody.

Caleb went in, and shut the door on him gently, as Paul had once shut a door on him in Dornhöhle.

\* \* \*

The candles had been lit for him. Vesna had been, or one of the housemaids, and every candle in the bedchamber was burning, and the violet lamps in their brackets were turned up as high as they would go, and a fire had been made up in the grate that nobody but Caleb had any use for, so that the room was warm and gold and full of light from wall to wall, like a lantern somebody had set in a window to bring a traveller home.

He put it out.

He went round the room and did it himself, one candle at a time. He pinched out the tall ones on the mantel and the short fat ones by the bed and the three in the iron stand by the harpsichord, and the smoke went up from each in a thin grey thread and curled and was gone, and the smell of hot wax came after it. He turned the wall lamps down until their light was no more than a bruise on the black stone. He took the tongs and broke up the fire, and spread it, and let it go down to a red glow in the grate, and then to a dull one. The heavy plum velvet of the hangings swallowed what was left. Passing the window, he saw a man in the smoked glass against the violet and took a moment to know him, because the man in the glass had no light about him anywhere and stood there as flat and plain as a figure cut out of the Coast’s black paper. And when there was nothing left in the room to put out, he went to the bed and lay down on it in his clothes, in his coat, on his back, on top of the coverlet, as he had lain on the first night in this room, and stared up at the dark.

It was the Coast’s dark. Lying in it now, with the last red of the fire breathing on the ceiling, he found it only dark, and very old, and quite indifferent to him, like the far end of a great house at night, and tonight he was glad of it, because nobody could see his face.

He thought about supper.

He thought about the shutters thrown wide and the noon pooling gold in the soup plates, and Andrew across the little table in his rooms, where Andrew had not sat since the year Caleb turned twenty. He had been so pleased that Andrew had come that he talked through three courses without drawing breath. And at the end of it Andrew had asked, in the easy voice he kept for asking nothing much, whether he might borrow the signet, for a wine merchant in the Marches. And Caleb had worked it off his own finger, and when it stuck at Andrew’s knuckle he had eased it back off himself, as he would have eased a ring off a swollen hand in the Vales, and let it fall into Andrew’s palm.

He had always given Andrew things. He had been told so all his life, at every birthday, until he was twelve and begged them to stop. At a year old he had crawled the whole of the Throne Hall before the court, across the white marble, to a brother he had never met, and taken the circlet off his head and given it back, and dropped it, and gone to sleep on his shoulder. He had given him every toy he owned that Andrew ever looked at twice, and his place by the window of the yacht on the long flight to the Vales, and his share of the honeycomb at every feast of First Light from the age of four, and he had given his father the benefit of every doubt he had ever had, and his mother the courtesy of not asking why her chamberlain looked so tired, and the whole of the Solar Throne nine weeks of the most beautiful excuses he could invent, in the dark, every night, in the voice the Vales had taught him for telling a patient that the bone would knit. And to his brother, at supper, in the spring, with the noon on the plates, laughing, because it was Andrew, and he had been saying yes to Andrew since before he could say anything else, he had given his name.

His hand had found the ring through his coat, a cold, heavy round against his ribs, and he did not remember putting it there.

“I gave it to you.”

He said it to the dark. He said it aloud and quite quietly, in the plain voice a clerk uses to read a sum back, and the dark took it and gave nothing back.

Then the shadows came.

They slid down the walls and out of the hangings and down from the carved posts of the bed, where the nightshade and the serpents twined, slowly, as they had come to him on a hundred nights in the garden doorway and in the carriage and in a cave, unasked. He was not warm tonight. There was not a grain of gold left in him anywhere for anything to come to. He lay on the coverlet as cold as the iron on his finger, and they came all the same. They lay down over his chest, and over his hand, and over the place under his hand where the ring was, soft and heavy and dark, like a quilt drawn up over a sleeper by a hand that means not to wake him, and they did not move away.

He did not tell them to go.

He lay under them and looked up at the place where the ceiling was, and did not sleep. The fire went down from red to grey in the grate behind him. The violet at the windows stayed as it was, because on the Coast it always stayed as it was, and somewhere in the fortress the bells went by unheard, and there would be no sun to come up at the end of it. And on the far side of the door, on the black stone of the corridor, he heard Paul lie down again on his blanket, and turn over, and turn back, and lie still, and not sleep either.
