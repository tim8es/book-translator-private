## Chapter 14: Chapter Thirteen - The Cloak

*The *Lantern* is happy to report that the youngest son of the Solar House, who was taken ill at Old Meridian on the day of the Eclipse, is sitting up, and has asked for cake. The Solar Throne describes his indisposition as a chill in the mist. The *Lantern*, which was also in the mist, caught nothing at all.*

— From *The Lantern of Lutèce*, in the month after the last Long Eclipse

\* \* \*

The fire in the grate went out at some hour that had no name, and Caleb lay under the shadows and let it.

He watched the last coal go. It went from red to the sullen red of a smith’s iron left lying on the anvil while the smith eats his supper, and then a skin of ash crept across it from the rim, and he could not see it any longer. The cold came out of the black walls after it, as the cold comes into a house where nobody has thought to bank the fires, and lay down on the coverlet beside him, under the shadows, and was no colder than he was. A long way below, in its gorge under the walls, the red river was running as it had run beneath the carriage on the road up, near enough that evening to warm the boards through his boots. He lay above it now with half a mountain of black rock between, and could not feel it at all.

At some point he held his hand up before his face.

He had never needed a lamp to find his own hand. In the shuttered nursery of the Citadel, in the east wing behind the plum velvet, at the back of a sheep cave in the Fault where no daylight had come in a thousand years, it had always been there in front of him, faintly gold, the one thing in any dark that he could be sure of. He held it up now as a dwarf in the deep galleries holds up his hand when the lamp in his cap has failed, to learn whether he has gone blind or only gone dark, and there was nothing there to see. He could feel the air move between his fingers. He put the hand down again over the ring in his coat, and the shadows closed back over it like water over a dropped stone, and he lay still.

The physicians of the Vales prescribed for sleeplessness that one lie on one’s back with one’s hands on one’s breastbone and count one’s own heart, as one counted a patient’s, until somewhere past the third hundred the body grew bored of being listened to and went to sleep to escape it. He had prescribed it to novices, and to a weeping ambassador, and once, in a fit of pique, to Eleanor. He tried it now, under his breath, in the even voice of the tutors, *one*, and *two*, and *three*, and got as far as seven.

At seven a boy’s voice said *What are you counting?* in a cold glasshouse above a dead city, and he stopped.

He lay with the number in his mouth. He thought of a cloak of grey wool stitched through with silver that would not meet across a boy’s chest, and of a boy’s hand coming up out of the roses to close on it at the throat. *It was too small*, Arian had said in the cave, under the vein of glass, as if that were the part worth keeping. Caleb had not asked what became of it. There had been a sword at his breastbone since then, and a sister in his arms, and a river, and a road home, and it had not once crossed his mind until this hour, lying in the dark with no light left in him, that a thing he had given away when he was eight years old might still be somewhere in the world, and that he might be allowed to know where.

*I know where your rooms are.*

He had let it fall on the Consigliere’s stair without meaning anything by it. He found, lying there, that he had known all night he would go back and pick it up.

He sat up. The shadows slid off him into his lap and lay there, heavy and unwilling, and when he swung his feet down to the floor they came too, round his ankles, as the last of the bathwater clings to a child lifted out of the bath. He did not light a candle. He went across the room by memory, past the black bulk of the harpsichord, to the table by the window, where the violet of the Coast came in through the smoked glass and lay on the table top as faint as the bloom on a plum. The books he had been sent on his first day were stacked there still, forty-two of them, and on the top of the pile, where it had lain since the evening of his nineteenth day, was the primer.

He picked it up. It was slim and light, bound in cloth that had once been green, and the lower edge of every page was wavy and stiff where water had got into it long ago and dried. He had read it so often in the east wing that it fell open by itself at the Blight. He kept it shut. Of the forty-three, it was the one that nobody would explain, and a voice in a cave had said *I have read the primer* as if that were nothing at all. If he needed a reason at this hour to climb the Sovereign’s stair, he had one in his hand.

Paul was awake.

He was on his feet before the door was fully open, with the blanket still round one ankle.

“Your Highness.”

“Lie down, Paul.”

“I wasn’t asleep.”

“Nor was I.”

Paul looked at the book, and at the dark all round Caleb, where for fourteen years there had always been a little light, and said nothing.

Then he turned and took down the lamp from its hook beside the door, a common lamp of the kind the lycans of the household carried on the night stairs, with a horn shade and a stub of tallow in it, and lit it from the violet lamp in the bracket, and held it out.

“You’ve no light, Highness.”

Caleb looked at the lamp. He had never carried one in his life. Every stair he had ever gone up in the dark he had gone up by his own glow, and Paul had come up a pace behind him on all of them with his hands free, because there had never been any need.

He took it.

“Thank you,” he said, and then, because Paul was still standing in the cold with nothing left to hold, “Go back to bed. On the stone. Since you insist upon it.”

“Yes, Highness.”

He did not lie down. When Caleb looked back from the turn of the corridor, Paul was still in the doorway with the violet lying on him, watching him go, but he did not follow.

\* \* \*

At that hour Castel Nerezza was as violet as it was at every other, and only quieter. The galleries lay empty under their high bruised lamps, and the black glass of the floors gave the lamplight back in long soft streaks, like the lights of a harbour lying on still water, and Caleb’s lamp went along them in a little moving room of yellow a yard wide, and he walked inside it. A lycan housemaid on her knees at the foot of a stair, scrubbing the tracked ash out of the black glass by the light of one candle, with her knuckles split pink from the lye, sat back on her heels to let him by and touched her fist to her breast with the brush still in it. He wished her good night, because he could not help it, though there was no gold left in him to say it with, and she looked after him down the gallery as if somebody had set a warm cup down beside her on the stone. Somewhere far below, the mountain breathed up the stairwells, warm, and drew the breath back down. He passed the long gallery that led west towards the lamp room, where the dead of House Conti burned all night in their bowls of dark glass, and the newest of them burned for a woman he had been brought across the strait to answer for, and he did not look along it.

The shadows went with him. He had supposed they would stay behind in the east wing among the hangings, where they lived, and they had not. They came along the floor at his heels, just past the reach of his lamp, as quiet as ink running along the grain of a page, and when he came at last to the foot of the Sovereign’s stair they went up it ahead of him, all of them, into the dark, as if they knew the way and thought him slow.

He put his hand in the hollow that Conti hands had worn into the banister over a thousand years. The stone was as cold as it had been in the days after the gallery, when he had stood here three times and gone back down. His hand was no warmer than the stone tonight, and fitted the dip just as well.

He went up.

Two lycans kept the head of the stair in Conti black, with their backs to the wall. The younger of them had been in the courtyard when the escort came home, and Caleb watched him take in the lamp, and the book, and the plain brown hand that held them both, and saw the ripple of it go across his face.

“Highness.” The older one straightened. “The Sovereign’s not here. He’s gone down to the lamp room, to sit with the Consigliere.”

Caleb stood on the top step with his lamp, and felt the whole of the night go out of his knees at once.

“Then I shall come back. Another —”

“He said if you came, Highness, you were to go in.”

Caleb looked at him.

“Those were his words,” said the lycan, a little stiffly, as a man repeats an order he has been made to say over once already to be sure he has it. “*If the prince comes, he is to go in.* And the door’s to be left as it is.”

The door stood open. Caleb had not seen it. It stood wide at the end of the short passage behind the two guards, and out of it, along the black floor towards his boots, came a path of gold that trembled at its edges, the light of a room full of candles.

He went in.

Every candle in the study was burning. They burned in their iron stands and their wall sconces and their dishes on the shelves, in clusters along the window ledge and all down the long mantel, scores of them, so that the whole long low room swam in gold from end to end and the air above the flames wavered as the air wavers over a white road at noon. It smelled of hot wax and leather bindings and the smoke of the hearth. The fire in the great stone hearth had been built up high. It roared and cracked and threw its light up the chimney breast, and the heat of it came down the length of the room and met him in the doorway, so that he stopped on the threshold and stood in it, with Paul’s lamp gone dim and homely in his hand.

He set the lamp down on the floor inside the door, where its yellow looked like a child’s drawing of a flame set beside the real ones, and left it there.

The desk had been cleared.

He had only ever seen it heaped with papers. Tonight there was nothing on the black wood but the inkwell with its rime of frost, and a pen laid square beside it, and in the middle, in the full light of the candles, a square of unbleached linen that had been folded round something and opened out again, its four corners lying back on the desk like the petals of a flower brought into a warm room.

On the linen, folded, there was something grey.

He knew it from the door. He knew it before he had taken three steps, as one knows one’s own hand on a letter lying across a room, by nothing one could name. He came up to the desk and stood over it, and did not touch it.

It was a child’s cloak. It had been folded in four as a laundress folds a thing that is to be put away for a long time, with the collar uppermost, and it was of grey wool so fine that it took a sheen like silk wherever the candlelight crossed it, and threaded all over with silver. The thread ran in long lines through the weave from hem to collar, close as the ruled lines on a page, and every line had drunk the candlelight and kept it, so that the cloak lay on the dull linen glittering, as a beck glitters at dusk where it runs shallow over stones. On the collar the sun of his house spread all its rays in gold.

The gold had gone dull. Every sunburst of the Solar House was worked to take in light and give it back, and this one had lain twenty years somewhere with nothing to drink, and it was only yellow thread now, the colour of honey forgotten at the back of a cold larder.

He put out his hand and laid it flat on the wool.

It was soft. It was softer than he remembered, though he did not truly remember it at all, only the weight of it on his shoulders on a slope above a valley. Somebody had washed it. He could feel the washing in the wool, the slack laundered softness no cloak has on the morning it is first put on. At the hem, where a fold lay turned back against the linen, the cloth had been torn once on something sharp, briars, he supposed, and somebody had mended the tear in silver thread to match, with stitches so fine and so close that he had to stoop to the candles to find them, a darn laid into the weave a thread at a time by someone with all the patience in the world, who had not hurried, and had meant it to last.

He turned back the collar.

Inside it, along the silk of the lining at the neck, where the cloak had lain against a boy’s throat and been held there in a fist, there was a stain. It had been washed, and washed again. It had gone in the washing from whatever it had been to a faint brown shadow on the silk, the brown of an oak leaf that has hung on the tree all winter, and no washing in the world had been able to lift it out. Caleb had been trained by the physicians of the Harp Vales. He knew old blood when he saw it. He knew whose.

He had knelt in it. It had soaked up cold through the knees of his breeches with the black earth of the glasshouse, so much of it that the soil among the roses had gone to a red mire, and it had come welling between a boy’s white knuckles from the hole in his side where the sun-forged silver still sang. It had been on that fist, wet to the wrist, when it took hold of the collar and would not let go.

Tied through the ring of the clasp, on a loop of red waxed twine, there was a label.

It was the kind the cellarers of the Coast tie round the neck of a bottle when it is laid down in the dark to wait, a slip of stiff card with a hole punched through it for the twine, and the words on it were in ink that had faded to the brown of an old cask hoop. The hand was quick and slanting and sure of itself, the hand of someone who had written a great many labels by lamplight among the casks and had never once needed to cross out a word.

*Laid down at Old Meridian, on the day of the Long Eclipse.*

*Not ours. To be given back.*

He read it twice, and something stood up in his throat and stayed there. He held the card between two fingers in the warm still air, with the candles burning all round him and no light at all in his own hands.

Tucked into the fold of the cloak, under the collar, where anyone who opened it would find it, there was a letter. It was folded close and pressed flat, and on the outside, in the same quick slanting hand, in ink that had not faded at all, was a single letter. *A.*

He did not take it out. It had not been written to him. He laid the collar down over it again as he had found it, and let the label go, and took his hand away, and stood looking at the cloak until he could not look at it any longer.

Then he turned round, and saw the wall.

He had sat with his back to it on the morning of the *Lantern*, in the chair across the desk, while he argued the terms of his own betrothal, and afterwards he had walked the other walls of the room with his finger along the spines, the cracked calf of the histories and the Coast’s poets all in black, and he had never once turned round. It faced the desk from across the room. Whoever sat at the desk would have it before him every time he lifted his eyes from his papers. It went up from the floor to the low ceiling like the others, shelf over shelf, with a branched iron stand of candles burning before it, and the bindings on it were not black.

The whole of the lowest shelf was grey.

It was the soft dove grey of the boards the binders of Lutèce put on the broadsheets of the fae when a library wishes to keep them, and the spines were lettered in silver by the year, and there were twenty of them. Caleb went down on one knee before them, as he had gone down on the stone of the lamp room before a newly cut name, and drew out the first.

It was *The Lantern of Lutèce*. He had known it would be. It was the year he was eight, and it fell open in his hands near the front, at a page that had been turned to so often the binding had given up arguing, and there at the foot of a column, between an item on the price of harp strings in the Vales and a scandal at the Court of Lys that his mother had forbidden the whole nursery to discuss, were four lines about a boy taken ill in the mist at Old Meridian, who was sitting up now, and had asked for cake.

Someone had drawn a line in the margin beside it, in pencil, unsteadily, the way a child draws a line who has been told that the ruler is for lessons.

He put it back. He drew out another, further along, and it opened at his own face. He was sixteen, smiling out of an engraving of the portrait in which the painter had first begun to improve upon his jaw, under an account of the Solar youngest’s first season at court. He drew out a third, and found himself at twenty-two, at a masked ball at the Court of Lys, with a long column of verse beneath the account, signed by the second daughter of a marquess. He had last seen it at breakfast in the Citadel, read aloud by his three sisters in three different voices until he hid under the table. He had forgotten it ran to forty stanzas. Somebody had read all forty. The corner of the page had gone soft, as corners go that have been turned back to, and beside the stanza that rhymed his name with a fruit there was a crease across the paper, as if somebody had once closed the book on it rather hard.

He put that one back quickly.

The last of the grey was not a volume. It was a box of the same board, with the year on its spine and no lid, and the sheets in it lay loose as they had come, still creased from the courier’s satchel. He could read the top one from where he knelt. *THE PILGRIM PRINCE: BETROTHED?*, in crimson an inch high, and beside it an engraving of a man in black who might have been any vampire at all, by an engraver who had plainly worked from rumour.

He let that one lie.

He got up off his knee and went along the wall, and the candles in their stand burned straight up as he passed, with nothing in him to lean to.

Above the *Lanterns*, in no order he could see, was everything else. There were histories of the Solar House in three tongues, and a book of the forms of address at the Citadel with a ribbon at the page that set out how a prince of the blood was to be greeted on the first occasion and on every occasion after, and an Alorian grammar for students of the Coast so worn that its spine had been sewn back on with black thread. There was a long run of Alorian poetry in white and gold, with gaps in it, one here and two there. At the end of a row of novels in the cheap red cloth of the circulating libraries of Lutèce there were three more gaps side by side, and beside them, alone, a fourth volume with a ribbon in it two-thirds of the way through.

He knew those novels. He had read the first three in one sitting on his second evening in the east wing, out of pure defiance, and then the third again, out of something less dignified.

He took down the fourth and opened it at the ribbon. It marked the chapter in which the heroine, carried off by a corsair, discovers that the corsair keeps a library.

He put it back, and stood with his mouth pressed shut until he was sure that he would not laugh, because he was not at all sure what would come out of him if he did. At the ends of his fingers, where they still lay on the red cloth, something stirred, faint as the warmth left in a bench by somebody who has just got up from it, and was gone before he could look.

At the end of the shelf was a book of Alorian recipes, of the kind every great kitchen in the Citadel keeps chained to the wall, and it had been opened so often at one page that it would no longer open at any other. *Scones, after the manner of the Citadel kitchens.* There was flour in the gutter of it. There was a broad floury thumbprint on the margin beside *a quick oven*, and beside the thumbprint, in silver ink, in a hand finer than the print, a single word of the Wildmarch tongue that he could not read, written, and struck through, and written again.

He thought of a tray set down on his first morning with the air of evidence laid before a court, and of scones heavy as cobbles, burnt and raw by turns, and a woman with her arms folded watching him eat every one. He remembered what he had thought that morning of whoever wrote out the recipe for her. Out of long habit he began to count back from that first morning, and stopped, because he did not want the sum tonight, and put the book back on its shelf with the flour still in it.

The Harp Vales had a shelf to themselves.

He would have known it across the Citadel. Every book the temple physicians of the Vales had ever printed was bound in the same cloth, the green of beech leaves in their first week, and a novice was given the first of them at six and could expect to be given the last, if he lived, at about forty. He had grown up under that green, and here it was at the top of a black stair in a vampire’s fortress, a whole shelf of it, from the fat commentaries the physicians themselves did not open until their second decade down to the thin books of the first year, and every one of them had been read, which was more than could be said for the shelves of most physicians in the Vales. The spines had gone soft. Slips of black paper stood up out of their tops all along the row like the markers in a clerk’s ledger at the end of a long quarter.

At the head of the row, before the first of them, there was a gap.

It was one book wide, and a thin book. The dust lay along the shelf edge on either side of it and none lay in it, as the dust lies round the place on a mantel where a candlestick has stood all winter and been lifted down, and the green books on either side leaned a little towards one another across the space, as books lean that have stood a long while beside a neighbour who is not there.

Caleb looked at the primer in his hand.

He had carried it up the stair without opening it, and he did not open it now. He put it into the gap. It went in as a key goes into its own lock, without a sound, and the two books on either side stood up straight against it, and it was the same green as all the rest, only faded, and stained along its lower edge where the others were clean. It was the first of the row, and the oldest, and the most read, as if every other book on that shelf had been bought on account of it.

He drew out the fattest commentary at the far end, because a black slip stood up out of it higher than any of the others. It was *Of the Solar Blight, with the Remedies Known*, which he had been told at fourteen that he might read when he was thirty, and the slip marked a page near the end, in the chapter on the keeping of a patient beyond the reach of the sun.

*Failing the sun*, it said, in the serene scholarly hand of every primer of his childhood, *let the patient be laid under stones of the deep trench, not fewer than twelve, and these so hung that no part of him lie in shadow.*

Someone had underlined *twelve*.

He stood with the book open in his two hands in the gold of somebody else’s candles and read the line until it would not keep still, and he thought of the garden, of twelve crystals hung in a spiral down the mountain’s throat so that no terrace of it lay in shadow, and of a ledger at the foot of a black stair with *Withheld* underscored twice in red, and of forty-three books arriving on the first day of his captivity with a primer among them, and a harpsichord, and white tea that was not exported, and he understood, slowly, as one understands at last a word in a language one has heard spoken all one’s life and never been taught, that he had been wrong about every one of them, the books and the tea and the garden, and that whoever kept this wall had sat at that desk and lifted his eyes to it for twenty years, and learned, one green book and one grey volume at a time, how a prince of the Solar House was to be greeted, and fed, and kept warm, and kept alive.

Somebody had learned him by heart.

He put the book back where it had been, with its black slip standing up out of it, and went to the fire, because there was nowhere else in the room he could bear to stand.

A chair stood before it that he did not remember. It was low and deep and old, upholstered in velvet the colour of ripe wheat, the one thing in the room besides the candles that was any shade of gold, and it had been set close to the hearth, closer than anyone who could not feel heat would ever think to set a chair, and turned to the fire. Its cushion was plump and smooth and without a crease, like the pillow in a guest room nobody has yet come to stay in. A footstool stood before it. Over its arm lay a rug of undyed wool that might have been the twin of the one Vesna had dropped round his shoulders on the second morning after the gallery, and when he bent to it, it smelled of the same kitchen fire.

He did not sit at once. He stood before the hearth and held out his hands to it.

It was a good fire, hot and clean and steady, and its heat lay on his skin as the heat of every fire he had ever stood before had lain on it, from the nursery grate to the forge mouths of Dornhöhle, and he waited for it to go in. It went no further than the skin, as the sun goes no further than a closed shutter. His hands stayed grey and plain in it, and nothing in him came up to meet it.

After a long while he went back to the desk and gathered up the cloak, linen and all, and brought it to the fire and sat down in the gold chair with it folded on his knees under both his hands. He could not have said why. It was his, and there was nothing else in the room that was.

The fire burned on. He sat in a study it could not warm, and was not warmed by it either, and the candles stood up straight all round him, and he waited.

\* \* \*

The candles told him first.

All down the long room, on the shelves and the sills and the mantel, every flame leaned at once towards the door, as the golden lilies in the garden turned their heads when somebody opened the iron doors, and every shadow that had lain quiet under the shelves came out and went along the floor the same way. The heat of the fire on his face did not change. The air behind it did. It cooled a breath at a time, from the door inwards, as the first of the night comes into a room through a window someone has opened at one’s back.

Arian stood in the doorway.

He had stopped on the threshold with one hand on the frame, as he had stopped at the garden doors, and at the edge of the sunlight in the mouth of a cave. His face in the candlelight was as white and still as Caleb had ever seen it. He looked at Caleb’s face, and then down at his hands on the grey wool, and kept looking.

“Your guard said I was to go in,” said Caleb.

“Yes.”

“So I came in.” His own voice sounded quite steady, and light, and seemed to be coming from a little to his left again. “You may come in too, if you like. It is your study.”

Arian came in. He shut the door behind him without looking at it, and Paul’s lamp on the floor beside it flickered in the draught and steadied, and he glanced down at it, at its horn shade and its stub of tallow, and his eyes stayed on it a while longer than a common lamp deserved. Then they went back to Caleb’s hands.

He did not come to the fire. He went to the desk and sat down behind it in the hard chair, with the bare black wood before him and all the candlelit width of the room between them, and laid his hands flat on the desk, and the frost began to come out round them on the wood in faint white ferns.

“Caleb.”

The candles did nothing.

They burned straight up in their stands as they had burned since Caleb came in, tall and gold and wholly unmoved, and Caleb saw Arian look at them, and look back at him.

“How is your uncle?” asked Caleb.

Arian took his time.

“He has the chain.” He looked at his own hands in their frost. “He sent me up to see whether you were warm.”

“And am I?”

Arian looked at him across the room, at his hands on the cloak and his face lit by the fire.

“No.”

“No,” Caleb agreed. “I appear to be the one thing in this room the fire will not touch. Apart from you.”

He looked down at the grey wool.

“You left this on your desk.”

“Yes.”

“Where I would find it.”

“If you came.”

“And if I had not come?”

“Then it would have gone back in its drawer.”

Caleb turned up a corner of the collar, so that the dull gold sun showed in the firelight.

“How long have you had it?”

“Since the spring.” Arian’s hands lay still in their frost. “My uncle brought it home with her.”

He said no more, and Caleb found he did not need it. He looked at the square of linen lying open on the desk, and saw it as it must have come up the mountain, folded and tied, in the hand of a man carrying a dead woman home, with Caleb’s own signet in his other hand for the Council, because the law required it. Whatever had burned at the Sanctuary had kept to the vines and the cellars, then, and left the rooms at the top of the house alone.

“And my signet?”

“I opened the linen the night before the Nine heard your name.” Arian’s voice did not change at all.

Caleb sat with that. The fire burned. He thought of a man sitting at this desk alone with a child’s cloak in his hands, and the next morning hearing the name that had been called through the mist read out in a clerk’s voice before the Nine, beside the word *murder*.

And he thought of the other name, the one that ought to have been read out there in place of his, and looked across the room, and saw that Arian had got there before him.

Arian sat as he had sat. Only the frost went out from under his hands across the black wood with a faint dry whisper, as frost goes over the milk left out in a pail on a dairy step, and the ink in the well went solid with a sharp tick, and the candles nearest the desk sank low and burned blue at the wick. Caleb had seen that cold before, on a ledge of the Fault. It had left the prints of the Ashward’s rogues in the rime of the road, and the dent of somebody’s two knees, and nothing whatever standing in any of them.

“He is my brother,” he said.

Arian looked at him for a long time.

“Yes,” he breathed, and the candles stood up again.

“So that is how you knew,” said Caleb at last. “In the hall, on the first night. *I know you were not at the vineyard.* You had my cloak in a drawer.”

“No. I knew because I had met you.”

A coal spat out of the grate and lay glowing on the hearthstone, and neither of them moved to it, and it went grey.

One of Arian’s hands moved on the desk, a finger’s breadth towards the cloak in Caleb’s lap, where the corner of a letter showed under the collar, and was still again.

“Read it.”

“It is addressed to you.”

“It is about you.”

Caleb drew it out from under the collar. It was a single sheet, folded close, of the heavy cream paper the vineyards of the Marches make for themselves, and its folds had gone as soft as an old glove with being opened and closed again. When he unfolded it the firelight came through it from behind and showed him a watermark in it, a vine leaf. He spread it on the grey wool on his knees. The words lay on it in the hand of the label, only the ink was black, and new.

*Tesoro,*

*His name is Caleb. You have known it longer than I have, so I am not telling you anything. I am only saying it, which in this family is a great event, and ought to be marked with a feast.*

*I have washed it three times in cold water and salt, and mended the hem where the briars had it. The collar would not come clean. Leave it so. It is the only receipt he will ever be given for what he did, and I will not have him handed one that has been tampered with.*

*It is to go back to him. Not by a courier, and not by your uncle, who would make a treaty of it. You are to put it into his hands yourself, and tell him what it was for.*

*And then you are to bring him to the Sanctuary, and I will feed him.*

*Zia*

He read it to the end.

He read it again from the top, as Eleanor had taught him, and then he sat with it open on his knees on the grey wool and did not read it a third time, because the words had begun to run on the page, and he could no longer make them out.

*She liked anyone who ate*, the Consigliere had said, in the lamp room, on the day he wore ash on his brow. *She would have liked you.*

She had meant to feed him. She had sat down at a desk at the top of her house among the vines, in her last spring, and set his name down in black as if it were the most ordinary name in the world and she had known it for years, and then she had begun to arrange for him to be brought to her table. And in that same spring his brother had worn his name on a chain down her cellar stair, with her vines going up in fire overhead and forty-one of her people burning in the rows, and she had closed her hand on it as she fell.

Something gave in his chest, as a stitch gives.

And at the inside of his left wrist, below the heel of the hand, where the physicians of the Vales lay two fingers to take a pulse, a thread of gold came up under the skin.

It was a thread, and no more. It came up faint and fine as the first line of light along a ridge before the dawn, the dawn he had stood in on the high pass with his arms flung out and laughed at, and lay there, and he looked down at it through the water in his eyes and at first could not think what it was. Then it took, as a flame takes when the wick has drunk enough wax to trust it. It ran along the inside of his wrist and a little way across the back of his hand, and stopped, and stayed.

On the desk, every candle leaned towards him.

He laughed, cracked and wet and not in the least princely, and put the back of his wrist to his mouth to stop it, and it would not stop, and he let it go on.

“She meant to feed me,” he said, when he could. “She was going to feed me.”

“Yes.”

“I would have gone. I would have gone, and eaten everything she put in front of me, and been ill on her floor, and let her scold me for it.”

“She scolded only the people she meant to keep.”

Caleb folded the letter along its folds and slid it back under the collar where it had been, and smoothed the collar down over it with his gold-threaded hand.

“*Put it into his hands yourself*.”

“Yes.”

“You left it on a desk.”

Arian looked at the cleared black wood in front of him, and the frost spreading across it.

“You did not know me. When you did, you slept. Then your sister came with a sword.”

“That is a great many reasons for a man who never gives any.”

“It is the order in which it happened.”

“And tonight?”

The frost had spread out from his hands across the desk as far as the edge of the empty linen, and there it stopped, at the cloth, as if the linen were a threshold.

“Vesna told me you had put out every candle she lit for you.”

He lifted his eyes, and they went down the room, along the mantel and the window ledge and the shelves, to every flame that was burning there, and he said nothing more.

Caleb looked down at the label on its red twine.

“*Not ours*,” he said. “*To be given back.*”

“I never asked her where it went.” Arian looked at the grey wool in Caleb’s lap. “We do not ask, in this family.”

His eyes went past Caleb then, to the wall he faced across the room, and stopped. Caleb knew without turning where they had stopped.

“You brought it back,” said Arian.

“It fitted.”

“It was the first.”

“Who gave it to you?” asked Caleb. “It is a novice’s book. The Vales hand it to children of six by the dozen, and they spill their milk on it. It is not a book anyone sends to the Coast.”

“My aunt. At the winter fair at Wolfsgrund, the winter after Old Meridian.”

Caleb thought of the lower edge of every page, wavy and stiff where the water had got in.

“And the water?”

“The ford below the town was in flood. She held it over her head.” He was looking at the faded green spine at the head of its row. “I had asked her what had been done to me.”

Caleb could find nothing to say for some time.

“And the rest of them?” he asked at last.

“I bought the rest.”

“All of them.”

“Most of them twice.”

Caleb knew the booksellers of Lutèce, who sent the wrong edition as a matter of principle, and then apologised for it most charmingly and at great length, and sent the right one, and charged for both.

“You read *Of the Solar Blight*. With the Remedies Known.”

“Yes.”

“The physicians of the Vales do not read *Of the Solar Blight* until they are thirty.”

“The physicians of the Vales,” said Arian, “were in no hurry.”

Caleb looked into the fire.

“And the *Lantern*.”

“And the *Lantern*.”

“Twenty years of it.”

Arian said nothing. Caleb supposed it was the one broadsheet in the Dominion that had never printed a word about him that was not true, since the fae could not help themselves.

“There is a poem. From the year I was twenty-two.”

Arian’s face kept perfectly still.

“Did you read it?”

“Twice.”

“And?”

“She rhymed you with a pomegranate.”

Caleb made a sound. It was the sound he had made in the back of *Kolyska* with his hand on his sister’s sword, and he shut his mouth on it, and his shoulders shook, and the thread of gold at his wrist ran up his forearm under the cuff a finger’s length, as a flame runs up a spill.

“And the novels,” he managed.

“I wished to know,” said Arian gravely, “what became of the corsair.”

Caleb put both his hands over his face.

He sat like that a while, with the cloak on his knees and the gold coming and going faintly at his wrist, and he could hear the fire, and the candles drinking at their wax, and nothing at all from the desk, where a man sat with his hands in his own frost and waited for him, as he had waited in the great hall with his hand held out for a seal.

He took his hands down.

“Why do you keep a fire?”

It came out of him plainly, without any wit on it at all, and he heard it go across the room and did not try to call it back.

Arian looked at the hearth.

“You were cold.”

He looked at the fire as a man looks at a horse he has bought at great expense and cannot ride, and Caleb remembered his own hands on the first night, held behind his back in the hall so that nobody should see that he wished to chafe them. It was in the third of the green books, near the beginning, that a child of the sun takes the dark for cold. He had taught it to novices himself.

“That night?”

“That night.”

“And every night since.”

“Yes.”

“I was in the east wing. And then in the garden. I have come up here twice in nine weeks.”

“And tonight.”

“Three times, then. And you have kept a fire every night in a room I came into three times, for a man who cannot feel it.”

“It was not for the man who cannot feel it,” said Arian.

“Tonight,” said Caleb, “it is no use to me either.”

Arian said nothing. He looked at the thread of gold at Caleb’s wrist that had come up at a dead woman’s letter and gone no further, and at the firelight lying on his hands and not going in.

And Caleb thought, sitting in the gold chair, of a dark carriage on the road home, and a voice across it saying *I have stood beside a great many fires*. He had promised him something that was not a fire, by the Coast. And he had come back to the Coast and spent the first night of it in a Consigliere’s office learning what his father had known, and on his back in the dark putting out every light in his room, and he had thought all night long that he had nothing left to give anybody, that he had given it all away, his signet and his portion and the last grain of his light in a cave, and his name at supper, laughing. Yet he sat now in a chair that had been set before a fire for him on the first night he came and never once sat in until tonight, and a dead woman had written his name in black ink and meant to feed him, and across the room a man sat at a desk in the frost of his own hands and waited, as he had been waiting nine weeks at the edge of every light Caleb ever stood in without once stepping over it. And Caleb found that there was one thing left in him after all that he had never given anybody, and he knew where he meant to take it.

He laid the cloak over the arm of the chair, and stood up.

It was eleven paces from the hearth to the desk. He had walked the length of the great hall under the eyes of every house on the Coast and not hurried, and he did not hurry now. The candles leaned after him as he went, faintly, along the mantel and down the shelves, and he felt the heat of the fire leave his back a pace at a time and the cold of the desk come to meet his face. At the eleventh pace he stood at the corner of the desk, beside the hard chair, and Arian looked up at him.

He had never seen that face from above. From above, with the candles leaning in, it was younger, and the one lock of black hair that the barbers of the Coast had long since given up on had come down across the white brow, and the frost on his lashes held the candlelight in grains.

Arian stayed where he was. He sat as he had sat when he asked his question across this same desk, and did not move from his chair.

“On the morning of the *Lantern*,” said Caleb, “I gave you terms.”

“Yes.”

“The third was fair warning. Before any kiss.”

Arian’s hands lay on the black wood in their frost, and did not move.

“It seems only just that I should keep it myself.”

For a moment he was not sure his voice would carry the rest. It carried it.

“May I kiss you?”

Arian looked up at him.

“Yes.”

Caleb lifted his hand and laid it along Arian’s jaw. It was cold, as it had been cold round his wrist on the night of the ring, and he had known that cold ever since only from the outside, as one knows the weather, by the skin. He had never once reached for it. He reached for it now. He bent his head, as Arian had bent his before the whole of Vesperia, and kissed him.

Arian’s mouth was cold under his, and still.

It stayed still. For one breath nothing answered him at all, as Caleb himself had answered nothing on the floor of the great hall, standing in his coronation silk with his mouth gone cold and his wit gone out of him, and a quick cold fright went through him that he had read every book on that wall wrong.

Then Arian leaned.

It was the leaning Caleb had felt once before, in the hall, too slight for anybody watching to have seen, a single heartbeat in which the mouth under his grew firmer and something in the man beneath it leaned to him as helplessly as the shadows of that hall had leaned. Caleb waited, with his hand on that cold jaw, for him to lift his head and step back a pace, as he had then.

He did not step back.

His hand came up and found the small of Caleb’s back, where it had rested for a heartbeat on the threshold of the garden and again before the Nine, and this time it did not tremble once and go. It stayed. It spread there, cold through the silk and the linen beneath, and drew Caleb down, and Arian’s mouth opened under his, and its cold went into Caleb as the first breath of a winter morning goes into a man who has slept too long in a shut room, sharp and clean and all the way to the bottom of him.

He had thought it would taste of iron and snow, as it had in the hall. It tasted of nothing he had a name for. It tasted of cold, and then, where his own mouth lay, it did not, and he understood that it was warming, that the cold of it was taking the warmth of his mouth and keeping it, a breath at a time, as the panes of a glasshouse take the sun and keep it after the sun has gone.

Then Arian rose.

He rose out of the hard chair slowly, without taking his mouth from Caleb’s, as the dusk rises out of a valley in the Marches until it has filled it to the brim, and Caleb’s hand went from his jaw into his hair, and Caleb’s head went back, and back, until he was looking up again as he always had and being kissed from above, and he found that he did not resent it in the least.

And the gold came back.

It rose in him from wherever it had gone, from the soles of his feet and the bottom of his chest, as the tide comes back across a long flat shore that it has left grey and wet all night, from every side at once, filling every channel. It came up his wrists and over his jaw and into his palms, the one in Arian’s hair and the one that had found its way to the black silk over his breast, and his hands warmed, and the warmth went into the cold of the man he held and was not put out by it.

Under the second hand, under the silk, the heart went. He had last had it under his hand in a cave, going as slow as a shepherd walking his bounds. It had given up all its patience now, and ran with his own, stride for stride.

The frost on Arian did not melt. It lay along his shoulders and in the black of his hair and over the hands that held Caleb, white and fine, and it took the gold that was coming up in Caleb and lit with it, every grain, as a field of hoar lights all at once when the sun clears the hill, until he stood in Caleb’s arms glittering from the collar to the crown. The shadows left the walls. They came out from under the shelves and down off the high dark of the ceiling, every shadow the room owned, and gathered round the two of them where they stood, soft and close and heavy, like a cloak too small for one that has been pulled round two.

And all down the room, on the mantel and the window ledge and the branched stand before the wall of green books and grey, every candle leaned in towards the desk, and burned there on its side in its cup, gold, and did not stand up again.

Behind them the fire went on roaring up the chimney, and for once nobody in the room was paying it the least attention.

When at last Caleb drew back, it was only far enough to breathe.

He did not let go, and nor did Arian. They stood there in the leaning light, Caleb’s hand still in that black hair and Arian’s still spread at the small of his back, their foreheads all but touching, and Arian’s breath went out against his mouth and showed, a white breath hanging in the gold, as it had on a carriage step in the Fault with a sun coming undone inside him, and Caleb, who had a physician’s eye even now, saw it, and a thread of the old fear went through the warmth he had only just found.

“Your breath.”

“It is warm.”

He said it low, against Caleb’s mouth, as if he had only now found it out and did not altogether believe it. He had said it so on the carriage step, with the dawn coming. There was no sliver singing in him now to make it so. There was only the frost lit gold all over him, and Caleb’s two hands, and Caleb’s mouth a breath away.

“Yes. That’s me.”

Arian looked at him. His face was white and still under its lit frost, as it had been before the Nine. His eyes were not. They had the look of a cellar on the night its door is first opened after a long laying-down, with a lamp held in at the head of the stair and every bottle in the dark giving the light back at once, and Caleb, who had been looked at all his life by every court in the Dominion, and painted, and engraved, and rhymed with fruit, found that he had to shut his eyes.

He felt Arian’s hand leave his back.

It came round his side, slowly, under the open edge of his coat, as if it were finding its way in the dark, and up towards the breast, where his heart was going like a wren in a cupped hand, and where the ring sat against his ribs behind his own black stitches, cold, beyond the reach of any of his gold.

He caught the hand before it got there.

He caught it at the wrist, lightly, and turned it, and laid two of those long cold fingers on the inside of his own left wrist, over the pulse, where the gold had first come back.

“If you wish to count it, it is easier here. It is the usual place.”

Arian’s fingers lay where he had put them. He did not press. He stood with his fingertips on Caleb’s pulse, as Caleb had once laid two fingers on his in the back of a cave and counted twelve, and Caleb felt his own heart going under them far too fast, and could do nothing about it at all.

He knew the stillness of a man counting a pulse, and could see that this one did not care for the sum.

“It has just been kissed,” teased Caleb. “Give it a moment. It will remember its manners.”

The fingers stayed. After a while they rested more lightly, and he understood that Arian was only making sure of it, as a thumb had once made sure of it on the floor of a black gallery, a long time ago.

“Caleb,” murmured Arian.

Every candle in the room went up.

They had lain on their sides all this while, leaning in, and at his name they stood up straight all together and flared, tall and gold, from the mantel to the window, so that the room went bright from end to end, as a lantern goes bright when it is lifted to a face. The green books and the grey stood out on their shelves in it, every spine, and the gold in Caleb went up to meet it until it showed through the cuffs of his own shirt.

“Ah,” said Caleb, not quite steadily. “There it is.”

“Yes,” said Arian, and did not look at the candles at all.

“I said I would find you something that was not a fire. By the Coast.”

“You found it a long way short of the Coast,” said Arian, with his two fingers still on Caleb’s wrist and the candles blazing all round them. “You did not know.”

“When?”

Arian did not answer. He looked past Caleb’s shoulder, down the long bright room, to the gold chair by the fire, and the grey cloak lying folded over the arm of it.

Somebody knocked on the door.

It was a knock Caleb knew, two blows and then a third, heavy and unhurried, the knock of somebody with both hands full who has kicked the door with the side of her boot and does not intend to kick it again.

They did not step apart at once. When they did, it was by perhaps a hand’s breadth, and Arian’s fingers left Caleb’s wrist last.

“Come in,” said Arian.

Vesna came in backwards, shouldering the door, with a tray in her arms. On it was a pot of Wildmarch tea under a knitted cosy the colour of beetroot, and a cup, and a plate of scones under a cloth, and beside them a single black glass of blood-wine carried up hot and breathing a thin dark thread of steam. She turned round with it in the doorway, and saw the room.

She saw the candles all standing up bright, and the grey cloak folded over the gold chair, and the empty linen on the desk, and the Sovereign of the Obsidian Coast standing at the corner of it a hand’s breadth from her *malyshka*, and the gold standing up in Caleb’s skin from his collar to his knuckles.

Her face did nothing whatever.

She came down the room and set the tray on the desk, at the far end of it from the Sovereign, on the other side of the linen, without a word. She moved the black glass a little towards the Sovereign with one finger, and the cup a little towards Caleb with another, and took the cloth off the scones and folded it and laid it over her arm. Then she looked at Caleb.

“His Highness has his colour back.”

“His Highness,” protested Caleb, with all the dignity left to him, which was not a great deal, “was just going to bed.”

“His Highness will eat first.” She pushed the plate an inch towards him. “And the Sovereign will drink that, and not leave it on the windowsill to go cold like the last.”

“Vesna,” said Arian.

“Sovereign.”

Caleb, who had never yet heard anybody on the Coast tell its Sovereign to finish his supper, studied the ceiling with great attention until he could trust his mouth again.

Then he looked at the scones. They had been good for weeks, risen and golden and split along their sides, and he thought of a floury thumbprint in a book on the wall behind him, and a word in silver ink that had been crossed out and set down again.

“Vesna. Who wrote out the recipe for these? For my first morning?”

Vesna considered him. Then she looked at the Sovereign, who was regarding the black glass of blood-wine as if he had never seen one before, and back at Caleb.

“Ask him yourself, *malyshka*.”

And she went out with the cloth over her arm and pulled the door to behind her, and Caleb heard her go away down the passage, past the two lycans at the head of the stair, and say something to them in the Wildmarch tongue that made the younger one laugh out loud and stop at once.

Caleb turned his head.

“Whoever wrote out that recipe had plainly never met a scone in his life.”

“No. I had not.”

Caleb opened his mouth to ask the rest of it. He looked at the scones, and at the man standing beside them with the frost still lit gold along his shoulders, and found he had no wish for the answer tonight, or not nearly so much as for the scone.

Arian went down the room to the fire. The flames sank low on the logs as he came near, and lay there until he had gone by, and he took up the cloak from the chair and came back with it, folded, in both hands, as a steward carries a thing on a cushion before a court.

He did not hold it out. He put it into Caleb’s hands. He laid it in them, and closed Caleb’s fingers round the grey wool with his own, and kept his hands there over Caleb’s a moment, cold over warm.

“Into your hands. Myself.”

Caleb looked down at it, at the grey wool and the silver thread and the tarnished sun of his house at the collar, lying in his two gold hands.

And as he looked, the sun at the collar woke. It took the gold out of his fingers as every sunburst of his house takes in the noon, slowly, from the centre out along its rays, and gave it back, until the little sun on a cloak that had lain folded in the dark for twenty years shone up on his palms as bright as it had shone on a slope above Old Meridian, on a morning when he had been told four times not to get it muddy.

He took the gold chair before the fire with the cloak across his knees and a scone in his hand, and ate it, every crumb, because he had been told to that night by two women, and only one of them was left to see him do it, and he meant to obey them both.
