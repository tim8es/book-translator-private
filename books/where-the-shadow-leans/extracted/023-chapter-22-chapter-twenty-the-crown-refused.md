## Chapter 22: Chapter Twenty - The Crown Refused

*The sun shall go from the King to the eldest son of his body, and failing him to the next son, and so down through the sons of the house in the order of their birth. While any son of the house lives and is not set aside, the sun shall be asked of no daughter. The daughters of the house shall stand upon the steps, and answer for the blood.*

— From the Statutes of the Solar Throne, the Article of the Sun

\* \* \*

It took the High Paladin of Aloria, twenty white cloaks hung on twenty halberds, and a stair that no prince of the Solar House had ever been supposed to know about, to get the Coast out of the Throne Hall, and it took Caleb’s two arms to keep the noon off it until the wall closed behind them.

He held the light bowed back all the way down the lane to the west doors, with the grey cloak up in his hands, and on through the vestibule, where the noon lay across the marble in white bars from a row of tall windows that Arian had walked past on his way in.

Matilda laid her palm flat on a carved sunburst in the panelling, and a door opened in it no higher than her shoulder. The dark came out to meet them. It smelled of tallow and cold stone and the burnt ends of wicks, and Arian went into it under the cloak with his head still bent. The Guard brought their white awning up to the door and lowered it, and Enzo stepped out from under it into the dark of the wall with his blistered hand held a little away from his coat, as a man steps down from a carriage into a puddle he has been warned about.

Caleb let the light go.

It went back all at once. He heard it go, a long soft sigh down the length of the hall behind them, as the breath goes out of a choir at the end of an anthem. Matilda drew the door to on the noon and the court and two hundred faces, and the world came down to the lamp-men’s stair and the gold coming off his own seams.

His arms came down. They came down of their own accord, like a drawbridge when the men at the windlass have let go of the chains, and the cloak came down with them, and Arian took it out of his hands before it touched the step.

The stair went up through the thickness of the palace wall, narrow and steep and turning, with a niche at every turn for a lantern and no lantern in any of them, because the lamp-men knew the way. Caleb went up it by the light of his own seams, which lay on the worn treads in thin gold lines, and somewhere about the fortieth step his knees began to shake. He had held up the noon of a hall that had been built to hold it. His legs had been keeping an account of it, as legs will, and had chosen the stair to present the bill.

A hand found the small of his back.

It did not push. It only stayed there, flat and cold through the black silk, as it had stayed in a square at Wolfsgrund, and the stair went on, and he went on with it.

The lamp-men’s door to his own rooms came out at the back of his dressing room, behind a panel that his nurse had told him at six was where the mice kept their parliament, and was never to be opened in case they were sitting. He opened it. Nobody was sitting.

He went through into the long room beyond, where the noon was standing in at every window as he had left it that morning, and went down the room and closed the shutters one after another and set the bars in their brackets, and the light went out of the long room in white lengths, as washing comes in off a line ahead of rain, until the last of it lay in threads at the cracks and there was nothing between the three of them and the dark but his own gold.

Five days ago he could not have told anybody which way the bars went. His hands knew now.

Behind him, the frost was coming back.

He heard it before he turned, a faint dry whispering like sugar poured into a bowl. Arian was standing in the middle of the dark room with the grey cloak hanging from one hand, and the white was coming out on him everywhere, on his hair and lashes and the scorched black silk of his shoulders, grain by grain, as if the dark had been keeping it for him at the door and was handing it back.

Enzo had found the window seat. He sat in it with his back against the barred shutter and his burned hand palm up on his knee, and looked about him at the books lying open on their faces wherever they had been left, with their spines cracked, with the forbearance of a man who keeps a library and has always been obeyed in it.

Caleb found the lamp on his writing table, a pretty thing of white glass on gilt feet that nobody had ever needed to light, and the wick, which some patient lamp-man had trimmed every quarter for twenty-eight years and must every quarter have wondered what for, took the flame with an air of great astonishment. The light made a round yellow room of its own in the middle of the long one.

The coronation silk was still on its chair, in tissue, stiff with gold thread. Caleb lifted it off by the shoulders and laid it on the floor, where it went on sitting up by itself, having had five days’ practice.

“Sit down.”

Arian looked at the chair, and at the coat on the floor, and sat.

In the lamplight the burns were worse than they had looked in the noon. The whole of his face had gone the dark red of a quince in a slow oven from the brow to the line of the jaw, and along the cheekbone the long blister had burst and wept and dried, and the frost was coming out over all of it, white on red, like rime on a windfall apple. The knuckles of his left hand were blistered from the vestibule windows. And across the palm of his right, where Andrew’s light had met it, the skin had gone black and split, in a burn the breadth of a man’s throat.

Caleb knelt between the chair and the coat that was sitting up by itself, and took the burned hand in both of his and turned it palm up, and laid his own palm over it, and the Grace came.

Five days of the Citadel’s noon had gone into him on the landing and the terraces and in the Throne Hall, whether he wanted it or not, and filled him as a cup is filled that has been left under a courtyard fountain, and now it went out of his hands into the cold one under them like a breath let go at last. The flesh knew the way, and remembered it, and the burn in the palm went down beneath his hands, and the skin closed over it white and smooth and cold, and the frost came out across it at once.

He did the knuckles. Then he came up on his knees and put his hands on Arian’s face.

Arian shut his eyes.

He sat quite still under Caleb’s palms with frost in his lashes and let it be done, as he had let it be done once in a jolting carriage on the road from the Fault, with Caleb keeping his eyes on his work. Caleb did not keep his eyes on his work. He watched the red go out of the cheekbone under his hands, slowly, as the colour goes out of the west after the sun is down, and the blister flatten and close, and the white come back, and when there was no red anywhere under his palms he left them where they were.

“You walked past every window in that vestibule.”

“There was one more on the stair.”

“There is a door in the wall.”

Arian opened his eyes. “My uncle chose the route.”

From the window seat, Enzo made a sound like a page being turned.

“The Grand Alchemist’s back stair. Van Houten left us on her step in two casks before your city was awake. She had her mouth full of pins.” He regarded his blistered hand. “She told us not to tread on anything.”

Caleb thought of his sister on the step above him that morning with her chin up, saying nothing. He had not known what else she had been keeping behind her teeth.

“Did you? Tread on anything?”

“Your Sovereign trod on a cat. It forgave him.”

Arian’s eyes had gone past Caleb, down the long room, to the bed.

The lamp did not reach it, and Caleb had no need of it. He knew without turning round that the coverlet lay smooth over the whole of the bed but the foot, where it kept the long hollow his mother had worn into it, with a white shawl folded at its head.

“Who sleeps at the foot of your bed?” Arian asked.

“My mother.”

Arian said nothing.

“She came every night while I was on the Coast. Eleven weeks. She did not light the lamps. She lay down at the foot of an empty bed and waited for whoever it belonged to.” He took his hands from Arian’s face at last, and sat back on his heels.

“I sat on a sill,” said Arian.

“I know.”

For a while they both looked at the hollow in the dark.

Then Arian reached inside his coat, where the cloak had ridden north against his chest, and took out a napkin tied at the corners.

“Vesna.”

There were three. They had crossed the Marches in a wine cask on a Sovereign’s knee, and they were as hard now as the cobbles of a hill town. Caleb ate all three, sitting on the floor of his own bedchamber with his back against the leg of the writing table and the crumbs going down the black silk, and Arian watched him do it as Vesna had watched him eat her first dreadful scones, with her arms folded, as if it were evidence at a trial.

“They are very bad.” Caleb brushed the crumbs off the crescent. “You will tell her they were the best she has ever made.”

“She will know it is not true,” said Enzo.

“She will know what it means.”

“Donna Valentina asked that you be told you dance very well,” said Arian. “Once, without anything after it.”

Caleb looked up from the empty napkin in his lap. She had told him so once already, at the gala, with a dreadful evening after it. He thought of a lacquer box with a jet thorn on its lid, and what a woman might have had to set on the other side of the scale to carry it to a Sovereign’s door.

“Her grandfather.”

“Yes.”

“Then tell her that she dances very well also. Without anything after it.”

Arian said nothing. He laid his healed hand on his knee.

On the window seat Enzo drew a paper out of his coat, folded long, and held it out without getting up. Caleb went and took it, and sat down again by the chair to unfold it under the lamp.

It was a list in Enzo’s hand, in more than one ink. Don Salvatore stood at the head of it. Under him came the brother who had kept the Ashward’s books and never once blotted a line, and the daughter who had kept its gardens at the water’s edge, and the grandson who had paid the Marches for the hour of Arian’s road, and three more, and a ruled line went through every name. Beside some of them, in a smaller hand, the Consigliere had entered where the ash had been found, as a clerk enters the place of delivery. *At his desk.* *On the strait.* Beside the daughter there was only one word, *gloves*, and he did not understand it, and then he did. He turned it over. On the back, by itself, in ink that was still black, stood *Valentina Caruso*.

Enzo had turned his own burned hand over on his knee and was looking at the palm of it, as a man looks at a coin he has already counted.

Caleb had felt a heart go up high and hard and alone on the fourth night, and had counted it down from a long way off, and had told himself afterwards that he did not need the rest. Here was the rest. It sat in his own chair in the lamplight with the frost returning over a face he had just healed, and had let eight lives be handed to him on a sheet of paper without a word, and the hand on its knee was the hand that had called them home across a harbour and a strait, and the hand was perfectly steady.

“The children?” Caleb asked.

“Alive. They are not Carusos.”

“Would you do it again?”

“Yes.”

He had said it to Caleb once already, with Caleb’s blood still gold at his mouth, and Caleb had not forgiven him the floor. He did not soften it now. He sat in the chair and let Caleb look at him, the Sovereign of the Obsidian Coast as the Nine had seen him on the fourth night, and Caleb looked.

“Is there anybody that you would not?”

Arian looked down at him.

“One.”

Caleb did not ask which. He was sitting at the man’s feet with the crumbs of Vesna’s scones down his coat, and he had been told the answer in a black gallery, and a cave, and a lane of shadow in his father’s hall, and he understood now that nobody else on the Coast had ever been told it at all.

He laid the list down, and reached up and took the hand off Arian’s knee, the one he had healed, and held it in both of his. It was cold and steady, and it was the same hand. It turned over in his palms and lay open there, and did not grip.

“She danced very well,” Caleb murmured.

Nothing came after it.

They stayed so a while. The lamp burned in its round of yellow on the writing table, and Enzo looked at the barred shutter beside him as if there were something to be seen through it.

The speeches were still in his pocket, under the violets and the flask, folded in four, five mornings of them.

He took them out and held them up.

“Your notes. You told me you did not care to be read to.”

Arian held out his hand. “I have looked.”

Caleb gave them to him. He did not need the lamp. He read them in the dark with his head a little bent, as he read the Council’s papers, and Caleb sat on the floor and watched his face do the reading.

He read the first, and turned it over. He read the second, and something happened at the corner of his mouth.

“I should like to be introduced also.”

“To the cat?”

“Yes.”

He read the third, the one with nearly everything crossed out, and the one line left. *Cecily is going to put up her hair.*

“She did.”

“It came down.”

“I watched it come down.” He turned to the last, the one about what the noon had been waiting for.

Arian read it slowly, to the end. Then he held out his hand again, without looking up.

“Pencil.”

Caleb gave him the bitten one from the writing table. Arian wrote something under the last line, short, in a hand Caleb had seen in silver on black paper and never in pencil on white, and folded the five papers along their own folds and put them inside his coat, where the cloak and the scones had been.

“It is written down.”

Caleb had no need to see it. He knew what it said.

On the Sovereign’s right hand, when he laid it on his knee again, there were three rings, and his thumb crossed to the third finger as it had every day for four weeks and found the heavy gold there, and turned it, a quarter turn, at something.

He healed Enzo last.

Enzo said it was nothing. Caleb said that he had healed five burns from the noon on the backs of vampires’ hands this spring and had become a great authority, and held out his own, and after a moment Enzo surrendered the hand as a man surrenders his hat to a cloakroom he does not trust, and Caleb sat beside him on the window seat with it in his lap.

“My wife had a name for every soul she meant to keep,” said Enzo.

The blisters went down under Caleb’s hand. He did not look up from them.

“*Tesoro*. I have read it.”

“I have only titles,” said Enzo. The hand in Caleb’s lap lay quite still. “I should like one to mean more than is owed. With your leave.”

Caleb took his palm away. The back of Enzo’s hand lay white and smooth under the lamp, with the frost coming back over it in a fine grey bloom.

“Which?”

“*Altezza*.”

Caleb looked at him.

“That is *Highness*. You have called me Highness since the first night.”

“Not in my tongue.”

The lamp’s flame leaned a little towards the window seat, as lamps lean for the Umbral houses, and stood there leaning. Caleb watched it lean and could not say anything at all for a moment, and something had got into his eyes, which was absurd, over a word he had been called ten thousand times by people who meant nothing whatever by it.

“Yes.”

“*Altezza*,” said Enzo, as a man sets a coin down on a counter in a strange country and finds that it is good there.

\* \* \*

There were two knocks at the outer door, light and quick and not waiting for an answer, and the lamp on the writing table, which was the only lamp in the room, brightened.

Eleanor came in with a man behind her and stopped in the round of yellow light. She looked at the Sovereign of the Obsidian Coast in her brother’s chair and the Consigliere in her brother’s window seat and her brother with crumbs on his coat, and did the sum, and put the answer away.

“Sovereign. Consigliere.”

“Your Highness.” Arian rose. Enzo was already standing.

The man behind her was a long dry elf in the grey of the Statutes, with spectacles on a white ribbon and a book under his arm as thick as a hearthstone, bound in white vellum. Caleb knew him. He had sat on the Keeper of the Statutes’ knee at seven and been told the names of the Articles in order, as children in other countries are told the names of the stars, and had fallen asleep at the Article of Tithes.

“My lord Keeper has something to read you,” said Eleanor. “He has read it to me. Twice.”

The Keeper bowed. He opened the white book on his forearm, where it lay as heavy as a sleeping child, and put on his spectacles, and read the Article of the Sun in a voice like a pen that has been cut very fine and seldom used. Then he turned back to a place marked with a white ribbon.

“And from the Order of the Crowning. *If the blood will not stand under the sun of him that kneels, he shall rise without it, and be set aside, and the sun shall be asked of the next.*”

He closed the book.

“The Order has set His Royal Highness the Crown Prince aside. His Majesty has laid the crown down and may not take it up again. The Council sits at the second bell of the night in emergency session, because the Accord is to be made new at the hour of the Eclipse, and the Solar Throne must put a crowned hand to it. His Majesty keeps the great seal until there is a crowning, and may seal what this Council lays before him, but he may not swear a treaty under a crown he has laid down. There is no crowned hand.” He turned a page. “And so the Article goes down the sons of His Majesty’s body in the order of their birth, and finds the next. It does not look at the steps. While a son of the house lives and is not set aside, it asks no daughter, though she be the elder by eight years and the High Paladin besides. Until there is a crowning, the son it has found takes the King’s place at the Council of the Throne, and the Council answers to him.” He took his spectacles off and folded them, and regarded Caleb as he had once regarded a boy asleep over the Article of Tithes, with great kindness and no help at all. “By the Article of the Sun, Your Royal Highness, the sun is yours.”

Nobody in the room said anything.

Caleb looked at his sister. She stood in the round of light with her gloved hands quiet on her despatch case, and her face gave him nothing whatever. She had read it twice. He saw that she had not read it once on her own account.

Behind him in the dark, he heard the Sovereign of the Coast not move at all.

The Hall of Petitions had been built for asking.

It ran the length of the palace’s west front under a ceiling painted the deep blue of a night nobody in the Citadel had ever seen, set all over with stars in gold leaf. A fae astronomer of Lys had once leaned across a state dinner in real distress to tell Caleb that every one of them was wrong.

Down both sides ran the benches of the petitioners, polished pale along their front edges by centuries of people sitting on the very ends of them. At the far end the King’s chair stood on its step under a canopy with nobody in it. Tonight the tall west windows were shuttered and barred, and the hall was lit by lamps, which it had never been built for, and the gold-leaf stars had caught the lamplight and were giving it back from all their wrong places, as pleased with themselves as the gilt buttons on a page’s first livery.

On the wall above the benches, between two shuttered windows, hung Andrew’s portrait, painted the year he turned twenty-five. Somebody had put a white cloth over it.

The eleven lords of the Council sat at the long table below the step in primrose and rose and dawn-blue silks gone creased with the day, and Eleanor sat at its head in the Arch-Chancellor’s chair. The Keeper stood at his lectern. On a stool at the end of the table, which nobody had offered him, the high priest of the Citadel temple sat with the sun on its white velvet cushion in his lap, holding it as a nurse holds an infant she has been told on her life not to wake, because he had not let it out of his sight since noon. At a little table of his own, set a measured distance from the lords, the dwarf of the Holds sat with his ledger open.

Matilda stood at the door with her shoulders against it. Cecily sat on the front bench of the petitioners with her hair down her back and her hands between her knees.

And beyond the last of the lamps, by the shuttered windows at the hall’s far end, Arian and his uncle stood in the dark, some paces apart, and every lamp flame down the long table leaned towards them, as the candles on a temple altar all lean together when somebody opens the vestry door behind them.

They had set the King’s chair on its step for Caleb, since the Statutes said that it was his until morning. He had looked at it a while, and then taken the chair at the foot of the table, facing the empty step, and nobody had been able to find the Article that forbade it.

The Keeper read it all again, and the Article that permitted an emergency sitting after it, because the Statutes required it, and the lords listened with the patience of men who had heard every word of it before and never once expected it to be read about them.

“The Coast will speak to the Accord,” said Eleanor, “since it is a party.”

Enzo came forward to the edge of the lamplight and no further.

“The Coast will put its hand to the Accord beside a crowned hand of the Solar Throne, my lords. Beside no other.” He inclined his head to the empty chair on its step. “It is only what the paper says.”

“Then the Solar Throne must have a crowned hand by the first bell,” said a lord in primrose, “if it is to be in the Marches by the hour.” He turned to Caleb, and the turning went down the table, lord after lord, as the dancers in a long set turn all together at the end of a figure. “Your Royal Highness will kneel at the third bell. The priest is ready.”

The priest on his stool held the sun a little tighter.

“The court saw what Your Royal Highness did in the Throne Hall today,” said a lady in rose, and her voice had gone soft with it. “The noon bent for you. The Vales say that the first kings of the Solar House carried their own sun.”

“And the Article is silent on the bond-law,” said another, who took care not to look towards the dark end of the hall. “There is no reason why a King of Aloria should not be promised to the Sovereign of the Coast. There may be some advantage in it.” He coughed. “The ships.”

Caleb sat where he had put himself and listened to them.

He had been told a great many things in that palace. He had been told to wait a season, and that he was needed at court, and by a decree in his father’s florid hand that he had gone on pilgrimage. And now, now that the case had come round again, now that the Keeper had read him the Article and the Order and the Article that permitted the reading, now that a sun lay in a priest’s lap waiting for his head and every face down that green table had already set it there, he understood that since the day he was born he had been told everything that had ever been decided about him, and fitted for it, and dressed.

Nobody had asked him.

“I should prefer,” said a voice from the end of the hall, “that somebody asked him.”

It was not raised. It came down the hall out of the dark, unhurried, as a draught comes under a door in winter and finds every ankle in the room, and every lord at the table turned his head towards it, and every lamp flame along the green cloth leaned further, and stayed leaning.

Arian had not moved from the shuttered window. Caleb could see only the white of his face in the dark, and the hand at his side with the heavy gold on its third finger, quite still. The lord in primrose looked at the Keeper. The Keeper looked at his book, which was no help.

“Thank you, Sovereign,” said Caleb. “I am asked so seldom in this hall that I had nearly forgotten what one says.” He turned back to the table. “Well, my lord? Ask me.”

The lord in primrose drew a breath.

“Will Your Royal Highness kneel at the third bell, and take the sun?”

“No,” said Caleb.

The lamps along the table did not know what to do. Neither did the lords. At his little table the dwarf of the Holds dipped his pen and began to write, slowly.

“I was taught before my letters that the Crown Prince cannot be given, and that the second son is there in case. I was there in case for twenty-eight years, my lords, at every feast and every embassy that wanted a prince who could be spared, and in the spring the case came, and I was given.” He did not look at the white cloth on the wall. “Now I am told that the case has come round again. I find that I am not what it needs.”

“Nobody raised me for that sun, my lords. My brother had the masters of law at nine and the voice of the dais at twelve and a chair at this table at sixteen, and he has never once in his life been allowed to say a thing in a room only because it was funny. I had a harpsichord, and a pony that bit, and the Harp Vales, because nobody much minded what the second son learned so long as it was not this.” He spread his hands on the cloth. “And I have been happy. I do not suppose this hall has ever been told so by anybody it meant to crown, so you had better hear it once. I have been happy all my life. I have gone where I pleased and said what I thought and danced with whoever asked me. I have lost every game this court plays, on purpose, and won at cards.” His voice did not stay quite level, and he let it go where it wanted. “And I have watched my father sit at a window with the whole of Aloria on his back for twenty-eight years, and never once go anywhere, and never once turn round. I know what that sun weighs. I watched a man hold it up all day over an empty floor until his arms shook. I am not afraid of it, my lords. I am only not such a fool as to want it.”

“Highness,” the Keeper began. “The Article —”

“Says sons. I heard it. You read it beautifully.” He stood up. “You have three daughters of this house in this hall tonight, my lords, and the Article has asked them for nothing but to stand on the steps. Shall I tell you what they have done this year, while the sons were otherwise occupied?”

“My sister Matilda stood in the snow at the Pas de la Dame for forty-four days with two toes she could not feel, because a letter had told her not to come, and today she turned the whole Radiant Guard with a single word, and the word was *Stand*.”

Behind him at the door, the High Paladin’s mail made no sound at all.

On the front bench, Cecily had pressed the back of her wrist to her mouth.

“My sister Cecily was lied to by the one person she trusted most in the world, and when she learned what it had done, she came down a step that was not hers before the courts of the world and said so. And then she said no.” He let that lie on the green cloth. “I know no harder word in any tongue, my lords, and she said it to the heir.”

The lamps had begun to brighten, and he knew why without turning his head.

“And my sister Eleanor.”

He looked at her then. She sat at the head of the table in her dark Chancellery blue, the Treasury’s chain lying flat across her breast, and her gloved hands folded on her despatch case, and all the lamps in the Hall of Petitions were burning higher and whiter over her than they had any right to, and she was looking at the green cloth.

“Eleanor has kept the books of this kingdom since she was twenty-three. She dined at the Sovereign’s own table with a warrant in her glove that would have put me on a yacht, and never drew it, because I had asked to be asked. And when the Throne of this house would not require an inquiry for its own son, she required one, and tomorrow she will hold it, and she will begin with us. Every lamp in this palace brightens when she comes into a room. I have always told her it was fright. My lords, I think it is the lamps knowing who keeps the oil accounts.”

The dwarf of the Holds made a noise into his beard and went on writing.

“Any one of them is far better suited to that sun than I am. I should make Aloria a very poor King.” He thought about it. “I should make it a very entertaining one, for perhaps a season. And then you would all want a Queen.”

The Keeper of the Statutes took his spectacles off.

“The Article says sons, Highness,” he said, quite gently. “It has said sons for a thousand years. It does not know how to be refused. While Your Royal Highness lives and is not set aside, the sun is yours, and if you will not kneel for it, it will wait for you in a priest’s lap, and the Accord will wait with it.”

“Then it was written by men who had never met my sisters.”

At the door, a laugh got out, short as a bark, and was shut in again at once. It was a sound Caleb had heard perhaps one year in three since he was born. He did not turn round.

“It has been amended twice. Once to forbid a King of Aloria to marry his first cousin, and once, forty years later, to allow it again. You read me both, my lord, when I was seven, from the back of the book, under a ribbon. I fell asleep at Tithes. I stayed awake for the cousins, because it seemed to me the only place in all the Statutes where anybody had changed his mind.” He held out his hand across the table. “You told me an hour ago that until there is a crowning, the Council of the Throne answers to me. I have never given this Council an order in my life. I should like to give it one.”

The Keeper looked at the hand for some time. Then he took the pen from his lectern and laid it in Caleb’s palm, as a man gives up the key of his own house to a guest who has asked for it civilly.

Eleanor pushed a sheet of the Chancellery’s blue down the green cloth. It came the length of the table and stopped against his knuckles, and she did not look up from her case.

He wrote it standing, in the long untidy hand that the Vales had despaired of, and the lamps leaned over his shoulder to see. Then he read it out to them.

“*The sun shall go from the King to the eldest child of his body, be it son or daughter, and failing that child to the next, and so down through the children of the house in the order of their birth. And every one of them shall stand upon the steps for the others, and answer for the blood.*”

He laid the pen down.

“That puts me fifth. On the lowest step. I have stood there all my life, my lords, and I can tell you that the view from it is excellent.” He looked down the table. “The Statutes ask it of the Council standing, under the King’s seal. Will you stand?”

For a while nobody moved.

Then the oldest of them got up. He was a lord in a white so faded that it had gone the colour of milk, who had sat at that table since before Caleb’s father was born, and he got up with both hands on the table, slowly, as a man climbs a stile, and looked at the door, and the front bench, and the head of the table, for a long time, and said to nobody, “My mother would have liked this,” and stood.

Nobody stood with him.

“No, Highness,” said the lord in primrose, with great courtesy, as a man declines a second helping. “This Council will not unmake a thousand years in an evening on the word of a prince who will not take up the crown he gives his orders with.” He laid his hands flat on the green cloth. “We will sit. The sun will keep. Your Royal Highness will be reconciled to it in time. Princes are.”

“And the Accord?” Caleb asked.

“The Accord has kept a thousand years. It will keep a day.” The lord in primrose glanced down the hall towards the dark at its end, and away again, quickly. “The Coast has as much need of it as we.”

Down the table, chair by chair, the lords of the Council of the Throne folded their hands and sat on, as a field of barley lies down under a squall and waits for it to have done. The lady in rose would not look at him. The old lord in milk-white stood by himself with his hands on the table, and did not sit down again.

“The Coast renews the Accord,” said Arian, “with the crown the prince names.”

He did not raise his voice. He did not move from the shuttered window. Every lamp flame along the green cloth lay down flat at once and pointed at him, as the weed in a river lies out along the current, and went blue at the root, and the Hall of Petitions went dim from end to end. Only the lamps over Eleanor’s chair stood up straight and white, as if they had been told something the others had not.

“Or with none.”

The lord in primrose wet his lips. “With none, Sovereign, there is no Accord.”

“Then there is no Aloria,” Arian answered, “to renew it with.”

The cold came down the hall.

It came along the green cloth from the dark end in a white bloom, as rime comes over the inside of a window on a night of hard frost, and the old varnish of the table crazed under it as it came with a dry ticking, like a deathwatch beetle in a wainscot. It went up the legs of the King’s empty chair on its step and out over the gilt sunburst of the canopy, and the gold under it went as dull as the coins they lay on the eyes of the dead. The glass chimney of the nearest lamp split across with a crack like a knuckle, and the flame inside it lay down on the wick and went out.

Then the shadows went.

The lamps had laid a shadow behind every chair at that table, long and soft across the boards, as lamps will in any room of the Citadel where the noon is not standing overhead. They began to leave. They came out from under the lords of the Council of the Throne by inches, the primrose and the rose and the faded blue, every one down the table, and drew away over the floor towards the shuttered windows and the man standing among them, and they pulled at the heels they belonged to, as the sea pulls the sand out from under a man who stands in the shallows at the turn. The lord in primrose looked down at his own going away from him across the boards and took hold of the table with both hands. Every lord in that hall had heard by now, off the Lowmere quays, what had become of a house of the Coast five nights ago, eight of its blood in a single hour, one at his desk and one on the open strait, at a word. None of them knew what came after for an elf whose shadow was called in, and none of them wished to be the first to learn it.

At the edge of the lamplight, Enzo spoke, as a man remarks on the weather to a neighbour at a gate.

“Every stone on every spire of this city came up out of the Coast’s trench, my lords. Last winter the Coast sent half.” He inclined his head. “The Sovereign was being patient.”

Caleb did not tell him to stop.

He stood at the foot of the table with ink on his fingers, and the frost went past him along the cloth on either side and did not touch his hands, and he found, a little to his own dismay, that he did not wish it gone. Down the thread the other heart went on keeping his count, slow and even, in no hurry for anybody.

The lady in rose stood up.

She stood up white to the lips, and after her the rest of them, the chairs going back on the boards one after another like doors banging shut down a corridor in a gale, until the whole Council of the Throne was on its feet, all eleven, with their hands on a table gone white with frost and their shadows stretched out behind them the length of the hall.

The lord in primrose stood last.

The dwarf of the Holds wrote a line, and blotted it, and turned his ledger round on his table so that the Council could see that it had been written.

“The Holds have it,” he said, in stiff hammered Alorian. “Eleven, standing.” He regarded the rime that had come up over the edge of his page. “The Holds will also have it that they stood in some haste.”

“Thank you, Sovereign,” said Caleb. “They are standing. You may give them back their shadows. I expect they will want them for the walk home.”

The shadows came home. They came back across the boards one by one, each to its own chair, slowly, and looking back, as children come in off a frozen pond when they are called for the third time, and lay down where they belonged. The lamps stood up again in their glasses, all but the one with the split chimney. The frost stayed on the green cloth. Nobody touched it.

“And the King’s seal,” said the Keeper, faintly.

Since the morning he laid down the crown, nobody had gone through the King’s door but a page with a tray, who had come out again with the tray untouched. Eleanor had opened her despatch case and taken out a long white case of leather with the sunburst stamped on its lid in gold, and laid it on the frost in front of her, and kept her hand on it.

“I will take it to him,” said Caleb.

\* \* \*

The great seal of the Solar Throne was a sunburst of old gold no bigger than a child’s palm, on a handle of white ivory worn yellow by forty years of one man’s grip. Caleb carried it down the long west gallery in its case under his arm with the new Article folded on top of it in his own untidy hand and eleven names under it in theirs, and the last time he had held it he had been nine years old and had pressed it into a slice of cheese at a state dinner, and been sent to bed.

The noon came in at the gallery windows, as it did at every window of the palace at every hour, and lay down the white floor in long bars, warm on his face as he passed through them.

At the end of the gallery was his father’s door. There was still no light under it.

He had stood outside it all his life with questions, and four days ago with his hand raised to knock, and had gone away again. He did not stop now. He knocked, twice, and did not wait for an answer, because no answer had come through that door to anybody since the morning his father walked off the throne, and opened it, and went in, and shut it behind him.

It was dark. The shutters were closed and barred, and the dark had the close stale stillness of a room nobody has opened in a long while, and it smelled of cold ink and old paper and the lilies in a vase on the desk that had died standing up, as lilies seemed to die everywhere in this palace when his mother was too unhappy to have them changed. The gold came up at his seams and lay out from him on the floor.

And for once his father was not at the window.

He was sitting at his desk. He had turned his chair round from the shuttered window to face into the room, towards the door, as if he had been waiting all those days for somebody to come in and had not known who it would be. He wore no mantle and no coat. He sat in his shirtsleeves, which Caleb had never once seen him in, with a hand on each arm of the chair, and in the faint gold of the seams Caleb could see his face from the front.

It was the face from the coins and the Throne Hall and the portrait in the Hall of Petitions, the public stillness, the blue eyes that he had not inherited, and it was as smooth as it had been on the morning of Caleb’s naming day, because the Solar House did not line or grey. Only it had stopped holding itself up. That was all. It looked like a mask that somebody has laid down on a table for a moment and will presently pick up again.

“Caleb,” said the King.

“Majesty.”

He laid the case on the desk, and the paper on the case, and stood back from it.

“The Council asks Your Majesty’s seal.”

His father looked at the paper for a while without touching it. Then he reached out and drew it to him across the desk, and unfolded it, and read it, in the gold of Caleb’s seams and the dark.

He read it once.

Caleb waited. He had waited all his life, at that desk, across that window, about the colour of a carpet or the length of a journey or whether a boy of six might go to the Vales. *Let me have a season. We shall see in the spring.* He stood in the dark of his father’s study and waited for the season to be asked.

It was not asked.

The King laid the paper flat on the desk. He opened the white case and took out the seal, and a stick of gold wax from the drawer, and held the end of the wax between his fingers, and waited. Then his fingers lit, faintly, with the old Solar glimmer that came and went in all of them at feasts and quarrels and birthdays, and Caleb thought it must be the first light his father had made since the day he walked off the throne. The wax softened in it and dropped onto the foot of the paper in a little gold pool, and his father pressed the sun into it and held it there, and lifted it off.

“It seems you have decided,” said the King.

“Yes.”

His father put the seal back in its case and closed the lid, and sat with his hand on it.

“Your signet was found at a vineyard in the Marches, among the dead.”

The room was very still.

“And I knew that you had never been there. And I sent you.”

He said it to Caleb’s face. He said it plainly, without any voice of the dais in it, as a man reads a line off a page that has lain a long time face down on his desk, and stopped.

Caleb’s hands had nothing to do, and he could think of nothing to give them.

He had forgiven his father for that unfinished sentence on the yacht with the ash in his mouth, and again on every night after, and had not known until a fae’s bells were laid out on a desk on the Coast what it was he had been forgiving.

*You let me stand there*, he thought. *You let me stand in the middle of my own floor and wait for it, and then you sent for the wine.*

“That is the rest of the sentence.”

“Yes.”

“I waited for it.”

“I know. I heard you wait.”

The dead lilies on the desk let fall a little rust onto the wood. Neither of them looked at it.

“Why did you never turn round?” Caleb asked.

The King was quiet a long time.

“So that nobody could argue with me,” he said.

He said it to his own hands on the white case, and did not look up.

“Your mother and I will go to the outer temples,” said the King, “when your sister has judged your brother. I have written it.” He moved one hand towards a sheet on the corner of the desk, in the long florid hand Caleb had last read turning in slow gilded circles on the panel of a yacht. “I am told that it is restful.”

“I understand that the privacy of a pilgrim is to be respected, in his season of reflection.”

His father looked up at him.

For a moment the mask lay on the table between them, and neither of them picked it up.

“I wrote that,” said the King.

“I know, Majesty. I read it on the yacht. I thought it very well drawn.”

He took the paper with the seal on it, and the case, and went to the door, and stopped there with his hand on the leaf.

“Shall I leave it open?”

His father looked at the door, and at the gallery beyond it, where the noon lay along the white boards in its long bars, as it had lain at every hour for a thousand years.

“Yes,” said the King.

Caleb left it open. The light came in from the gallery across the threshold and lay along the floor of the study in a long white bar, as far as the leg of the desk, and stopped there. His father did not turn his chair back to the window. He sat where he was, facing into the room, with the light on the floor at his feet, and Caleb went back up the gallery through the bars of noon with the seal under his arm and did not look round, because he found that he could not have borne to see whether his father was looking after him.

\* \* \*

The lords had sat down again while he was gone, and kept their hands in their laps, off the frost.

He laid the paper before the Keeper with the King’s gold gone hard at its foot, and the Keeper read the Article of the Sun once more, in its new words, in his voice like a pen cut fine and seldom used, and when he came to *son or daughter* the voice did not alter by a hair, which Caleb thought did him great credit.

“The Order has set aside the eldest child of His Majesty’s body,” said the Keeper. “The sun is asked of the next.” He turned towards the door, since the law required him to say it to somebody. “The High Paladin.”

Matilda took her shoulders off the wood.

She stood at the door in white mail, with the Highland road dried grey on her boots and her hand on the hilt of a sword that had *Again* cut on its guard, and looked down the length of the table at the eleven lords, and at the sun in the priest’s lap, as she looked at a picket line that had let something through.

“I am a sword,” said Matilda, “not a crown.”

She said it as she gave the Guard its orders of the day, flat and clipped, and it went down the hall and stopped against the King’s empty chair. Caleb saw that nobody at that table was fool enough to answer it.

She glanced at Caleb, once. “I have learned this evening that nobody in this hall knows how to ask a question. I do not know how either. I give orders.” She put her shoulders back against the door. “Ask Eleanor.”

The Keeper looked helplessly at the front bench.

“I should put it down on a bench somewhere,” said Cecily, “and lose it under an alembic, and somebody would stand a flask in it.” She took her wrist from her mouth. “Ask Eleanor.”

Every face at the table turned to its head.

Eleanor had not moved. She sat looking at the green cloth, and the lamps stood up tall and bright over her like the candles round a bier, which Caleb would have told her on any other night of their lives.

“You have not asked me,” she said, to the cloth.

Caleb left his chair at the foot of the table.

He walked the whole length of it, past the lords and the Keeper and the priest with the sun in his lap, and stopped beside her chair, because some things ought not to be asked down the length of a table.

“Eleanor. Will you?”

She looked up at him.

He had seen her look at a great many things. He had seen her look at sums and at ministers and at a lemon leaf going brown in her hand, and at a bill for candied violets with GOOSE in the pence, and at him across a garden with the light coming through the white roses. He had never seen her look at anything as she looked at him now, as if she had been handed a column she had been adding in her head since she was seven and had never once been allowed to write down.

She drew off her right glove, finger by finger. There was ink on the second finger, where there always was, where the pen sat.

“Yes,” said Eleanor.

The lamps went white from one end of the Hall of Petitions to the other.

She laid the glove on the frost beside her case, square, and spoke to the lords of the Council in the voice of a quarter’s returns that had come in better than anybody had dared to hope.

“Somebody in this family had better be able to add.”

\* \* \*

They crowned her at the fourth bell of the night, in the Hall of Petitions, by lamplight, under the wrong stars.

The Order of the Crowning said that the heir should kneel at the foot of the throne. It did not say which throne. The priest had read the Order every night for forty years against just such an hour, and he pointed this out, and every petitioner since the hall was built had knelt on the floor before the King’s chair to ask for things. Eleanor knelt there.

She knelt on one knee in the Chancellery’s blue, with her left hand over her heart and her ungloved right hand flat on the boards, with its inked finger. Caleb had seen Andrew kneel for the sun that morning in white and gold, in a column of noon, glazed with a borrowed hour of what he ought to be. Eleanor knelt in lamplight in the coat she had worn to the Chancellery, and looked at the floor in front of her as if it were a page that needed checking.

The blood stood on the step of the King’s chair, but there was only one step, and so they stood along it in a row, as the Order had never imagined, with Caleb at the end.

The high priest stood up off his stool with the sun in his two hands and lifted it over her head.

There was no blaze. There was no column of noon in the Hall of Petitions to pour into its slivers, and so they only took the lamplight and held it, gold on gold, as a ring holds the firelight on the hand of a woman sewing late. To Caleb it looked rather like what it was, an old circlet that a great many people had worn, held up by a priest whose arms had held it most of a day and were beginning to tremble with great dignity.

“Caleb of the Solar House,” said the priest. “Youngest of the blood. Will you stand under this sun?”

He had been asked it once already that day.

“Yes,” said Caleb.

“Cecily of the Solar House. Will you stand under this sun?”

“Yes,” said Cecily, with her hair loose to her waist and both her hands holding each other in front of her, too hard.

“Matilda of the Solar House.”

“Yes.”

The priest drew breath, and his arms shook, and he held on.

“Andrew of the Solar House. Eldest of the blood.”

Nobody answered.

The Order kept the sun aloft until every one of the blood had answered. It did not say what was to be done if one of the blood was shut in his rooms with Matilda’s Guard on the door, and the priest looked at the door, and so did the lords, and the dwarf of the Holds lifted his pen and waited.

Then the door opened.

His mother came in first, in white, with her hand under the arm of the man who came in beside her, and two of Matilda’s Guard came after them and stood inside the door looking hard at nothing.

Andrew had put on his grey, and buttoned the collar high, and above it the marks of four long fingers and a thumb stood out dark on his throat, where Arian had held him.

It was the travelling grey he had worn on every road since the Vales, and it hung on him now as a sail hangs in a calm. The gilt had gone off his face at noon and nothing had come to take its place. He was grey under the grey, and thin, and his hands at his sides shook from the wrist down, finely and without stopping, as a leaf shakes on a twig in a wind nobody else can feel. Nine years of mornings had gone out of him in the Throne Hall all at once, and taken with them whatever they had been holding up.

He saw his sister kneeling under the sun at the end of the hall, and nothing else.

“Andrew of the Solar House,” said the priest again, faintly. “Will you stand under this sun?”

Andrew’s mouth moved. Nothing came. He stood with his mother’s hand under his arm, and his own shaking at his side, and Caleb watched him try twice, as a man tries a door in the dark that he knows is not locked, and on the third time it opened.

“Yes,” said Andrew, in a voice the Sovereign’s hand had left him only half of. “Wherever she puts me.”

The priest set the sun on Eleanor’s head.

He did it with both hands and enormous care, and then sat down on his stool without asking anybody’s leave. Eleanor knelt under the sun for one breath, quite still. Then she stood up.

The lamps were white already. It was the stars that went.

They lit on the ceiling all together, every gold-leaf star of them, every wrong one, white as the lamps and whiter, so that the painted night over the Council of the Throne blazed as no night over the Citadel had ever blazed, and the lords stood up from the table without anybody telling them to, and the Guard at the door forgot to look at nothing.

Queen Eleanor of Aloria looked down the hall at her brother in his grey.

“You will keep your rooms until I have heard the inquiry. Then I shall judge you.” Her voice was the Chancellery voice, cold and clear. “I shall not ask for a season.”

“No,” Andrew answered. “You never would.”

\* \* \*

He turned to go as he had come, on their mother’s arm, with the two Guard behind, and he did not look at the end of the step where Caleb stood. At the door he stopped, and looked down at his own two hands as he had looked at them in the west doors that morning, as if they belonged to somebody he had been told about. Then their mother said his name, low, and he went out between the Guard.

She did not go with him. She stood in the door with her hand still lifted where his arm had been, and turned, and Caleb saw her look past all of them to the shuttered end of the hall, where the lamps had all leaned the same way since the second bell and were leaning still.

She walked the whole length of the Hall of Petitions in her white, her hands folded before her, through the light of her daughter’s lamps and out of it, and stopped where the last of it gave out, as a woman might stop at the edge of a pond at night to see what was in it. Arian stood in the dark some paces beyond her. He bowed, the depth owed by the Sovereign of the Coast to a Queen of Aloria, to the hair.

“I worked the sunburst on that cloak,” said his mother. “There is a drop of my blood in every ray of it.”

“Yes, Majesty.”

“I am told that I cannot thank the woman who washed it.”

“No, Majesty.”

His mother stood at the light’s edge, and the frost of him came out across the floor towards the hem of her white gown and stopped a finger’s breadth from it, as if it had been given orders.

“Then I shall thank whoever it was she taught to keep things.”

She did not curtsey. Queens of Aloria did not curtsey to anybody. She inclined her head to him, once, the depth a Queen owes the head of a house, and then a little lower, which nobody had taught her, and turned, and came back up the hall through the white.

At the end of the step she stopped beside Caleb.

“Will you write to me?” she asked. “At the temple.”

He tried to answer it as a physician would, and then as a son, and could keep the two apart no better than he had five days ago.

“You may write to me.”

“You need not answer.”

“I know. I shall read them, Mother.” He looked at her. “I shall read them twice.”

His mother held his look a long moment, with his own eyes, and he saw her hear it, both halves of it, the one that cut and the one that did not, and keep them both.

Then she went out after her son, and the door closed behind her.

Eleanor was looking at the door.

She stood under the sun with the stars still burning white over her, every wrong one, and she had taken up her glove from the frost and begun to put it on again, and could not get the second finger in, where the ink was. Caleb watched her try three times.

She would do it. She would hear the inquiry under a black sun with the Holds writing it down, and add the column, and give the sum in the right order, cold and clear, and carry it at the head of the first page of her reign for the rest of her life without once telling anybody that her brothers had written it there. He had been the one wronged. He could leave it to her and go up the hill tomorrow with his hand in the Sovereign’s, and never have to look at it. It would be the easiest thing he had ever done, and the most cowardly, and she would let him.

He took the glove out of her hand and held it open, as their nurse had held gloves open for every one of them at the top of the nursery stairs, and she put her hand into it.

“Majesty. Let me go to him first.”

The lamps over her went still.

“It is mine to do.”

“It is yours to say. It is mine to have asked him first. It was my wrist, and my road, and my name they found in the vineyard.” He settled the glove at her wrist. “You have been Queen for a quarter of a bell. The first page of your book ought not to be in his hand and yours, and nobody else’s.”

She looked at him a long while, as she looked at a figure that had come out right by a method she would never have used.

“Tilda,” said the Queen of Aloria, without turning her head. “The east tower. Let him up.”

At the far end of the hall the dark by the shuttered windows had not moved, and Caleb did not raise his voice.

“Alone.”

“I will be at the foot of the stair,” Arian answered.

“Of course you will.”

The lamp-men’s stair went on through the thickness of the palace wall long after it had passed his rooms, down and round and down again, and Caleb went down it with a lamp from the Hall of Petitions in his hand, because his seams had given the Throne Hall most of what they had and were keeping the rest for themselves. Arian came behind him. At every turn there was a niche for a lantern and nothing in it. At the bottom, where the stair let out through a panel into the foot of the east tower, there was one more, and Caleb set the lamp in it.

Beyond the panel the stair of the east tower went up through the noon. It came in at every arrow-slit in a white blade and lay across the treads one above another, so that the whole narrow throat of the stair was laddered with light from the bottom to the top. Arian stopped at the foot of it, where the first blade lay across the third tread, and stood in the dark of the turning with the frost coming out on his shoulders, and looked up the ladder that he could not climb.

“I am going to forgive him,” said Caleb.

Arian’s hand closed on his wrist. It closed on the left, over the ring of scorched silk, and held it.

The frost came off him. It went out across the stones of the turning and up the wall to the niche, and the lamp flame lay down on its wick under it as a dog lies down under a lifted hand, and shrank, and went blue. His shadow drew in about his boots, close and black, as a man gathers in his cloak before he steps out into weather.

“He has not asked you.”

The flame went out.

There was the white of the noon on the third tread, and the gold of Caleb’s seams lying faint on the stones, and between them the dark, with Arian in it.

“He has not begged you.” The frost came after it in a breath. “He told your father’s court that he would do it again.”

Caleb left his wrist where it was.

“He would. He was buying my life when he said it. And he will not ask me for anything, Arian, not tonight and not before the Holds, whatever it costs him.” The gold at his seams had begun to come up as he spoke, as it did when he was sure of a thing, and it lay out across the stones to the toe of Arian’s boot. “It is not pride, whatever the Throne Hall thinks it saw. He believes he has no right.”

The hand on his wrist did not move.

“Since the day he first saw me, my brother has loved me more than anyone in the world. But he was raised for the crown, Arian, and he has known nothing all his life but obedience to it. He let them take me because the crown had decided.” His voice had begun to go, and he let it. “I know my brother. He will never ask my forgiveness, because he thinks he does not deserve it.”

“He does not,” said Arian.

It came out harsh, and the frost came with it, a hand’s breadth further up the wall.

The corner of Caleb’s mouth curled up a little, sadly.

“He is my only brother,” he said. “Who else could deserve it more?”

The frost stopped where it was, on the wall below the niche. Then it began, slowly, to go back, off the wall and across the flags, as the tide goes back off a causeway and leaves the stones to be walked on. In the niche the wick caught again of its own accord, blue and then yellow, and stood up in its glass. The shadow that had drawn close about Arian’s boots let go of them and lay out across the turning to the foot of the stair, and leaned a little way up the first two treads after Caleb, as far as the light would let it.

The hand on his wrist opened. Arian let out a long breath, which he had no need of, and had not taken in a great many years.

“Go,” he said.

Caleb went up the stair without him.

The shutters were open a hand’s width, as they had always been. The lamp burned on the long table, useless, and in the oak where the light lay nearest the two dozen burnt hands lay one over another, black and glossy. There was no glass of water on it and no twist of white paper. Matilda’s Guard had been thorough.

Andrew sat beside the prints in his grey, writing.

He held the pen in his right hand and his right wrist in his left, and still the line went across the page as a man goes across ice that has begun to sing under him, a little at a time and with his weight held back. He finished the line before he looked up. It took him a long while.

“Thirty,” he said, to the page. “There are eleven more. I have them in the order in which the Coast’s Watch found them. I have not liked to change it.” He laid the pen down, and the shaking went on in his hand without it. “Sit down, Caleb.”

Five days ago he had not sat. He sat now, across the table, on the far side of the blade of noon, and the light lay between them on the boards and touched neither of them.

Andrew’s eyes had gone to his left wrist, to the black silk scorched round in a ring the width of four fingers and a thumb, and the skin under it white and whole.

“Who saw to it?”

Caleb did not answer, and did not need to. Andrew looked at the scorch a while longer. Then he laid his hand flat over the burnt prints in the oak, and the wood under it did not smoke. There was nothing left in him to smoke it.

“I had things to say,” Caleb admitted. “I said them over all the way up your stair, and they were very good on the stair. I seem to have lost the order.”

“Say them out of it.”

“I have forgiven you.”

It went down his brother from the face to the hands, and for the space of a breath the hands were still.

“I do not know when. Before I knew what for, I think. Somewhere on the Coast, in the dark, before I had read a line of Eleanor’s. I tried to take it back after the Fault, and again today when your letter was read out, and again just now on your stair, and it will not come back. It is a dreadful habit. Father has lived on it for years.” He looked at the prints, because he could not look at the face above them and keep his voice. “And I love you. I have not been able to stop that either, though it would have been a great deal more convenient this week.”

Andrew’s mouth moved. Nothing came out of it. His hand had gone a little way across the oak towards Caleb’s, and there it stopped, as a child’s hand stops at the edge of a dish that has been set out for the guests.

Caleb reached across and took it.

At the touch Andrew’s eyes went red at the rims, all at once, as the sky over the Vales goes red at its rim when the sun is under it, and Caleb read in them everything his brother could not say.

“I will be with you.”

“But you must be punished,” said Caleb.

“Yes.”

It came at once, as a man answers a knock he has sat up all night to hear.

“Not because I am angry. I am, a little, at the edges, as a burn is angry at the edges after the middle has been seen to.” He made himself look up. “At the Vales, when a bone has knitted crooked, they break it and set it again. It is the cruellest thing they teach, and the kindest, and nobody has ever thanked them for it at the time. I have done it four times, and been sick behind the stables after every one.” His voice had begun to go, and he went on anyway. “If nobody punishes you, you will do it yourself, every morning, at this table, a line at a time, for as long as you live. That is not mercy. It is only —”

He could not finish it. He sat with the end of it in his mouth, and could not for anything in the world put it on the table between them.

“Another glass,” said Andrew. “Yes. I know.”

“Punishment is a mercy. It is the only one I know of that ends.”

Far below in the courtyard a halberd rang once on the flags, and stopped.

“So I came to ask you something. If you were King.” He had to begin again. “If the sun had stayed on your head this morning, and somebody were brought before you who had done what has been done. What would you do?”

Andrew looked down at the column in front of him, thirty names in the square hand of the Treasury’s returns with not one struck out, and laid his shaking hands on either side of it, as he had laid them on either side of a speech five days ago. When he spoke there was no dais in his voice anywhere.

“I should not need to do anything. He would ask it of me. He would ask to be put to death.”

The blade of noon lay on the boards between them, and did not move.

“I have done the sum, Caleb. Nine years at this table, and it does not come out any other way, however I carry the figures. There is nothing at the foot of forty-one. It only sits there, and it will be sitting there when I am dead.” His hands had begun to shake again. “And that was only what I did to strangers. When Father named you, I stood in his study with my hands behind my back and told him it need only be for a season. I wrote your road down for a Caruso and sealed it in my own wax. And today I put my hand on you.”

He looked at Caleb’s wrist at last.

“I burned you,” said Andrew. “You. I would have put my arm in the fire to the shoulder to keep it off one hair of your head, and I put my hand on you before the whole court, and did not even feel it go.” His voice had thinned to a thread, and it did not break. “There is no atoning for that. I deserve it, Caleb. I have deserved it since the cellar stair.”

Caleb reached for something to say against it.

He went through everything he had, the Vales and the Coast and twenty-eight years of being the one in the family who could talk anybody round, and all of it was true, and not one word of it was against it. The only other thing left was the true thing that would have finished it, and he had never in his life been able to say a thing because it was true if it would hurt. He could not learn it tonight, for his brother.

He took his brother’s other hand instead, across the blade of noon, and held them both. They were cold to the fingertips, and they shook in Caleb’s palms as a bird shakes that has flown against a window and been picked up off the sill.

“I do not want you to die.”

It was the only thing he could find that was true and not cruel, and it was not enough, and they both knew it.

“I know. You never wanted anything to.” Something happened to Andrew’s mouth. “You cried a week over the carp in the fountain court when the heron had them. You held a funeral. You made me read the service.”

“You read it beautifully.”

“I was practising for the dais.” He looked down at their hands on the scorched oak. “I shall tell Eleanor myself, tomorrow, before the Holds. She ought not to have to say it first.” His eyes went to the column at his elbow. “They will be replanting the vineyard. I should have liked to be of use, once, to something I burned.”

“I will ask the Consigliere whether he will let you go under the new rows.”

“He will refuse.”

“Then I will ask him twice.”

Andrew laughed, hardly a sound, a breath through the nose, and for a moment his face was almost as it had been.

“Do you remember your bath? Not Cecily’s flask. The first. You were five. I came up to say goodnight, and the nursery bathroom was gold from wall to wall, and the steam was lit, and you were standing up in the middle of it with your arms out, shouting at me to look, because you were swimming in the sun. I lifted you out and carried you to bed in the towel, dripping, and you talked about the sun the whole way, and the gold came off on my coat.” He looked at his hands in Caleb’s. “It never washed out. I have the coat still. I never could think why I kept it.” His thumb moved once on Caleb’s knuckle. “You were heavy. I did not drop you.”

“No,” Caleb murmured. “You did not drop me.”

They sat with that a while.

“I am being selfish,” said Andrew, “and I am hurting you, to the very end. But you asked me, and so I will ask you for one thing, the last.” He did not look away. “Let it be you who takes my life. It must be no other.”

Caleb’s hand trembled in his brother’s.

For a long while he said nothing at all. He only looked at him, and his eyes filled and ran over, and he let them. Then his fingers tightened round Andrew’s.

“You have my word,” he said.

He knew, as he said it, that it was the only way either of them would ever be at peace.

“Open the shutters,” said Andrew. “All of them. I have kept them a hand’s width since I was sixteen. I should like to see it once, the whole of it, before it goes out.”

Caleb went round the room and threw them back one after another, and the bars came up out of their brackets with a groan of old iron, and the noon of the Citadel came into the east tower from every side at once. It lay on the brown papers and the black hands in the oak, and on the man sitting among them in his grey with his face turned up to it. It did not make him gold. He looked at it as a man looks at a coast from the deck of the ship that is taking him away from it.

“I will be with you tomorrow,” Caleb promised. “From the first to the last. You will not have to look round for me.”

Andrew did not look away from the window. The corner of his mouth went up, and stayed up, terribly.

“I know,” he breathed. “You never could keep away from anything that was hurting.”

He stood at the door with his hand on the leaf and could think of nothing that would let him go through it, and so he went through it without. Behind him, in the full noon, his brother had taken up the pen again and was holding his wrist to steady it.

“Thirty-one,” said Andrew, to the page.

\* \* \*

He came down through the blades of noon, and at the bottom the dark was waiting with frost in it, and he walked into it as a man walks into a wall he has not seen in the night.

Arian’s arms closed round him. Caleb put his face into the black silk, and under his cheek the heart that kept his time was going hard and quick and keeping nobody’s.

“He asked —” said Caleb, into the silk, and could not say the rest of it here either.

“I heard.”

He had heard all of it, then, from the foot of the stair, as he heard a heart through a wall. He offered no opinion of it and no sorrow, and Caleb would not have wanted him to lie. But the frost had come up the stair behind him as far as the third tread, where the first blade of noon lay, and stopped there with its edge against the light, as a man stands at a door he has not been asked through. He stood in the dark of the turning with the ladder of noon going up over their heads, and held on.

Later, at the door of the Chancellery, Caleb tried to tell his sister, and found that he could not say it to her either. He stood in the doorway with his mouth open on it. Eleanor looked at his face, and then she said it for him, plainly, in four words, as she said a sum aloud so that the clerks could write it down, and he nodded, and she shut the door, and under it the lamps went out.

\* \* \*

It was still dark behind his shutters when the first bell of the day began.

He had not tried to sleep. He had lain on his bed in his boots, on the far side from the hollow, with the grey cloak folded inside his coat against his heart, in the ring’s old place, and counted to three hundred while the other heart kept his count from a few paces off in his own chair, and at three hundred he had gone on counting, because if he stopped he would have to think, and the other heart had gone on with him past a thousand without once asking why. Enzo sat in the window seat. Caleb had not supposed that he slept.

The bell rang first from the tower of the Throne Hall, as it had rung every morning of Caleb’s life, and the next tower took it up a breath behind and a note lower, and the next, down the terraces of the Citadel, as he had heard them go down in his sleep for twenty-eight years without once listening.

He listened now.

At the cracks of the shutters, the noon was going out.

The white threads that had lain across his carpet at every hour since he could remember, laid there by the spires as faithfully as a maid lays a fire, began to thin. They went from white to the grey-white of skimmed milk, and from that to the faint sheen of a pearl, and lay on the carpet a moment longer like the ghosts of threads, and were gone. And then he felt it, in the nape of his neck and the palms of his hands, as one feels a bell stop that has rung so long one has stopped hearing it, and the silence where it was is louder than the bell.

The noon of the Citadel had gone out.

In the east tower, with every shutter thrown back, his brother would have watched it go, all of it, to the last thread.

On the walls, a long way down, the watch called the hour. It called the first half as it had called it every morning since there had been a wall to call it from. *First bell.* Then there was a pause, a long one, the pause of a young man on a cold wall reading the rest off a card by a light that had gone strange, because it was a thing no watchman under forty had ever had occasion to say aloud.

*And all’s well.*

Then, all down the white hill below the palace, the shutters began to open.

They went as they went at night, house by house, with a soft booming, but backwards, as a long table of guests all push back their chairs at once when a toast is called. Somewhere a child was asking, high and clear and furious, why it was dark when it was morning, and being told.

Caleb got up off the bed and went to the first shutter, and lifted the bar out of its bracket, and threw it back.

The noon did not come in.

What came in was the light of every nursery tale of the Long Eclipse, the light that Matilda had carried him down through at sixteen and that he had slept through at eight, pearl and grey and silver, coming from nowhere and falling nowhere. He threw back the next shutter, and the next, and the long room filled with it, and nothing in the room threw a shadow, not the harpsichord nor the chair nor the coat on the floor that was still sitting up. There was nowhere left for a shadow to fall. Every shadow in the Citadel had been let out of its corner at once and had run together over everything, as the tide runs together over the sandbars, until there was no edge to any of them, and the whole room stood in the one shadow, and so did the city, and so did the sky. His own gold rose at his seams and was the warmest-coloured thing in the room.

Over the spires of the Citadel, in a sky that was not white, hung the sun.

It was black, and it hung over the great tower of the Throne Hall with a thin white fire crawling round its rim, as fire runs along the edge of a letter laid on the coals. Under it the great crystals on every spire of the Citadel, which had burned noon at every hour since before there was a Solar House to own them, had gone as dull as river pebbles left to dry on a sill.

And round the black sun, in the deep grey of the sky, the brightest of the stars had come out to see what had happened, and every one of them was in a different place from the ceiling of the Hall of Petitions.

He heard the chair behind him.

Arian came down the long room and stopped at Caleb’s shoulder, at the open window, where for twenty-eight years the noon had come in at that hour as punctually as breakfast. He laid his right hand, the one with the heavy gold on it, on the stone of the sill, in the open.

Nothing happened.

Frost spread from his fingers across the white stone and lay there, and did not smoke. The skin of his hand stayed white. There was no shadow under it on the sill, since the sill itself lay in shadow, and on this one day in twenty years there was nothing overhead that could burn him.

He stepped out onto the balcony.

Caleb went with him. The air was cold, as the Citadel’s air was never cold, and smelled of stone and of hot bread from somewhere down the hill, because the bakers of the Citadel had evidently decided that whatever the sky did, people would want breakfast. Below them the white city went down its hill in the pearl with every shutter open, and people were coming out onto the steps and the flat white roofs in their nightcaps and their shawls to stand in their own streets at morning and look at a sky they had never seen.

They glimmered faintly all down the hill, a little gold at the wrists and the throat of every elf in the city, as the cottages of the Highland March glimmer on a winter night when every house has left one candle in the window for somebody still on the road.

And on the balcony of the youngest prince’s rooms, at the top of all of it, the Sovereign of the Coast stood under the Citadel’s sky at the first bell of the day with nothing over his head at all.

He was not looking at the city. He was looking at the white stone at his feet, where his shadow should have lain.

“Where is it?” Caleb asked.

“Everywhere.”

Caleb looked, and saw that it was. It was the grey on the stone of the balcony and the grey on the roofs below and the grey that the whole white hill had gone down into, and it lay round Arian as evenly as the light did, fastened to nothing, with nowhere in the whole of the world to lean.

“Arian,” said Enzo, from the window.

He had come to the open shutter and no further. He stood in it with his hands clasped at his back and the frost come out on the shoulders of his dove grey, and for once his face was not composed at all.

“At Old Meridian your aunt never looked up. She was looking at the ground for you.”

Arian did not move.

“You are found,” said Enzo. “Look up.”

Arian lifted his head.

It came up slowly, as it had come up under a child’s cloak in the Throne Hall, and he looked up past the dull spires into the grey, at the black sun with its rim of white fire hung over the city of his enemies, and Caleb stood beside him in the pearl with the gold coming off his seams and looked up too, for a while, at the sky of his own city, which had never before had anything in it to look at. Then he found that there was something he would rather look at, and looked at Arian’s face.

It was turned up to the sun. Arian had stood under it once before, at twelve, and spent the whole of it on his back among the roses of a glasshouse, looking at a boy. The frost lay on his lashes white and fine and unmelting, and his lips had parted. And under Caleb’s breastbone, in time with his own, unhurried, the other heart went on keeping the count of a boy of eight.

“You are not taking notes,” said Arian, without looking down.

“You are here,” Caleb protested. “You can see it.”

“All the same.”

“Then you shall have them.” He did not take his eyes off the frost on Arian’s lashes. “It is grey. Not the grey of your uncle’s coat. The grey of the ash in a grate in the morning, with something still warm under it that you cannot see. Nothing has a shadow, and yours has gone out over the whole city, and I cannot find the edge of it. The sun is black, with a rim of fire round it like the brandy round a pudding at the feast of First Light, and the crystals have gone out, and there are eleven stars, and the Hall of Petitions has every one of them wrong.”

He stopped.

“And there is a man on my balcony who has never stood in the open air of my city before, and he has his face turned up to it, and has forgotten to close his mouth, like a child at a window on the first night of snow.”

Arian looked down at him.

“That last is not a note on the sun.”

“No. It is the only one worth writing down.”

\* \* \*

The *Spaarzaamheid* stood on the Guard’s field below the palace walls among the Radiant Guard’s white yachts, black with tar from keel to rail, as plainly not one of them as a rook in a dovecote. Eleanor had sent down to the wharves for her before she knelt, and paid Van Houten’s rate without asking what it was, which Maartje Bosboom said had never been done to Van Houten in four generations.

Two casks stood at the foot of the gangway on the white flags, lids off.

“Scrubbed,” said Maartje. She looked at Arian standing out in the open on the Guard’s field of the Citadel, with frost on him and the same grey round his boots as round everybody’s, and something went across her face that she had plainly not given it leave to do. “Three times is a tradition.”

“Not today.”

“Not today, Sovereign?”

Arian looked past her, up the gangway, at the deck with nothing over it.

Maartje looked from him to the black sun over the spires, and to the Consigliere a few paces off, who was already considering the deck with the air of a man inspecting a room he means to take for a season. She opened her ledger, wrote a line, scored it out, and wrote it a second time.

“Van Houten never enters a remark,” she said, to the page. Her voice was not quite steady. “Mind the rail. It’s new.”

The Guard’s yachts were lifting one after another into the grey, with their sunbursts gone dull on their prows for want of a noon to drink. Matilda’s went first. There was a man in grey in its stern between two of the Guard, with a woman in white beside him, holding his arm, and Matilda was at its rail not looking down, because she never did, and then, at the last moment before the hull took her out of sight, looking down. Cecily went up after her with her hair down and a satchel clutched to her chest as if somebody might take it off her.

At the head of the field the Queen of Aloria stood on the deck of her own yacht with her despatch case under her arm and a page held up before her, as if she were reading, because the Marches were some bells off yet and she did not intend to waste them. She had not turned the page since Caleb came onto the field.

And at the edge of the field, by a white yacht of the Guard with its gangway down, stood four lycans of the Highland March.

They had their shawls on and their best boots and a bundle each, and the largest of them had a grey blanket rolled under his arm.

Paul left them and came across the field. He stopped two paces off, as he had stopped behind him on every stair in that palace for fourteen years, and looked at Caleb, and then at the man standing in the open beside him, and then at Caleb again, as if to make certain that a dream had followed him into the morning.

“The Paladin’s sending us home, Highness. To the March. On that.”

“Good.”

“My mother says I’m to sleep under the roof she’s under. For a bit.”

“Your mother,” Caleb teased, “has come a very long way to cough at you, and you should let her.”

Paul’s mouth moved. Then it stopped, and Caleb saw the question come up into his face and stand at the back of it, as Paul’s questions had stood there for eleven weeks without being asked.

“Highness. Will there be a door? After.” His big hand closed on the blanket. “I’m good at doors.”

Caleb looked at him.

He had carried two words once in the lining of a coat, beside his heart, across the Coast and through the Marches and down under a mountain, against the day he would know whether they were true. They had stopped being true of one man, and he had burned them at a dwarf’s lamp. They were true of this one. They had never been meant for the end of a sentence.

“Not yet,” said Caleb.

Paul stood very still. Behind him, at the gangway of the Guard’s yacht, his mother had put her two hands together before her mouth, cupped, and spoken into them words the wind did not carry, and opened them.

“That’s not a no, Paul,” she called across the field, in the voice of a woman who has called sons in off hillsides at dusk her whole life.

“No,” Caleb answered. “It is not.”

Paul looked at him a moment longer. Then he turned and went back across the field to his mother, and Caleb let him go without calling after him.

\* \* \*

The *Spaarzaamheid* lifted at the second bell.

She went up off the field slowly, as broad-bellied Lowmere yachts go up, with Maartje on the ladder shouting at somebody in Lowish, and the Citadel went down beneath her, the palace and the white terraces and the open shutters and the people standing in their own streets in their nightcaps, all of it grey and pearl and faintly gold.

Arian stood at the rail on the open deck with his face turned up.

He had not looked down at the city once. He stood with both hands on the new rail and the frost white on his knuckles and his hair, in a wind off the spires colder than any that had ever blown over them, and looked at the sun.

Caleb stood beside him, his own hand on the rail a little way from Arian’s, not touching.

He could feel the cloak against his heart under the black silk, and the flask of his sister’s sunlight in his pocket beside the violets, which was warm still, all through, in a morning that had put out every crystal in the Citadel, because it was the only thing she had made that year that did what she had said it would. He could feel the other heart, close enough to touch, keeping his time. Somewhere ahead of them in the grey, on a white yacht with its sunburst gone dull, his brother was going south to the Marches too, and Caleb did not doubt that his face was turned up to the same sun.

Arian’s hand moved on the rail. It came across the little space between them on the new wood and stopped short of his, as it had stopped every night at a garden sill.

“May I?” said Arian.

He had asked it from a chair in a study with the whole width of a room between them, and been told no, and he had asked it in a bed hung with frost and been told yes, with a word after it, and now he was asking it in the open air over the city of the Solar House, under a sun that could not touch him, with every window in the Citadel below them full of faces turned up to see a vampire stand in the sky.

“You need not ask. Not today. Not in the open.”

“I know,” Arian murmured. “I should like to.”

“Yes,” said Caleb.

Arian’s hand closed over his on the rail, cold and certain, with the heavy gold of the Sovereign’s ring cold against Caleb’s knuckle and the black iron of his own warm under Arian’s palm, and the *Spaarzaamheid* turned her tarred stern on the white city and its open windows and went south, under the black sun, towards the Marches.

**Author’s note**

A quick word on the succession, for anyone wondering how the crown went from Andrew to Eleanor.

Aloria followed male-preference primogeniture: the crown went to the eldest son, and daughters came after every son, however much older they were. With Andrew set aside, that made Caleb, the youngest of five, next in line, ahead of all three of his older sisters.

Caleb was never raised for the throne. As the spare prince he has spent his whole life free to go where he likes, read what he likes, and lose at the court’s games on purpose, and he has no wish to carry the weight of a kingdom. So he refuses the crown and changes the law to absolute primogeniture, under which the eldest child inherits whatever their sex (much as the UK’s Succession to the Crown Act 2013 did for the British throne). That makes Matilda next. Matilda, a sword and not a crown, refuses in her turn, and the crown passes to Eleanor.

And Arian, being Arian, makes sure the Council of the Throne carries out Caleb’s order, with the reminder that the Coast will renew the Accord with the crown Caleb names, or with none at all.
