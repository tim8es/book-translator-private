## Chapter 23: Chapter Twenty One - The New Eclipse

*At the height, when the sun is black and the whole world lies in its shadow, so that nothing under it throws one of its own, let the two stand in the open with nothing over their heads, and let each be asked, by one of the other’s house, what he brings that the other has not. They are wed by neither the light nor the dark, for at that hour the one cannot be told from the other. They are wed by what they answer.*

— From *The Rite of the Black Sun*, as it was kept at Old Meridian before the realms were sundered, and written down by the Holds, who were asked to keep it

\* \* \*

The Marches came up out of the grey a little after the fourth bell, and Caleb, who had crossed them twice by carriage and once asleep in a sling of white silk, found that he had never seen them from the air.

He looked now. They lay under the black sun fold on fold, like the patches of a counterpane that three generations have mended with whatever cloth came to hand, the grey-green of the high sheep runs and the brown of turned earth and the dark green of beech woods in full leaf, stitched across from hill to hill with dry-stone walls. The light lay over all of it pearl and even and from nowhere, and it had no hour in it. Nothing had a shadow of its own. The whole of the Marches lay inside one, from rim to rim of the world, and there was nowhere left in it for any other to fall.

The sheep on the slopes stood about on nothing at all, as sheep stand in the margin of an old herbal, and a gnome trading post went by beneath the keel with its orange and white stripes gone the colour of barley sugar sucked half away, and its weathercock pointing at nothing, because no wind had told it anything since the first bell. Along the ridges the pylons of the Holds marched south with their light-lines slack between them and the crystals in their iron crowns gone dull and brown, like the eyes of a doll left out in the rain.

And over all of it hung the sun.

It had climbed since the Citadel and hung black over the hills with its rim alight, and round it the stars had come out, eleven of them and then a twelfth, low in the south, that Caleb had not counted from his balcony and that had evidently come late, as somebody always does.

Arian stood at the rail with both hands on it, as he had stood since the Citadel. The wind had put frost into his hair, and the one lock that had defied every barber on the Coast had come forward over his brow, and he had let the wind argue with it all the way south. His face was turned up. Further down the rail Enzo’s was turned up too, and the two of them had stood so for some bells now, like a pair of herons on a weir, and neither, Caleb thought, had blinked as often as a man should.

The *Spaarzaamheid* came over the last ridge and the ground fell away beneath her into a long valley, and the valley was full of mist.

It lay in the bottom of Old Meridian as the steam lies in a laundry copper when the lid comes off, white and heavy and slow to stir, and the black sun lit it from no direction, so that it glowed all through like the inside of a lantern made of horn. Out of it stood the city. Its arches came up white through the mist, and its long white streets ran up the flanks of the valley between the ghosts of walls, and every stone of it had been worn by the weather to the colour of a bone button, and the yacht went down over it all as quietly as a leaf goes down over a pond.

The courts of the world had come before them.

The Solar Throne had pitched on the far slope, where it had pitched twenty years ago and every twenty before that, and its white pavilions climbed the hill in rows with a sunburst on every ridgepole, all of them gone the tired yellow of a brass knocker on a house where nobody answers. On the near slope, low and long, stood the black silk of the Coast.

The Holds had pitched in the old market at the bottom, in iron, measured to the inch, and the gnomes of Lowmere in stripes beside them, and the fae had pitched nothing and were sitting along the tops of the arches in their feathers like a row of finches on a washing line, and somewhere up the valley a lycan cookfire was going, and the smoke of it went straight up into the grey without a breath of wind to lean on and stood there, a thin brown thread, as if the valley had been tacked to the sky at that point and somebody meant to come back and sew it properly.

Somewhere among the white pavilions, under Matilda’s Guard, his brother had come down an hour before them. Caleb looked at the tents a long while, and could not tell which.

And at the top of the near hill, alone, in its wilderness of briars, stood the glasshouse.

It caught nothing today. There was nothing for it to catch. Its ribs stood up out of the briars thin and black and empty against the grey, with the black sun hanging over them, and the few old panes left in the iron hung dull as the scales of a fish a day out of the water.

Arian was looking at it.

“It is smaller.”

They were the first words he had spoken since the Citadel.

“You were twelve,” said Caleb.

“I was lying down.”

Maartje brought her down on the yacht field at the mouth of the valley, among the white yachts of the Guard, with a great deal of shouting in Lowish and a bump that she entered in her ledger before the gangway was out.

The Coast was waiting on the grass.

They had come up from the strait with the Nine, as the sergeant had promised they would, forty lycans in Conti black with their fists on their hearts, and before them the Nine themselves. The Nine had stood on grass in the daytime once in every twenty years for as long as there had been a Summit, and it had not yet become a habit. They stood on it now in black and smoke-grey and plum, with their faces turned up and their hands a little out from their sides, as people stand who have come out of a house in their stockings and do not yet trust the ground. One of them had taken off his gloves and was looking at the backs of his hands as if they had been lent to him.

Between two of the sergeant’s lycans, in mulberry velvet gone shabby at the cuffs from a fortnight on a cellar stool, stood the lord of the inland vineyards, and he was not looking at the sky. He was looking at the gangway.

The sergeant came forward three paces and stopped, and his fist went to his heart, and stayed there a breath longer than the salute required.

“Forty, Sovereign. They’re here. They’ve stayed.”

“The house will pay them for it, Sergeant.”

The sergeant looked at the Sovereign of the Coast standing at the top of a gangway in the open daylight of the Marches with frost in his hair and nothing at all at his feet, and whatever he had meant to say next went away somewhere, and he said instead, quite loudly, to nobody, “Well,” and looked at the grass.

Somebody was coming through the lycans.

She came through them as she came through everything, as a loaded barrow comes through a market crowd, with a basket on one arm and her sleeves pushed up and her grey hair coming out of its pins in the wind of the yacht, and she did not run, because she had run three times that spring and said it was enough for anybody. She walked fast instead. She walked straight past the Sovereign of the Coast at the foot of the gangway, and set her basket down on the grass, and took Caleb’s face in her two hands.

Her hands smelled of bread and woodsmoke and the linen press. She turned his face to the left, and to the right, as she turned a loaf to see whether it was done underneath.

“*Malyshka*,” said Vesna.

“I ate the scones. All three. They were the best you have ever made.”

“They were like slates off a roof.” Her thumbs moved once on his cheekbones, where the salt had dried, and she did not ask. “I know what it means.”

She let him go and looked at him properly, from his collar to his boots, and her eyes stopped on the breast of his coat and stayed there.

He had cut the stitches in the Throne Hall with his sister’s knife and buttoned the coat over the cut. But Vesna had sewn that lining by the light of the linen room, and she looked at the way the crescent sat over his heart and knew at once that something had gone out from behind it.

Then her eyes went past him, up the gangway, to the right hand of the Sovereign of the Coast, where it lay on the rail with three rings of heavy gold on it, and the stone in the third so black it resembled a hole in the world.

Her face went as it went when a loaf came out of the oven and sounded hollow to her knuckle.

“Well,” said Vesna, to Arian. “Did he say it?”

“Before both courts.”

“Hmph.” She picked up her basket. “I’d have done it in the kitchen.” She turned back to Caleb and held out her hand. “Coat.”

“Vesna, there is nothing to go in it.”

“Then it can hold nothing, like a decent lining, and I’ll know where it is.” Her hand stayed out. “His Highness has torn something.”

“I cut it.”

“Same thing,” Vesna insisted. “Coat.”

He took the cloak out from inside it and gave her the coat. She sat down on an upended cask under the black sun, with the forty lycans of the Coast standing round her and the Nine looking at their own hands, threaded a needle against the grey sky with one squint, and began to sew up the lining of a prince of the Solar House in close, even stitches, as if she were alone in her own linen room and nobody in the world was watching.

On the grass beside her, on a hazel crutch, with his splinted leg held out before him like a bowsprit, Mirek was looking at the sun.

“*Prächtig*,” he told it, with deep respect.

\* \* \*

Enzo had come down the gangway behind them. He stood on the grass in his dove grey with the despatch case under one arm and looked round him at the valley, and last of all at the ground a little way off, where a long white street began between two broken walls and went up, and up, towards the fountain court.

He looked at it for a while. Then he looked away, and was the Consigliere once more, and took a sheet of the Coast’s black paper out of the despatch case and gave it to Caleb.

It was written in silver, in the Consigliere’s hand, to the Nine and to every house in the valley. *The Rite of the Black Sun will be kept at the height, in the glasshouse, by Master Hild of the Holds, if both parties consent to it aloud.* Pinned beneath it was a half sheet of the Chancellery’s blue that had come back within the bell, in a hand Caleb had known since it ruled its first column. *The Solar Throne will attend. E.* The E had been gone over twice, as a pen goes over a letter when the hand holding it has stopped for a while in the middle.

Caleb looked up from it, standing on the grass of the Marches in his shirtsleeves and waistcoat, and found the Consigliere considering him, and saying nothing.

“I had not understood,” Caleb admitted, “that I was being married today.”

“Nor had the Sovereign. Until this morning.”

Caleb looked across the valley at the white tents on the far slope, and back at the black paper in his hand. His voice had done whatever he told it since the first bell. He was a little afraid of what would happen when it stopped.

“And has anybody,” Caleb asked, “thought to ask me?”

Enzo looked at his nephew.

So did Caleb. Arian stood at the foot of the gangway with the frost on his shoulders and the black sun over him and his right hand at his side, and his thumb turned the heavy gold on his third finger a quarter turn, and he said nothing at all. He was looking past them both, up the near hill, at the briars, and the empty iron, and the sky through it.

“It is not mine to ask, Highness,” said Enzo.

There was a silence on the grass, of the kind there is at a dinner when everybody at the table has understood something at once and is too well brought up to look at anybody.

Vesna bit off her thread.

“There is the other matter,” said Enzo, in the ledger voice.

He had no need to look at the Nine. Caleb looked for him, at the black and the smoke and the plum standing about on the grass, and counted. There were seven of them, and the Consigliere made eight, and the Coast would go on calling them the Nine, he supposed, for some centuries yet, as a town keeps the name of a gate that was pulled down before anybody living was born. Two of them had sat down on a bench the lycans had carried up from the yachts. Five had not. Among the five, between his two lycans, the lord in mulberry velvet had taken his eyes off the gangway at last and was looking at Arian’s right hand, and at the ring on it, with the patient face of a man who has read the last page of a book before the first.

“The Leaning stands,” Enzo went on. “A ring answers a contest. It does not unask a question.”

He took a second sheet from the case and held it out. It was a page of *The Oldest Laws of the Nine*, copied fair in silver.

*At the height let the Nine stand in a row, and ask of the shadows of the Coast whether the word of the Sovereign is to be obeyed. A Leaning once set is not unset by any thing that comes after it. A Leaning that finds for none finds against him of whom it was asked.*

The last line had been ruled under. Caleb supposed that nobody in the valley had troubled to read it in nine hundred years except one man. He read it again, and then looked up from it at the black sun that let the Sovereign of the Coast stand at the foot of a gangway in the open daylight, and understood what else that sun would do today.

Caleb looked out over the valley.

The whole of Old Meridian lay below him in the pearl, the mist and the arches and the tents of every house in the world and their people coming and going between them, the fae on the walls and the gnomes on their striped steps and the Guard by their dull lanterns and the lycans at their fire, and he looked for a shadow, at the foot of an arch or a tent pole or a passing fae, or under a gnome’s chair or a horse or a cask or his own boots on the grass, anywhere, the least scrap of one, a hand’s breadth of dark lying where it belonged, and found none, and none, and none, because the sun was a hole in the sky with fire round its edge, and every scrap of dark in the world lay everywhere at once.

There was nowhere in the valley for a shadow to lean.

“Don Salvatore chose the hour,” said Enzo. “He was a very tidy man.”

Arian’s thumb had stopped on the ring.

“Then let them ask.”

Somebody was calling across the field. It was Mirek, who had hopped some way off on his crutch to speak to a man of the Radiant Guard in white, and was coming back now at a pace that the Harp Vales would have had something to say about, with his face alight.

“Highness!” He arrived, and swayed, and was caught by the sergeant by the collar without either of them seeming to notice. “The Grand Alchemist’s compliments, Highness, and would His Highness come up to the white tents at once.” He frowned, to get it right. “And bring his bath.”

The Solar pavilions smelled of warm canvas and beeswax and the lavender that the Citadel’s laundry put into everything, and they were cold, because every one of them had been pitched to take the sun through its roof and there was no sun to take. Courtiers in the colours of morning stood about between the guy ropes with their shawls round them, glimmering faintly at the wrists, and turned as he went by, and saw the black. He went on up the rows with Mirek hopping beside him, past the place where Matilda had once lifted him onto her saddle and the place where Cecily had cried, and found that he remembered the way.

Cecily’s pavilion was the last in the top row. Its flap was tied back. Inside it the grey light came through the canvas roof as dim and even as the light in a dairy and lay on a camp table that had been cleared of everything a Grand Alchemist of the Solar Throne would be expected to want at a Summit, and covered instead with the contents of a satchel.

It held two pairs of tongs, one of them silver, and a saucer of white Vales porcelain, and a spirit lamp that had not been lit, a jeweller’s glass on a cord, a horn spoon, a fold of chamois leather, a brass balance in its case, and six notebooks, all open, all written to the edges in a hand that went uphill. And among them, with her elbows on the table and her chin on her fists and her hair falling every way it liked, sat his sister, looking at none of it.

“You brought it.”

He took the flask out of his pocket and set it down on the table among the notebooks.

It was warm still. It had been warm all morning against his side, through the black sun and the wind of the deck. Through the old glass the light in it moved slowly and gold, like a carp under a lily pad, and the label, in capitals that had been eight years old when they were written, said what it had always said. *FOR CALEB’S BATH. SUNLIGHT.*

“Every crystal in the Citadel went out at the first bell. Every one. The spires, and the lamps in the lower town, and the twenty on my bench, and the slivers in the sun on Eleanor’s cushion. I watched them go. And that did not.”

“No.”

“I lay awake all night thinking why.” She took her chin off her fists. “The crystals are hearts. Everybody says so in the nursery and nobody believes it, and Andrew believed it, and he was right. They are hearts, and hearts sleep when the sun goes. That is what the Long Eclipse is. It is every heart in the world asleep at once.” She touched the flask with one finger, as she had once touched its label on a bench in the spring. “And this is not a heart. It is only light. I made it, a little every morning since your birthday, out of nothing but what I had, as I made it when I was eight. It does not know the sun has gone.” She looked up at him. “It does not know any better.”

Caleb sat down on the stool across the table from her, because his knees had told him to.

“Eleanor came to my tent at the first bell,” said Cecily, to the flask. “With the sun on her head. She told me in four words and went away again.” She laid her burned hands flat among the notebooks. “I have been doing this ever since. It is the only thing in this valley I know how to do.”

“I have been trying to make it into something you could wear since Tilda came home and told me you had never had your bath,” she went on. “You cannot carry a flask about under the ash for a hundred years. You break everything. And in the Citadel it would not sit still for one breath. It ran back up to the spires whenever I took the stopper out, as a cat runs back to the house.” She pushed a notebook towards him, open at a page crossed out from top to bottom. “Today there is nothing for it to run to.”

“So it will stay where it is put.”

“If the right hand puts it.” She was already unstoppering the flask. “It is your bath. It knows you. It has been in your pocket for five days. Hold out your hands.”

He held them out.

“No. Together. As if somebody were going to pour water into them at a well.”

He cupped them together on the table between the notebooks, and his sister took the flask in both of hers and tipped it, slowly, and the sunlight came out.

It came as honey comes off a spoon, heavy and gold and in no hurry, and it smelled of the nursery bathroom of the Citadel, of warm tiles and white towels on the rail and the plain yellow soap that Cecily had always said smelled of sums, and it fell into his palms and lay there, and it was warm. It was warmer than his own hands. It lay in them in a gold pool that did not spill between his fingers, though nothing held it, and moved, turning over, as a child turns over in a bed it has been told it must stay in.

“Ask it.”

He had asked the noon of a whole hall to lean. He asked this to stay.

He did not do it in words. He did it as he had learned to do it on the Coast, under a canopy of frost, low, and warm, and without pushing, and the light in his palms heard him. It went still. It drew in on itself from the edges, as the dew draws itself up into a bead on a cabbage leaf, and the pool became a round, and the round became a bead, gold all through, and the bead grew heavier and smaller and brighter, until it sat in the cup of his two hands no larger than a pea, and was hard, and was warm, and was a stone.

Cecily had stopped breathing. She took up the jeweller’s glass on its cord, and her hand was shaking so that she had to hold her own wrist with the other, and she bent over his palms and looked.

It was clear gold, as clear as water in a gold cup. And in the heart of it there was a light that moved. The light of a crystal stands still in the stone and burns. This went round and round, slowly, in the middle of the stone, as the sun goes round in a dish of water set out on a nursery sill for a child to look at, and every time it came round it lit the stone from the inside to its edges, and went on.

“Oh,” whispered Cecily.

She sat back and looked at him over the glass with her face gone pink and crumpled, as it had gone at eight on the rim of a bath.

“It did what I said.”

“It always does. You said so yourself. It is the only thing you have ever made that does.”

“I know. I know.” She wiped her eyes with a burned wrist. “Andrew said the trenches give fewer every year.”

“Yes.”

“This is not a heart.” She said it slowly, as she said a thing to herself before she wrote it down. “It took me since your birthday to make a flask of it, and it will not light a street. It will not light a room. But it will not go out either, Caleb. Not under the ash, nor in the dark of the year, nor on a day like this. Nobody need ever hold out a hand and say *give* for it.” She took a breath. “I am calling it a Solar-Anchor. A ship’s anchor holds the ship where she is put, however the tide goes, and this will hold a sun where you are. Eleanor says it is a very poor name and that I ought to let the Treasury name it.”

“Are you going to let the Treasury name it?”

“I am not.”

The flap of the pavilion darkened.

Nobody threw a shadow that day, but somebody was standing in the doorway who filled it from post to post, and the grey light could not get round her.

“The Grand Alchemist,” said Hild, “sent to the Holds that she had a stone for a setting of mine, and that I should come and look at it before I was asked to set it.” She came in, and the canvas let the light back in behind her. “The Holds do not set what they have not tested.”

Hild had come in her black robe and scorched apron, as if a Summit of the Dominion were a thing to be met in one’s working clothes like any other. She came to the table and looked at the notebooks, and at the brass balance, and at the Grand Alchemist of the Solar Throne with her hair down and her face wet, and last of all at Caleb’s cupped hands.

She held out her own, palm up. It was hard as horn and burned to the elbow.

Caleb tipped the stone into it.

Hild looked at it a long while. She did not take up the jeweller’s glass. She closed her hand on the stone and held it shut, as a smith holds a thing to feel whether the heat has gone out of it, and opened it again.

“It is warm.”

“Yes, Master.”

“Stones are not warm.”

“This one,” said Cecily, “was a bath.”

Hild gave her the look she had once given Caleb across the Undergate, and the look went into her and stayed, and came out again with something decided.

“Who taught you?”

“Nobody. I was eight.”

“Hm,” said Hild, which Caleb suspected was a speech of some length from a Master Shaper of the Holds. She set the stone down on the chamois with great care, and took from the pocket of her apron a gauge of black iron like a little ladder, and held out her other hand. “Highness.”

He gave her his left. She turned it over, and back, and laid the gauge across the empty setting, and the stone beside it, and measured the one against the other with her eye.

“It will sit.”

“Thank you, Master.”

“Do not thank me. I have not set it.” She did not let go of his hand. She was looking at the setting, at the little open mouth of black iron that had sat empty on his finger since the first night, and her broad thumb went across it, once, as a mason’s thumb goes across a joint he made a long while ago to feel whether it has stood.

“I cut this,” she said, “in the first week of the winter.”

Caleb kept still.

“The Sovereign came to the Holds himself. By the door, on his feet, and stood on his line as any client of the Holds must. He was nine when he stood on it first and asked me whether the chain went all the way down, and I told him to come back when he had something to hang on it.” She turned his hand a little to the grey light. “He came back in the first week of the winter, and told me that he had. He asked for a ring in the old style, for a betrothal to be asked at the Long Eclipse, at the Summit, of the King of Aloria. For the King’s youngest son.”

“I told him the King of Aloria would refuse him,” Hild went on. “He said he expected so, and that he meant to ask the son also, and that the son did not, as a rule, take his father’s advice.” Her thumb lay still on the setting. “I asked what stone. He said the setting was to be left empty. He said there would be two bridegrooms, and the stone was the other one’s to choose.”

“The first week of the winter.”

“Three months,” said Hild, “before the vineyard burned.”

She looked up from his hand, then, into his face, and he saw her see it.

He had known since a ledger at the foot of a stair, with a word underlined twice in red beside it, that the crystals had begun to go missing three months before the vineyard, before any hostage, before any blood debt, when there had been no reason on earth for the Sovereign of Vesperia to begin hoarding sunlight in the belly of a mountain. He had known since a tower in the Citadel with burnt handprints on its oak that the half-weight ships had sent Andrew down a cellar stair. He had carried the two columns ever since and never once laid them side by side, and here was a dwarf in a scorched apron laying them side by side for him across a camp table with her thumb on his ring, twelve stones over three months from the first week of the winter, not fewer than twelve, a garden under a mountain for a boy the Sovereign of the Coast had met once among the roses and meant to come back and ask for, on the one day in twenty years he could stand in the open to do it.

It had been for him. All of it, from the first stone.

“You did not know.”

“No, Master.”

The Master Shaper of the Holds let go of his hand. She looked at it a moment longer where it lay on the table, with the iron ring on it and the empty setting, and then at the stone on its chamois, going round in its own gold, and nothing whatever happened to her face, as nothing happens to an anvil.

“The Holds do not discuss the business of their clients. I appear to have discussed it.”

“The Holds forgive anything once.”

Hild looked at him. Then something went across her face under the grey beard, slowly, as a line of light goes along the edge of a forge door when somebody inside lifts the latch, and was gone.

“So they do. I had not expected to need it.”

She picked up the stone on its chamois and folded the leather over it, corner over corner, and put it in her apron pocket where the gauge had been.

“At the height, in the glasshouse. Both parties present.” At the doorway she stopped, with the grey light round her, and looked back past Caleb at the Grand Alchemist of the Solar Throne.

“The Holds,” said Hild, “will wish to speak to you about baths.”

The Summit had been held in the old market of Old Meridian since there had been a Summit, because it was the only square in the city wide enough to keep every house at the distance every other house required, and the Holds had measured it again that morning, to be sure.

It was paved in the white stone, worn into shallow dishes where a thousand years of market carts had stood. At its head the Holds had set up an iron table, square, with an iron case upon it the length of a man’s arm, banded and sealed in the Holds’ manner, and beside it the dwarf of the Holds with the white rope of a beard had his ledger open already, and his pen wet. On the side of the square that gave onto the fountain court nobody stood at all. Nobody had been told not to. Caleb saw Enzo look once at the gap between the two broken columns, and the court beyond it, and the dry basin, and then not look again.

The courts stood round the square on their measured lines, and every face was turned up a little, as if the whole world had been asked a question by the sky and was waiting to be told it might answer.

In the middle of the square, a long stride before the iron table, somebody had swept a place on the stones. There was nothing in it. The Holds had measured that too.

Eleanor sat at the iron table in the Chancellery’s blue, crowned.

The sun on her head gave no light. Every sliver of the deep trench along its rays had gone dull at the first bell with every other heart in the world, and it sat on her smooth coil of hair like any old gold that has been handed down too often to be grand, and somehow looked less like a crown than it had ever looked on anybody, and more like hers. Her despatch case lay on the iron before her, and her right glove beside it, square. There was not a lamp in the valley to brighten for her, nor to go out.

They brought Andrew in at the far side, between Matilda’s Guard.

He wore his travelling grey. His hands had not stopped shaking since the Hall of Petitions, and his face had nothing on it but its own grey now, and its own lines, which the Solar House was not supposed to have. Their mother walked beside him in white, holding his arm. She did not look at the crowd, nor at the tents, nor at the black sun. She looked at the white stones in front of her son’s feet, as a woman looks at the path when she is carrying something that will spill.

Matilda brought them to their line and stopped there, in her mail, at Andrew’s shoulder, with her hand on the hilt of her sword. She had not taken it off the hilt since the yacht field.

Oriane had found herself a seat. It was an iron measure of the Holds, upended, and she sat on it with the green book open across her knees and the coin quiet at her throat, and her feathers had gone a careful grey to match the day.

“The Solar Throne required an inquiry,” said Eleanor, “before witnesses of the Holds, within the season. The season ends today. The inquiry is held.”

The dwarf wrote it down.

“It was required into what was done in a black gallery at Castel Nerezza, and what has been done since.” Her eyes came to Caleb, where he stood on the Coast’s side of the square, a pace from Arian, in the black coat with the lining sewn up behind the crescent and holding nothing. “Caleb of the Solar House. You are to be heard.”

“I have been heard in two halls, Majesty, and the *Lantern* has printed both. I do not believe there is a soul in this valley who does not know what was done in the gallery, including several of the mules.”

Along the arcade a fae tittered and was hushed. Across the square, between the Guard, the corner of his brother’s mouth went up, and stayed up a moment, and Caleb kept his eyes on it until it came down.

“I have only one thing to add that I have not said in either.”

“Then add it.”

“He has asked,” said Caleb, “every time since.”

Oriane’s coin did not stir.

Eleanor let the dwarf write it down before she answered.

“The Solar Throne finds that the Accord was broken in that part, upon the person of its son. It requires nothing of the Coast for it. Its son has asked it to require nothing, and it has learned this spring what comes of not listening to him.” She did not look up. “Caleb of the Solar House, who was given to the Coast for another’s hand, is given back. He is no hostage under the Accord, and never was. He passes into no one’s keeping, and the Throne makes no claim upon him. He has asked me not to.”

The dwarf wrote, and blotted. Eleanor turned a sheet.

“It was required also into what was done in a vineyard.”

The square went still.

She read from her sheet without looking at Andrew, in her Treasury voice, the whole of it, from the account with no name to the signet on its gold chain and the iron door that had never opened, and the forty-one, and the dwarf’s pen followed her line by line. When she had done she laid the sheet down on the iron, square to its edge, and looked across the square at her brother at last.

“Have you anything to say?”

Andrew’s mouth moved. His hands went on shaking at his sides.

“I said it,” he answered, “in the Throne Hall.” His voice was thin, and it was his own, and it had no dais in it anywhere. “I keep a column.”

Somewhere among the courtiers of the Solar Throne a voice muttered, not quite low enough, that the Crown Prince had lost the sun and kept the manner. Across the square his brother heard it, and nothing in his face disputed it.

He put his shaking hand into the breast of his grey coat, and the Guard at his shoulder moved, and Matilda said *Stand* without turning her head, and the Guard stood. Andrew took out a sheet of paper folded in four. It was creased so soft at its folds that it hung from his fingers like cloth, and his hand shook so that it rattled, and he held it out across the white stones to the table, as far as his arm would go, and could not reach.

Matilda took it from him and carried it to the table and laid it down beside Eleanor’s glove.

Eleanor unfolded it. It was forty-one lines in the careful even hand that had written *the Sovereign in the second*, with not one word struck out. Under them, after a ruled line, where the foot of a column is written, there was one line more. Caleb could not read it from where he stood. He did not need to. He watched his sister read it, and her face did not change, as it had not changed at the Fault letter, and her bare hand lay flat on the iron beside the page for a moment, as a hand lies on a table in a ship that has begun to roll.

“The account at the Seventh Chamber that has no name,” said Eleanor, to the square, “is forfeit to the Solar Throne. The Chamber will pay out of it, to the house of every name in this column, in the order in which my brother has written them, until it is empty.” She laid the column on the iron. “And then it will close the account, as is usual when a portion is spent.”

The dwarf of the Holds looked up at the Queen of Aloria, once, and wrote.

Andrew lifted his head.

“Majesty.”

It was the first time he had called her so, and it went across the white stones as plainly as a coin goes down on a counter.

“I told my brother last night what I should do, if I were King and a man were brought before me who had done what I have done. He did not ask me to say it here. I am saying it, so that you need not.” He drew a breath, and his hands were still. “The Throne asks what I have to say. I ask the Throne for my death.”

The coin at Oriane’s throat lay quiet on its ribbon. Every word of it was true.

Their mother made no sound. Her hand stayed where it was, under his arm, and closed.

Eleanor had known since the fourth bell of the night, when her brother had come to the door of the Chancellery from the east tower and stood in it, and could not say it, and she had said it for him in four words. She did not look at Caleb now. She sat under the dull sun with her bare right hand flat on the iron beside the column, and looked at the elder of her two brothers across the swept place on the stones, and said it again, in the voice of the Treasury, for the Holds to write down.

“The Solar Throne has heard what was done in the vineyard, and by whose hand, and what that hand asks of it,” said the Queen of Aloria. “It is granted. Andrew of the Solar House shall die. Here, under this sun, before the height.”

Caleb’s hand closed.

He had not told it to. It closed at his side until the iron ring bit, and he could not have said whether it closed for himself on a yacht with ash in his mouth, or for a boy of sixteen carrying a dripping child to bed, or for the grey man across the square with their mother holding his arm.

Something cold came over the back of it.

It was a hand. It lay over his fist, flat, the whole cold palm of it, with the frost coming out fine and white over his knuckles and the Sovereign’s ring against the bone, and it made no attempt to open anything. It lay there as a child’s hand had once lain over a dying boy’s on the hill above them while the silver sang, and waited, as if it had all the time in the world and had been told that this was the way it was done.

Arian did not look at him. He was looking at Andrew, as he had looked at the Nine on the fourth night, as a clerk looks at a figure he is about to carry to the foot of a page, and there was nothing in his face at all.

Under the cold palm, slowly, Caleb’s fist came open. He took his hand out from under Arian’s, and Arian let it go.

“The sentences of the Throne,” said Matilda, “are carried out by the High Paladin.”

She said it to nobody, flat, as she gave the watchword at a gate, and her hand tightened on the hilt.

“I will be the last one to say goodbye to him, Tilda,” Caleb murmured.

It was not loud. It went across the white square through the measured distances of the Holds, and the courts of the world heard it, and turned.

Matilda looked at him. Then she looked at her brother on his line between her Guard, and Andrew lifted his chin to her a little, as he had lifted it across a supper table all their lives when he meant *let him*. Beyond her, Eleanor had gone very still in her new gold, and Cecily’s hands had stopped wringing each other. They understood it, all three of them, in the same breath, as sisters understand a thing across a crowded room without a word passing. Andrew wanted Caleb to be the last face he saw. And Caleb meant to be the last face Andrew saw. Something in Matilda’s face that had stood forty-four days in the snow without giving an inch gave, very slightly, and stood again.

She took her hand off her sword.

At the hip of every knight of the Radiant Guard, under the sword, hangs a second blade, narrow as a reed and a hand and a half long, with no edge to speak of and a point like a needle, made for the gap between the plates of a fallen man’s mail. The Guard call it the mercy. It is for the ending of what cannot be saved, and every knight is taught on the first day of the yard where it goes, and prays never to need it. Matilda drew hers, and turned it in her gauntlet, and held it out across the stones hilt first.

Caleb did not take it. He was looking at Andrew.

“I will do it my way,” he told her softly.

Caleb crossed the square.

He went across the white stones through the measured distances of the Holds, and the courts of the world watched him go and did not make a sound. He came to his brother’s side and stood there.

Andrew looked at him.

“Mother,” he said, without looking away from Caleb. “You must let go now.”

Their mother let go of his arm. She put her two hands on his face, as she had in the Throne Hall, and held it, and looked at it, as if she meant to be examined on it afterwards and would not be allowed the page. Then she took her hands away and went back three steps to the line the Holds had paced out for her, and stood on it in her white, and did not look away.

Andrew went down on his knees on the swept stone, slowly, one knee and then the other, as he had knelt for the sun at twelve, home from the Vales with his words by heart, and Caleb went down on his knees in front of him, so that their faces were level. He did not bow his head. He knelt upright, with his face lifted and turned to his brother, and the grey hair at his temple lay against the grey of his collar. His left hand came up between them without his looking, and found Caleb’s left, and held on. It was cold to the fingertips. It had stopped shaking.

His eyes were on Caleb’s, and they stayed there.

“What is it like?” said Andrew.

Neither of them looked up. Caleb had seen it from his balcony at the first bell, and he would not take his eyes from his brother’s to see it again.

“It is black,” he said. His voice was the voice of a hundred embassy dinners, and it carried, and he did not care who it carried to. “It has a rim of white fire round it, like the brandy round the pudding at First Light, when you held the spoon because you were the eldest and nobody else was allowed. The sky is the grey of the ash in a grate in the morning. And there are twelve stars. Eleven came at the first bell, and one came late.”

“Of course it did.” Andrew smiled at him.

“That is a good note.” His thumb moved once on Caleb’s knuckle, as it had in the tower.

Caleb lifted his right hand and laid it flat on his brother’s breast, over the grey coat, over the heart.

It was there under his palm, as it had been under his cheek at five in a towel that smelled of gold, and under his hand on every stair and in every sickroom since. He had known that heart almost all his life. It went slow and steady, the heart of a man who has finished a long column and found that it comes out, and Caleb felt it and could not see. His eyes had filled and run over, and the black sun and the white stone and his brother’s face swam together in the grey as a window swims in the rain, and he did not wipe them, because he would not take his hand away.

Andrew’s right hand came up and lay over his, and pressed it flat to his breast, and held it there.

“Live well, Caleb,” he whispered. “It was my place to carry you. I am the eldest. It was never yours to carry me.”

He nodded, once, with his eyes still on his brother’s, the nod of an eldest brother who had always been the one to say when.

The Vales had taught him to keep a heart beating under his palm, to call it back when it faltered and hold it when it ran, and he had done it a thousand times. Nobody had ever taught him the other way, because nobody at the Vales had ever thought it would need teaching. He found it all the same. He let the Grace go into his brother as gently as he had ever let it go into anything, and asked the heart under his hand to rest.

It slowed.

He counted it, as the Vales teach, because he could not have borne not to. It went on, slower, and slower, as a man walking home at the end of a long day slows on the last of the road with his own door in sight, and under it Caleb felt his brother’s whole body ease, all along its length, as a knot eases that has been pulled tight a great many years. Andrew’s hand pressed once on his, hard, and stayed. His eyes did not leave his brother’s. They stayed on him through all of it, wide and calm and full of him, and Caleb held them through the blur, and did not look away, and did not let go, and watched the light go out of them as the light goes out of a window when the lamp behind it is carried into another room, slowly, and then all at once, and then the window is only glass.

The heart under his palm beat once more, and did not beat again.

The hand in his opened, and was only a hand.

The body came forward against him, and he caught it, and held it.

The light came out of him gold.

For the space of a breath it rose all over him, faint, at the wrists and the throat and the roots of the grey hair, the last of the Solar House in him, the hour of what he ought to have been that he had borrowed every morning out of a twist of paper and never once been able to keep. It was his own. It lit Caleb’s hands from beneath, and his wet face, the warmest thing in all that valley under the black sun, and went up off him into the grey as breath goes up off a sleeper on a winter morning. Then it was gone, and he was only a grey man in a grey coat, lying very still against his brother in the old market of Old Meridian.

And the gold went out of his seams.

It went all at once, as a lamp goes out when the oil is spilled, and left the black coat only black. There was no light left on Caleb anywhere, and he knelt with his brother in the grey with nothing to see by, and did not want any.

Matilda put the mercy back in its sheath, unused, slowly, as if it weighed a great deal more than it had. Then she stood where she was, at her brothers’ shoulders, with her hand on the hilt, as she had stood forty-four days at the Pas de la Dame, and did not move.

Somewhere among the people of the Solar Throne a man was being sick. Cecily was not. She stood at the front of them with her hands holding each other in front of her, too hard, and watched all of it, because she had made the blades, and had decided in the night that she would watch everything now. There was nothing on the white stone to see but two brothers and the grey, and she watched that.

Their mother came across the stones.

She knelt beside them on the stone in her white. She unpinned the shawl from her shoulders, the white shawl that had lain folded at the head of a hollow in a bed in the Citadel for eleven weeks, and she laid it over her son, over all of him that she could, and smoothed it, and her hands went on smoothing it after there was nothing left to smooth. Then she put her hand on Caleb’s head, on his hair, and left it there, and neither of them said anything.

Footsteps came across the stone, unhurried, and stopped behind him.

There was no sun today to burn anybody. Arian stood in the open behind him on the white stones of the old market and did not touch the shawl or the woman or the man under it. He laid his hand on the back of Caleb’s neck. It was cold. It stayed. Caleb leaned back against his legs, and found that he could breathe.

Enzo came last.

He came forward from the Coast’s line in his dove grey with his hands behind him and stopped at the edge of the shawl. He looked down at the shawl for some time. Then he took his hand from behind his back, and in it was a chain as fine as a hair, with one link at its end wrenched open. He stooped, which Caleb had never seen him do, and laid it across the open palm that still lay in Caleb’s, and straightened.

“Your son wished a thing, Majesty, and did not ask it,” he said, to their mother, in the ledger voice. “His Highness asked it of me for him, on the gangway at the Citadel, and again on the yacht field.” The voice held. “The new rows at the Sanctuary are dug. The vines will take him, when you are ready to let them.”

Their mother looked up at him from the stones.

“My wife fed everything that came to her door,” said Enzo, and went back to his line.

At her iron measure Oriane de Chardon wrote one line, and closed the green book, and sat with her feathers gone as grey as the sky.

They renewed the Accord at the iron table in the old market before the height, because the Accord said that it must be made new at the height, and the height would not wait for anybody.

Four of the Guard had carried Andrew up the hill to the white pavilions under his mother’s shawl, with their mother walking beside him, and Cecily behind her. Caleb had washed his face in a lycan’s pail, and his right palm still felt the last beat under it, as a hand that has held a bell rope feels the bell long after.

The dwarf of the Holds opened the iron case and took out the Accord as it had been taken out every twenty years since it was made, a long sheet of the Holds’ grey paper gone brown at every edge, with the seals of every crowned head that had ever put a hand to it pressed in two long rows down its margins, sun and crescent, sun and crescent, like the footprints of two people who have walked a long way side by side along a beach and never once touched. Enzo read it aloud from the first Article to the last, and when he came to the Ninth he stopped, and looked at the Queen of Aloria, and she looked back at him.

*Where the blood of an Umbral house is shed upon Umbral ground by a hand of the Solar House, the wronged may claim the debt in blood, or in a hostage of equal rank.*

“The Solar Throne will put its hand to the Accord as it stands,” said Eleanor, “save one Article. It has paid that debt today in the first coin. It will not pay it again in the second, nor ask it of anybody. It will give no more of its children to be held.”

“The Coast will strike it with you,” Arian answered.

The dwarf drew one line through the Ninth Article in black ink, square and level, and then a second beneath it, and blotted them, and turned the sheet round on the iron so that the courts could see that it had been done. “Twice. Once for each house. The Holds have it.”

Then Eleanor rose, and laid her bare right hand flat on the grey paper.

Arian laid his beside it, a finger’s breadth away and no nearer, as the two courts had laid their hands on that paper at every renewal, and as partners lift their palms to each other in the *distanza* and never once close the space. The frost came out of his hand across the grey and stopped at the edge of that space. On Eleanor’s side the paper under her palm went faintly warm and faintly brown, as a page goes that has lain a long afternoon in a window, and stopped at the same edge.

“It is made new,” said the dwarf.

On the Coast’s side of the square the Nine had risen from the bench the lycans had brought, because it was the custom of the Coast that the heads of its houses stand when the Accord was made new. They stood in a row on the line the Holds had paced out for them that morning, from the roll the Accord was last signed under, and that roll had nine houses on it. Between the woman with the careful voice and the lord beside her there was a square of white paving with a peg of black iron driven into it, where the Ashward should have stood, and nobody on it.

Nobody closed it up. The Nine stood on either side of it with a careful space at their elbows, as mourners stand at the edge of a grave and keep their boots off the turned earth.

The dwarf of the Holds looked at the peg, and then at his ledger, and turned back a great many pages to the roll of the houses, and drew one line through a name there, square and level, and blotted it. He did not turn the ledger round to show anybody.

Eleanor had sat down again. She drew on her glove, and could not get the second finger in, and stopped trying.

“Where will you go?”

She said it across the square as she had once said *Are you happy?* across a garden, plainly, as one asks a sum.

Caleb looked at her, at his sister in the Chancellery’s blue with the dull sun on her hair and the glove half on, and past her at the white pavilions on the far slope, and then at the hill behind the black tents of the Coast, with the briars on it, and the iron ribs at the top against the grey.

“Up the hill, Majesty.”

Nobody laughed. The Queen of Aloria put her hand over her mouth, with the glove half on it.

The Rite said that the two should go up to it alone, and the courts after them at the Holds’ distance, and so they went up the white street alone.

It ran uphill from the old market between the ghosts of walls, narrow and steep and paved in the white stone of the city, and it was the street. Caleb knew it before Arian slowed. There was the base of a broken column on the left, where somebody might lean who could not go on without leaning, and a little further a doorframe with nothing behind it but a fig tree growing up through a floor. Twenty years of rain had gone over all of it, and there was nothing on any of the stones, and Arian looked at every one of them as he passed, as a man reads down a page he wrote long ago and has been told he will be examined on.

He stood in the open with nothing over his head, and threw no shadow on them. Neither did Caleb. There was no gold at his seams to throw one by. There had been none since the old market, and he had not looked for it. They went up the street side by side in the pearl like two figures painted on a plate, with the black sun over them and the hill in front and, a long way behind, the soft many-footed sound of the courts of the world coming up after them at a measured distance.

Halfway up there was a fountain. It was dry, with a stone woman in the middle of it who had long ago given her face to the weather, and Caleb stopped by it, because his feet did.

“Matilda found me here. On the way down. I had got this far.”

Arian looked at the fountain, and at the paving round it, and at him, and said nothing.

Caleb stood with his hand on the cold rim. He thought of a room at the top of a stair with every shutter thrown back, and a man in grey sitting in the whole of the noon with his face turned up to it, who had kept the shutters a hand’s width for twenty-three years and asked at the last to see it once, all of it, before it went out.

He looked for his light.

He found it where it had gone, at the bottom of him, lying down with its face to the wall, as a child lies who has been told something at bedtime that it did not want to hear. He did not order it up. He had never ordered it anything. He asked it, low and warm, as he had learned to ask it on the Coast in a room where no lamp had ever been lit, and he told it, because it seemed to him that it ought to know, that somebody had once lifted it out of a gold bath and carried it to bed dripping, and had not dropped it, and had wanted nothing of it at the last but that it should be happy.

It got up.

It came back along his seams a thread at a time, as the lamps come out one by one along a street at dusk, faint and then gold, and lay out from him across the white stone of the street, the only warm colour on that hill, and the frost on Arian’s lashes caught it, and kept it.

Arian looked at the light, and then at him.

“You came a long way, for so short a person.”

“I was eight. I have since grown, as you see, nearly to your shoulder.” He did not move on. He stood by the faceless fountain with his hand on its rim and looked up at the man beside him, and found that the question he had carried up the hill from a camp table had grown tired of waiting to be asked.

“Master Hild has been indiscreet. She told me when you went to her about the ring.”

Arian did not move. The frost on his lashes stayed as it was.

“When were you going to ask me?”

“Today.”

The black sun hung over the hill. Somewhere far down the street behind them, the Holds were measuring somebody back onto his line.

“And if I had said no?”

“At the next.”

“That is twenty years.”

“I have done it before.”

Caleb took his hand off the fountain.

“The crystals.”

“Twelve.”

“My brother counted the ships.”

“I heard him.” The frost had come out along Arian’s jaw, white, and stayed. He looked down the white street, towards the market and the tents and the gap between the columns that nobody had stood in. “I have done the sum.”

“So have I.”

He had done it at a camp table that morning, and again at every stone of this street, and it came out the same each time. He said it anyway, because some sums ought to be said aloud before witnesses, and there was nobody on that street but the faceless woman in the fountain, and she would have to do.

“You kept back twelve stones to make a garden for somebody you had met once, so that you might come and ask for him. My brother went down a cellar stair for a heart that was not his, and burned forty-one people who stood in his way, and kept their names in a column, and paid for every one of them this morning, because he asked to. They are written in the same column, Arian. They are not the same sum.”

Arian was quiet for a long time.

“My aunt knew,” he answered at last. His voice did not change. Only the frost along his jaw went whiter. “She wished to see me ask.”

Caleb thought of a cellarer’s label tied on with red waxed twine, and a letter on cream vineyard paper in a slanting hand. *And then you are to bring him to the Sanctuary, and I will feed him.*

“Then we had better do it properly.”

Up the hill the iron ribs of the glasshouse stood against the grey with nothing in them.

“I had a speech,” murmured Arian.

“Of course you had a speech.”

He looked down at Caleb by the dry fountain. “I have forgotten all of it but the end.”

“Then say the end.”

Arian said it as plainly as he had once said *Yes* on an iron sill when he was asked whether a man was safe.

“Will you have me?”

“Yes,” said Caleb.

He had meant to say something after it, and had been composing it on and off since the foot of the gangway, and it was very good. He looked at the frost in the black hair, and the ring on the right hand that had cost him everything he had, and found that it was no use, and that he did not want it.

“Yes,” he said again, which was all he had left, and turned, and they went on up the hill.

Somebody with a measuring staff had cut the briars back from the glasshouse door that morning, in a lane as wide as the Rite required and not a thumb wider, and left the rest of the hill to its thorns, and the courts of the world stood in them.

They stood on the slope below the door on lines the Holds had paced out through the briars and marked with white pegs, the colours of morning on the left hand and the black and the plum on the right, and the briars caught at the hems of the Dominion without fear or favour and let none of it go without an argument. Three gnomes of Lowmere had climbed onto one another’s steps to see.

On a white peg near the front Vesna had sat Cecily down by the shoulders and was putting up her hair with lycan pins, which are made for hill wind, and the Grand Alchemist of the Solar Throne, who had never in her life let anybody touch her hair but a nursemaid long dead, sat still under it with her eyes shut and her face swollen and her hands in her lap, and let her. Vesna had looked at that face once, and asked nothing.

Among the three masters of the Holds who would stand witness was a dwarf with three plaits, who had brought his hammer down on an empty casket in Dornhöhle at the first bell and then come forty leagues by the Holds’ fast yacht to stand in a briar patch, and when Caleb came up the lane Master Brandt bowed to him by the fourth degree, to the hair.

Inside the iron, the roses had grown.

Twenty years ago they had put out new leaves for a boy, tender and red at the edges, under the last black sun. They had kept the leaves. They filled the glasshouse now as high as a man’s shoulder, dark green and thick with thorns, and they were full of buds, closed, hundreds of them, hard and green, as if they were only waiting to be told they might.

Before the door, across the lane, the Nine stood in a row.

They had brought the steward of the hall from Castel Nerezza with his staff wrapped in green baize, and he unwrapped it now and brought it down three times on the white stone of the threshold, and the sound went out over the hill and the briars and the courts as it went out over the black glass of the great hall, and the Nine stood. The woman with the careful voice stood in the middle of them. The lord of the inland vineyards stood last in the row between his two lycans, in his mulberry, and it was he who had moved the Leaning, and he who asked it.

“The Nine ask the shadows of the Coast,” he said, pleasantly, as a man reads out the terms of a lease, “whether the word of Arian of House Conti is to be obeyed.”

The hill was quiet. The dark of the black sun lay over all of it as evenly as the pearl, under every foot and every briar and the iron ribs alike, and none of it leaned anywhere, because there was nowhere it was not already.

“There is no answer,” said the lord of the inland vineyards, gently.

“The height,” said Enzo, “is not over.”

The lord inclined his head to him, civilly, and waited.

Hild came out of the roses.

She stood on the earth floor of the glasshouse among the thorns with her hammer at her belt and her hands folded on her apron, and spoke the old words in the plain Alorian of the treatises, each set down on the last as level as a course of brick, and the courts below heard every word. *Let the two stand in the open, with nothing over their heads.* She looked up once at the iron ribs going over against the grey, and the black sun beyond them. “The Holds have measured the iron. It is mostly sky.” Then she looked down the lane. “Who asks for the house of the Solar Throne?”

Eleanor was standing at the front of the Solar Throne’s people, crowned.

“The Queen of Aloria has been asking questions of this kingdom since she could hold a pen. The High Paladin has never asked anybody anything in her life. She may begin.”

Matilda looked at her sister. Then she came up the lane with a dozen roads’ mud still on her boots and in at the glasshouse door, and stood among the roses on Caleb’s side as straight as a halberd.

“Who asks for the house of the Coast?”

Enzo came in after her and took the other side, at the full length of his arm from his nephew, and came no nearer until the Rite required it.

Caleb opened his coat. He took out what lay folded inside it over his heart, where for nearly four weeks a ring had lain, and shook it out, and a child’s cloak of grey wool hung from his hands in the pearl light with its darned hem and its stained collar and the silver thread all through it, and the gold sunburst his mother had pricked her thumb on, ray by ray.

Arian held out his right hand, with the Sovereign’s ring on it. Caleb gave him his left, with the iron.

Matilda took the cloak by one shoulder and Enzo by the other, and between them they held it up over the two joined hands, with its little gold sun turned up to the black one. It covered the two hands and nothing else, which was all that it had been asked to cover. Under it Arian’s fingers were cold and steady, and closed, and the frost came out of them over Caleb’s knuckles, white, and caught his gold, and kept it.

Enzo looked at him across the grey wool.

“*Altezza*. What do you bring him, that he has not?”

The word went through Caleb as it had gone through him in the lamplight of his own rooms, and he let it.

“Something that is not a fire. He has stood beside a great many. They were very bright. I promised him on the Fault road that I should find him something else by the time we reached the Coast, and I am told I had given it to him already some while before, in this house, and did not know.” He looked at Arian. “I should like to give it again, knowing.”

Enzo said nothing. Over the grey wool his hand had tightened on the shoulder of the cloak until the knuckles stood white.

Hild turned her head.

Matilda opened her mouth. She shut it again. Caleb watched his sister, who had never once been at a loss before an enemy, stand among the old roses of Old Meridian with the shoulder of a child’s cloak in her fist and look for a question as she might have looked for a dropped sword in long grass, and he saw her find it, and pick it up, and hold it the only way she knew.

“Sovereign. What do you bring my brother that he has not?”

It came out like an order of the day. It was a question all the same.

Arian did not look at the Nine. He looked at Caleb.

“A heart.”

On the lane below the door, nobody in the row of the Nine moved, and every one of them heard it.

“He lent me his,” said Arian, “in this house, when I was twelve. I have kept time by it since.” His voice did not rise. It went down the hill over the briars, level and unhurried. “I bring him mine.”

Under the grey wool, where nobody on that hill could see it, his thumb moved over Caleb’s knuckles and was still.

“He told me in this house that he was not afraid of me,” said the Sovereign of the Obsidian Coast, before the courts of the world. “So there.”

Somebody down the hill made a sound of pure astonishment. The *Lantern*’s pen stopped. Caleb laughed aloud under the black sun, in the house where he had said it first, and Matilda looked at the Sovereign of the Coast for a long moment over the grey wool and said, as she said it of a sentry she had found awake, “Good.”

“It is answered,” said Hild.

She took the chamois out of her apron pocket and unfolded it, and the stone lay on it going round in its own gold. Matilda and Enzo lifted the cloak a little, and Arian turned Caleb’s hand over in his own, so that the empty setting lay uppermost to the grey light, and held it there. Hild laid the stone in it. It sat, as she had said it would. She took a setter’s punch from her belt, of black iron no longer than her thumb, and set its point to the rim, and took the little hammer.

“The Holds set a stone with one stroke, or not at all.”

She struck.

The iron closed. It came in over the edge of the stone all round, as a hand closes gently over a moth to carry it out of a room, and the runes round the band woke from the setting outward, gold, one after another, as they had woken on the iron doors of a garden at the bottom of the world, and the stone in the setting lit. Its light went round, and round, and came to the edge of the stone and went into the iron, and the ring was finished.

And every heart in the world woke.

It began on Eleanor’s brow. Every sliver of the deep trench along the rays of the sun on her head took light at once, white and gold, as they had taken the noon in the Throne Hall, and blazed. Then the lanterns of the Guard on their poles down the hill, and the violet lamps in the black tents of the Coast, and the crystals in the iron crowns of the pylons along the ridges one after another out to the rim of the valley and over it, and beyond the rim the grey went gold at its edges all the way round the sky, as the edge of a page goes gold when it is held to a candle, and Caleb knew without knowing how that it was every spire of the Citadel and every lamp of the Underglow and twelve stones on silver chains under the ash of the Coast, and the twenty under a cloth on his sister’s bench, and things older than any of them asleep in the trenches under the sea, and one behind a black iron door at the foot of a cellar stair a few valleys off, all of them, every heart that had ever been cut and set to give light, turning over once in its sleep and opening its eyes.

The light came from every side at once, and it came hardest to the top of the hill, as light comes to the middle of a burning glass. It came over the briars and in at the iron ribs onto the roses and onto Arian’s white face, the light of every heart in the world, enough to burn a city of the Umbral houses to ash, and before it the dark that had lain over everything since the first bell drew back off the hill, as the dark goes out of a room when somebody throws the shutters open. There was nothing left between Arian and all that light but a child’s cloak over two hands. Caleb’s hand closed hard on the cold one.

And something went over.

He looked up. There was nothing in the sky but the black sun with its fire and the twelve stars round it. But a shadow came across the valley from the far rim all the same, vast and soft and dark, as the shadow of weather comes across a moor on a day of high wind, though there was no weather and no wind, and it had wings. It was the only shadow that fell anywhere that day. He saw it go over the white tents of the Solar Throne and the iron of the Holds and the black silk of the Coast, two wings as wide as the valley, and between them the long dark line of something that had once had a neck and a head to hold up into the sky, and it came up the hill over the courts of the world, who put up their hands to it, and over the Nine in their row, and in at the empty iron of the glasshouse, and lay down over all of it, and over Arian.

At the bottom of Caleb, under the light, the old thing that had opened one eye in this house when he was eight and lent him its shoulder in the Throne Hall of his fathers opened both, and stood up in him, and he felt it stand as a man feels his own shoulders go back, and felt it spread, wide, over a boy it had chosen twenty years ago in the mud, and he did not know then, and never afterwards knew, whether the shadow on the hill was its or only like it. But he knew now whose fire had kept time under Arian’s ribs for twenty years, and why he could feel it from the far end of the world, when no other pair the Holds had ever sealed had felt anything at that anvil but iron.

The light of every heart in the world blazed round the glasshouse, and in the middle of it, under the wings, the Sovereign of the Obsidian Coast stood in shadow with his hand in Caleb’s and his face turned up, and the frost on him lit gold from every side, and did not smoke.

It did not burn him.

The Nine felt it. Caleb saw it go down the row of them on the lane as a draught goes down a row of candles. The woman with the careful voice put her hand flat against her breast and looked up at the wings with her lips parted. At the end of the row the lord of the inland vineyards did not look up. He looked down at the briars round his feet, where there had been nothing to look at since the first bell, and then across the lane at the Sovereign, and Caleb understood, watching his face, that his own shadow, the one his house had been lent when the houses were made, had gone up out of the pearl with all the rest of the Coast’s dark, and was lying in that one shadow over Arian of House Conti, as far towards him as a shadow can lean.

He sat down in the briars.

Far below the hill, in the old market, the Guard had washed the white stones, and the water had gone pink into the dishes the carts had worn, and lain there. Caleb was never afterwards sure of it, and never asked anybody who might have been looking. But he thought that in that one heartbeat, when every heart in the world woke, something in those worn dishes woke too, faintly, gold, for about as long as it takes to say a name, and went out again with the rest.

Then the heartbeat was over.

The hearts of the world went back to sleep. Eleanor’s sun went dull on her hair. The lanterns went grey on their poles, and the rim of the sky went back to pearl, and the wings went off the hill as breath goes off a glass, and the dark came back over everything, even and everywhere and leaning nowhere, and there was the black sun again, and the twelve stars, and the old roses, and nobody on that hill moved at all.

Then, in the roses, one of the buds opened.

It was white. It opened slowly, as a fist unclenches in sleep, and then the one beside it, and another, until all over the floor of the glasshouse the old roses of Old Meridian were coming into flower round the two of them in the pearl, white as the first page of a new ledger, with the grey cloak still held over their two hands in the middle of it all and the stone on Caleb’s finger turning its own gold.

On the lane before the door, the woman with the careful voice was the first of the Nine to speak.

“The Nine have asked, Sovereign.”

“Yes.”

“They are answered.” She bowed a hand’s breadth lower than one of the Nine owes the Sovereign of the Coast, as the Holds bow to a ring, and the Nine bowed with her, all but one, who was sitting in a briar patch in mulberry velvet and did not appear to have noticed that he had sat down.

He noticed presently.

Under the black sun the dark lay on everything on that hill, under every foot and every briar, belonging to nobody and keeping the years off everybody, and Caleb saw it draw back from the lord of the inland vineyards. It drew back off him all round, a hand’s breadth, as the water of a pond draws back from a stone that has been heated in a fire, and left him sitting in the briars in a ring of bare pearl, the only place on all that hill where no dark lay. His house had been lent its share of the Coast’s shadow when the houses were made. It had gone up into the wings in that heartbeat with all the rest, and lain down over Arian of House Conti, and Arian had kept it, without a word and without looking round, as a man keeps a coin that was only ever lent.

The lord of the inland vineyards looked down at the bare ring round him. Then he looked up, not at the Sovereign, but at Caleb, through the iron and the roses, as he had looked at him once across a long table under lowered lamps while a pale flower opened in his buttonhole. He inclined his head, the depth one of the Nine owes a consort of the Coast and a little lower, as a man inclines it across a card table to a hand that has beaten his fairly.

“Felicitations, Highness,” he said, pleasantly, and appeared to mean it.

Then the years came.

They did not come to him as they had come to Don Salvatore, kindly, as the ash comes down. There were a great many more of them than one face was ever meant to carry, and they had been kept off him a long while, and they came all at once and took him apart. His skin drew in to the bone, as a grape draws in that has been left on the vine past the vintage, and split along the knuckles of the hand he had lifted, and nothing came out of the splits. His knee went under him with the sound of a dry vine cane snapped for kindling. He put the hand out to the briars to hold himself up, and the fingers went to grey powder on the thorns, and the rest of him followed them, not softly, in pieces, the face last, still pleasant, still turned towards the glasshouse, until there was a heap of grey in the briars and a coat of mulberry velvet folding down onto it.

On the velvet lay a flower, gone brown and flat, that he had kept in his buttonhole a fortnight in the dark of a cellar. He had been the last of the Ashward’s creatures on the Coast.

Nobody on that hill had moved. On the top of the pile of gnomes, one had put both her hands over her mouth.

Arian had not looked round once. Under the grey wool his thumb went across the new stone on Caleb’s finger and rested there, as if it were the only thing on the hill that had happened.

Caleb’s hand had closed on his, hard, under the cloak, at the sound of the knee. He thought afterwards that it was why it had been so quick, and that it was all the mercy there had been in it.

Somewhere on the slope Mirek whispered “*prächtig*” to the place in the sky where the wings had been, and was not contradicted. On her step among the gnomes of Lowmere, Maartje Bosboom had opened her ledger on its chain and was writing in it, and did not strike it through. The *Lantern* sat on a white peg with her pen lifted over the green book, and wrote nothing for a long while, and then looked up at Caleb through the iron with her feathers gone every colour at once.

“I have no adjective, Highness,” called Oriane de Chardon. “I shall have to print it plain.”

Cecily’s hair had stayed up, every pin.

Hild put her hammer back in her belt. Enzo and Matilda lowered the cloak between them, and Enzo folded it with the stained collar turned in and laid it in Caleb’s free hand without a word.

“The Holds require nothing further,” said Hild. She considered Caleb’s face, as she had considered it once by an anvil with a link going from white to gold. “Whatever they may do in Lutèce.”

Caleb looked down the lane at the coat of mulberry velvet folded in the briars, and then up at Arian, standing in a glasshouse full of white roses with the frost lit gold on his lashes and nothing over his head but sky. Arian was looking at him, and waiting, as he had waited on a sill every night of a spring, to be told what he was.

“May I?” he asked.

Arian looked down at him.

“You need not ask. Not today.”

“I know. I should like to.”

“Yes.”

Caleb put his hand at the small of Arian’s back, as a hand had once been put at the small of his own before a Council that did not know what it was looking at, and came up onto his toes in the roses, before both courts and the Nine and the *Lantern* and the Holds and a good many gnomes, and kissed him. It went on a good while longer than the one in the great hall had, and it was a good deal warmer, as warm as frost allows, and nobody stepped back at the end of it. When it was done he found that he was in no way speechless, only that for some while he had no wish to say anything.

The sun came back at the last bell.

It came back as a door comes open on a lit kitchen at the end of a long passage, a line of white along one edge of the black and then a sickle of it, low over the western hills, and every shadow in the valley came home at once from wherever it had been all day and lay down at the feet it belonged to, a little out of breath, as dogs come in at dusk from business of their own and drop by the hearth without explaining themselves. By then the Sovereign of the Coast and his Consigliere were inside the black silk of the Coast’s great tent with its flaps let down, because the Consigliere had made arrangements.

Caleb was on the far slope, among the white pavilions.

The Solar House burned its dead by the sun, through a glass, as it had since there was a Solar House, and it would not wait for the noon of the Citadel to do it. The Guard had built the pyre out of their own tent poles and the staves of a broken cask, and laid him on it in his grey with his mother’s shawl over him, and when the first white edge came off the black, Cecily held up the glass. She held it in both burned hands as high as they would go, as she had held a halberd in the Throne Hall, and her hair stayed up, and the first sunlight in all that long day went through the glass onto the oak and the shawl, and took.

He went up gold. Caleb stood between his sisters with their mother in front of them, and watched the fire take his brother under the first sun of the evening, and Matilda’s hand found the back of his neck, as another hand had in the old market, and stayed there until it was done.

The sun lay a little while on the rim of the hills as if to see that everything had been left where it was put, and went down.

They feasted in the old market in the dark, under lamps, because the Accord said that there must be a feast when it was made new, as it said a great many things that nobody wished to hear that night.

Vesna had brought baskets from the Coast, and the Holds had brought their cellar, and the lycans had brought fiddles. The courts of light and of shadow danced the *distanza* together on the stones the Guard had washed, hand lifted to hand and never meeting, and the Queen of Aloria opened it with the Consigliere of the Coast, because the Accord required that too. Both of them, Caleb was nearly certain, kept count under their breath, and neither of them looked at anything but the count. Mirek danced on his crutch with a gnome, who had not been told, and was the better for it.

Some while after the second bell of the night, Caleb took Arian by the hand and went out of the lamplight, and up the hill.

Enzo had made arrangements for that too. He had written to every house in the valley, in silver on the Coast’s black paper, that the Sovereign and his consort would keep the glasshouse that night by the old custom of the Rite, and that the courts were asked, by the same custom, to look at something else. Caleb had watched the ambassador of Lys read it at the feast and go pink to the ends of her feathers.

Matilda was sitting on the rim of the dry fountain halfway up the white street, in the dark, with her sword across her knees and a rag in her hand, and by the look of the rag she had been there a long while.

Caleb sat down beside her. Arian stopped some paces off in the dark, and was still.

She did not look up. She went on along the blade with the rag, from the guard to the point, slowly, though there had never been anything on it to come off.

“He did not move. At the end. They always move. He did not.”

“No.”

“He looked at you the whole time.” She turned the blade over. “It should have been my hand, Caleb. I am the sword. I said so.”

“He asked for mine. He was always telling us when.”

Caleb put his hand over hers on the rag, and she let him, and they sat so on the rim of the fountain where she had once found him at eight, cold through, until the fiddles down the hill had got to the end of a tune and begun another.

“Go up the hill,” said Matilda, in the voice of the orders of the day. “That is an order.”

“It is night, Tilda.”

“Then it is an order of the night.” She put her shoulder against his, hard, once, as she had put it into him on a thousand mornings in the practice yard, and took it away. “Sovereign.”

“Paladin,” Arian answered, from the dark.

“He is not to be cold.”

“No.”

Caleb got up. When he looked back from the turn of the street she had begun on the blade again, from the guard.

The mist had come up out of the valley after the sun.

It came up the hill behind them as they climbed, cool and pale and faintly luminous, and by the time they came to the top it had closed round the hill entirely, so that there was nothing in the world but the briars and the iron ribs going up into white, and the white drifting through the ribs as it liked, and no valley below, and no lamps, and no courts, and no sound but the fiddles a long way down, thin and sweet, as a tune is heard through the wall of a neighbour’s house.

Inside, the roses had stayed open.

They stood open all through the glasshouse, hundreds of them, white in the dark, and they smelled of the cool green of rain on a stone sill and of something under it that Caleb knew and could not name, and then could. It was warm glass, and wet earth. There was no other light in the place but his own, going faint and gold along the seams of the black coat, and the stone on his finger turning its slow gold, a sun no larger than a pea held up in the hand of a man in a ruin in the mist, and it was enough to see by.

He turned to Arian in the middle of the roses, where a boy had once lain on his side with his knees drawn up.

Arian was looking at the ring. He had been looking at it all evening, across the feast and the dancing and up the whole of the hill, as a man coming home late looks up at his own roof from the end of the lane.

“May I?” asked Caleb.

Arian’s eyes came up from the ring to his face.

Ever since a chair in a study it had been Arian who asked, from across a room and at a bedside and on the rail of a yacht, every time, as he had been told to, and it came to Caleb, standing in the roses in the mist, that he had asked it only once himself, that afternoon, before both courts and a good many gnomes, and that a great deal of asking was owed.

“Yes,” said Arian.

“With a word after it,” Caleb teased, “if you please. I was promised.”

Arian considered him in the dark of the glasshouse, with the mist moving through the iron over his head, and the white roses round his knees, and the frost on him lit by the ring.

“*Nachdrücklich*.”

Caleb laughed, and the roses nearest him leaned in to the sound, and he was still laughing when he put his warm hands up under the cold silk of the Coast.

He undressed him slowly, because there was no hurry left in the world, and because he had never been allowed to look. The frost came out wherever his hands had been and lay on the white skin, lit by his gold and unmelting, and where the stone on his finger touched it the frost went gold in a ring round the place and kept that too, so that by the time the black silk lay in the roses Arian was marked all over in little suns, as a letter is that has been sealed many times by somebody who wished there to be no doubt at all whose it was. Arian took Caleb’s clothes off him in his turn, with rather less patience.

He found the old seam below the heart, closed in a child’s large earnest stitches, and laid his mouth on it. He found the new one beside it, from the Fault, and laid his mouth on that. Arian stood in the roses and let him, with his head tipped back and his eyes open on the mist going over, and his hands in Caleb’s hair, and the heart under Caleb’s mouth went up.

It went up on its own. It left the time that a boy had counted into it in this house twenty years before, and went high and hard and quick, as Caleb had felt it go only once before down the whole length of a thread, on a night when its owner had made up his mind about something, and he understood with his mouth against it that this, too, was a man making up his mind.

He went with it. For once it was his own heart that kept another’s time.

They lay down in the roses, on the black silk and the coat and the earth, among the white flowers and the thorns, which Caleb noticed and forgave. And when he went into him at last it was slowly, as the first warmth goes down into a stone of the deep trench that has lain in the dark under the sea since before there were kings, cold all through, and the stone takes it, and keeps it, and begins from the heart outward to glow.

His own light lay close enough on Arian’s face to show it, and Caleb looked. He had watched that face give nothing to two courts and the Nine since the first bell. It was giving now. The brows had drawn in a little, as if against a light too bright to look into, and the lips had parted on a breath he did not need, and when Caleb moved the whole of it went with him, helplessly, as still water goes with the first wind across it, and did not try to be still again.

Arian said his name. He said it as he had said it once in a cave, as a thing kept a long while and spent, and the stone on Caleb’s finger flared at it, and the mist outside the iron went gold for a moment all round the top of the hill, so that anybody in the valley below who happened, against the express request of the Consigliere of the Coast, to be looking up, would have seen the glasshouse on the hilltop lit from inside like a lantern in fog, and then dim, and then lit again, slow, and slow, in time with something.

Caleb gave the valley no thought. He thought about the cold arms that held him and the frost that would not melt and the heart going hard under his own, and the white roses all round them leaning in, and then for a while he thought about nothing at all, and neither, he was fairly sure, did the Sovereign of the Obsidian Coast.

Afterwards they lay among the roses with the grey cloak folded under Caleb’s head, because he had wanted it there and Arian had put it there, and the mist went over them through the iron as it pleased.

Arian’s hand lay on Caleb’s chest. The ring on it was cold against his breastbone, and the frost from the palm had gone out over his skin in white ferns that his gold came up through and lit, and under the palm his heart was coming down, slowly, from wherever it had been.

He wept then, for a while, for his brother, without any noise, with his face turned into the cold shoulder. Arian did not ask him anything. The hand on his chest stayed where it was until he had done.

He laid his own hand over the back of Arian’s, with the iron ring on it and its new stone.

“Thank you.”

Arian turned his head on the earth.

“That is one.”

Caleb looked at him.

“You are counting.”

“You stopped at eight. Somebody must keep the book.”

“I shall never catch up.”

“No,” said Arian. “I do not mean to let you.”

Caleb laughed, quietly, so as not to disturb the roses, and turned his face into the cold shoulder, and lay there, and after a while, because he had done it every night for a month and his body had come to expect it, he began to count. He felt for the place without thinking, two fingers at his own wrist, and the other heart went with his under Arian’s palm, and he said the numbers under his breath, *one*, and *two*, as the Vales teach.

“Not tonight,” murmured Arian.

Caleb stopped.

“I will keep it.”

He took Caleb’s wrist in his cold fingers, gently, as a physician takes it, and laid his thumb on the place where a physician counts, and began. He counted aloud, low, in the even unhurried voice of the Coast, into the dark of the glasshouse and the white of the roses and the mist going over, and Caleb lay with his head on a child’s cloak and listened to the numbers go by, kept to the beat a boy of eight had once counted into that same earth, and somewhere about ninety, without in the least meaning to, he was asleep.

He went on after it, in the dark, with his thumb on the pulse and the little sun going round on the hand under his, and the roses white round them, past a hundred, and two hundred, and on to three hundred, because that was what was done.

At three hundred he did not stop. Nobody had asked him to.
