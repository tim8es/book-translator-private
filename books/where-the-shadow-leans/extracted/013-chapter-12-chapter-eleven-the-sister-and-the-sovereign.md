## Chapter 12: Chapter Eleven - The Sister and the Sovereign

*Should the lanterns of the line lean towards a discharge of Solar arms beyond it, the officer commanding shall mark its bearing, report it to the Throne, and await the King’s seal. The officer shall not cross. No cause shall excuse the crossing, however near it lie to the officer’s heart.*

— From the Standing Orders of the Radiant Guard, Article the Ninth, as amended under the Accord

\* \* \*

The back of *Kolyska* kept no hours, and Caleb woke without the least idea whether it was morning.

He knew other things. He knew that his cheek lay on a black silk collar gone stiff with frost, and that the frost had melted in a patch the size of his own face and frozen again round its edges in a fine white crust, like the sugar round the rim of a cup of Lutèce chocolate. He knew that his hand lay flat on his coat, over the ring, where it had gone to sleep every night since Dornhöhle, and that at some hour of the night a great deal of shadow had come and settled on top of it, soft and heavy as a fleece, and had no intention of getting up. And he knew, before he had opened his eyes, that the shoulder he had gone to sleep on had not moved all night, because it had not moved in the carriage in the pass either, and it was plainly a matter of principle.

He lay still and listened to the dark. The river was there, faint through the rock. Nearer than the river was something that made no sound at all. His cheek had slipped in the night from the shoulder to the breast, and through the frost and the silk he could feel a heart going on at its own unhurried pace, so near the pace of his own that he would have had to hold his breath to tell them apart. He let them run together, and did not count.

“Good morning,” said Arian, out of the dark just above his head, low and level and entirely awake.

“Is it?” asked Caleb, without moving. “I have no window and no sun, and I suspect the shepherds tell the time in here by the sheep.”

“The sun reached the rim an hour ago.”

“You cannot possibly know that.”

“The sergeant’s men have begun to sing. Go into the sun, Caleb.”

High on the wall above them the old glass stirred along its whole length, faintly, as a sleeper’s face changes at a name spoken in the next room, and went brown again and lay still.

Caleb sat up rather quickly. The shadows slid off his coat as he went and pooled in his lap, offended. He brushed them away, which did nothing whatever, and got to his knees on the fleece, and found that his ears had gone hot to the tips. In the dark he could see nothing of Arian but the frost, which lay along his shoulders and through his hair and over his knuckles in a faint grey glitter, like the bloom on a sloe.

“You did that on purpose.”

“I said your name.”

“Then you will say it less often, in front of the glass.”

“No.”

Caleb went out to find the sun with his face burning and no answer to that whatever, which he was beginning to understand was going to be the general condition of his life.

\* \* \*

The sun lay along the top of the north wall like a line of gilt along the edge of a page, and had not yet come down as far as the cave. So Caleb went up the goat track in the cold with his hands under his arms to meet it, and came out onto the road just as the light came down onto it, and stood there, and let it come.

It came into the Fault from the top, as water comes into a jug, filling the gorge from the brim downwards. It found the stripes of the north wall one after another and turned each to its true colour as it reached it, and when it reached the road the frost along the ledge went up all together in a thin white steam, as if the road had been holding its breath all night and had only now been told it might let it go.

The prints went up with it.

Caleb had not known they were still there until he saw them go. They lay all along the ledge in the frost, from the buttress to the place where the circle had stood, the long clawed prints of lycan feet running and turning and standing still, and a place by the rock where somebody had knelt, set out on the black stone as plainly as the steps a dancing master chalks on a ballroom floor for pupils who will not come again. Every print faded in the sun from its edges inward, as a breath fades off cold glass, and was gone. In the hill towns there would be suppers kept warm tonight for the men who had made them, and tomorrow night, and then not. When the steam had cleared the road was only a road, wet and black, and there was nothing on it to say that anybody had ever stood there at all.

Then the light reached Caleb, and found his face, and then his hands.

His palms stung as it went into them. It was the sting of a numbed foot coming back to itself by a fire, a thousand pins and every one of them welcome. The warmth that had returned to them in the dark lay there still at the bottom of each hand, and the sun went down into it and stood beside it, and it grew. It did not grow much. He could feel the bottom of it, as one feels the bottom of a purse with one’s fingers. But it grew.

Along the road, past the buttress, the sergeant’s men had nearly finished. The first carriage lay under the last of the fallen rock with its black lacquer split from end to end and one iron wheel turned up to the sky. Six lycans had their bars under the last great boulder, moving the mountain a hand’s breadth at a time and singing to it so that it would not take offence. It was a slow Wildmarch song, and every few verses they all leaned on their bars on the same word, and the boulder groaned and went a little nearer the edge. Caleb found a rock in the sun and set it all down in his notebook.

When the boulder went over the edge at last the whole of the escort cheered, and the sergeant straightened, and saw Caleb on his rock in the sun, and lifted his iron bar to him across a quarter of a mile of air, as a man lifts his cup across a hall.

Caleb lifted his pencil.

\* \* \*

By the time he came back down the goat track the sun had found the mouth of *Kolyska*.

It lay across the shepherds’ hearth like a length of yellow silk unrolled across a draper’s counter, and the lycans of the escort lay in it like hounds before a fire, with their wounds bound up in strips of their own shirts, and the blood come through the linen and dried there in brown roses. Mirek had been carried out to the edge of it on his straw, with his lace-bound leg propped before him in state, and was teaching the wounded nearest him the word *prächtig*, with some success. Paul sat apart with his back to the rock, where the light ended. On a shelf of stone above the hearth, beside the shepherds’ clogs and well out of everybody’s reach, lay a long bundle of grey velvet tied with twine. Even in the full sun Caleb could see the glow coming through the pile in faint gold seams, and nobody in the cave was sitting near it.

He went past them all into the dark, and found Arian writing.

He sat where Caleb had left him, under the vein of glass, with the writing case from the second carriage open across his knees, and his pen moved over black paper in silver ink, steadily, with no light to guide it but the faint gold Caleb had brought in with him, which lay on the stone for a yard round his boots and no further. The ink was the Coast’s own. It was ground from the pearl of the black oysters in the strait and called silver only for its colour, and there was no metal in it at all.

“You are writing in the dark,” he said.

“It is not dark.”

“It is to the rest of us.”

Arian finished his line, and laid down the pen.

He folded the sheet twice with the long white fingers that had been blistered to the nails in yesterday’s sun. The blisters had gone down to a faint red roughness, like a washerwoman’s hands in winter. Then he set the ball of his right thumb against one of his own teeth, and pressed.

A drop came up, nearly black in the dark, and stood on the white of his thumb like a bead of ink on a quill. He laid it on the fold of the letter where the edges met. Then he closed his left hand on the air above it, slowly, as a man closes his hand on a moth, and drew it down.

The shadow came with his hand. It came off the dark of the chamber as thread comes off a spindle and went down into the drop, and the drop drank it and spread and rose, and set, black and hard and glossy as jet, in a round seal the width of a crown piece. It held the letter shut as well as any wax. And on its face there was nothing at all.

Every seal Caleb had ever seen bore something pressed into it by a ring, a sun or a crest or a beast, so that anyone who broke it would know whose word they were breaking. This one gave his light back to him like a pupil, and kept its owner’s name to itself.

*Any of the Nine may stand up and contest whatever he seals*, Maartje had warned on the bench, with her ledger in her hands. *And call for a Leaning on it, and let the shadows decide.*

The ring lay against Caleb’s ribs a hand’s breadth from the letter it ought to have sealed, and he kept his hand at his side.

“To your uncle?” he asked.

“Yes.”

“May I ask what it says?”

“That you are unharmed.” Arian laid the letter on the stone beyond the edge of the gold without looking round, and the sergeant, whom Caleb had not heard come in, came out of the passage and took it up. “The waystation at the rim. The Consigliere’s own hand.”

The sergeant’s eyes went to the black seal as he tucked it into his coat, and stayed there a breath longer than they needed to before he went.

Caleb sat down on the fleece, meaning to sit at a decent distance, and found that his shoulder was against Arian’s again and that he had no intention of moving it.

“I owe you a morning.” He opened his notebook.

He read it to the dark, the boulder going over the edge, and the deep cold boom that came up out of the Fault a long while afterwards when it went through the ice into the river, like a word arriving late to a conversation. Then he read the song. Mirek, who had a little of the Wildmarch from his mother and had been consulted on the way out, said that it was about a lycan who married a millstone, and that it ended well, being a lycan song, and that the millstone was very patient. Arian made a sound he had made once before, on a balcony over the forges, low in his chest, and stopped it.

“It is not funny,” said Caleb severely. “It is a tragedy. She was very patient. Do vampires have songs about millstones?”

“We have one. About a bell that wished to be a lamp. It is very long, and it does not end well.”

Caleb laughed, which was hard on the bell, and the shadows along the walls stirred at it and leaned in.

But Arian had turned his head towards the passage and gone still, as he had gone still in the carriage on the Fault road a heartbeat before the mountain fell.

“There is someone on the track.”

“One of the sergeant’s men.”

“No.” Arian listened to something none of the rest of them could hear. “Not a lycan. It is one of yours.”

Caleb had already felt it.

It had risen through him from the soles of his feet, leaning all one way towards the mouth of the cave. It was a single light, coming down the north wall of the Fault fast and without the least care for its neck, and he knew it as he had known every morning of his childhood which of his sisters was coming up the nursery stair, by the sound of the boots on the treads, before the door opened.

He was on his feet before he had decided to stand.

A voice rang down the passage from the mouth of the cave.

“*Walker.*”

It was a voice made for the far end of a parade ground, and it went through *Kolyska* like a struck bar of iron. Mirek let out a high astonished yelp, and after it, low, came Paul’s voice.

“Paladin.”

“Report.”

“*Tilda*,” breathed Caleb.

Behind him, Arian rose. He did it with one hand flat against the rock, and Caleb heard what it cost him in the scrape of his boot on the stone, and turned.

“Sit down.”

“No.”

“You can barely stand.”

“Then I will barely stand,” said Arian, and stood.

He stood straight, with his back to the wall under the vein of glass, and the shadows came to him from the walls of the chamber as they always came. But they came slowly this morning, grey and thin, as old dogs come to the door at the master’s step, as if they had been up all night as well and had not yet forgiven anyone for it.

Out in the passage there was a short sharp sound, a lycan’s growl cut off in the middle, and the sergeant’s voice, low and hard, and then the voice like a bar of iron, not raised at all now, which was worse.

“Stand aside, Sergeant, or be stood aside. I have no time this morning to do it politely.”

Then the light came.

It came along the passage ahead of her, as the light of a carried candle comes down a cellar stair before the one who carries it. The dark at the far end of the chamber went pale, and then gold, and then a hard bright gold that stood up every grain in the rock, and the shadows that had come so slowly to Arian went back to the walls all at once, flat and thin, like ink drawn back into a pen, and the cold of the chamber went with them.

Matilda came in under the low roof with her sword drawn.

She had to stoop to clear the rock, and she kept the sword up to do it. She came through with the point level before her, as if the roof were an enemy too, and straightened on the far side, and stood.

She had ridden a long way. Caleb saw it in her boots before anything else, because she had raised him to look first at the horse and the boots. They were black with wet to the knee and white above it with the salt of melted snow, her white cloak had gone the grey of a road from the hem to the hips, and her mail had been wet and dried and wet again until the frost had left tide marks on it. Her plait had come half undone. There was a fresh scratch along one cheekbone where a branch had caught her in the dark. And the gold was up in her, all of it, from the collar of her mail to the backs of her bare hands, so bright that the scratch seemed to have been drawn in shadow, and her light lay on the round walls of the back of *Kolyska* as the sun lies on the floor of a threshing barn when both doors are thrown open at harvest.

She saw Caleb.

It went through her as a blow goes through a bell, and she stood quite still and let it ring, and it was a long time ringing. The point of the sword did not move.

Then she saw what stood behind him.

“Caleb. Get behind me.”

“Good morning, Tilda.”

“Behind me. Now.”

He took one step the other way, to his left, and stood where he had been standing, only a little more so, with his back to the Sovereign of the Obsidian Coast and his face to his sister’s sword.

The point came up to his breastbone and stopped there.

Behind him he could hear the frost going off Arian’s shoulders and hair in a long faint hiss, as water hisses on a hot stone. He looked round. Arian stood against the rock with his face turned to the light and the steam rising off him into the glare in a thin grey veil, and along his cheekbones, where the burns of the Fault had faded in the night to the pink of a healing scald, the pink had gone back to red.

“Tilda,” protested Caleb. “You are burning him.”

“Good.”

“High Paladin,” said Arian, from behind Caleb’s shoulder, in the voice he used to the Council of Nine, as one names a guest at the door of a hall.

“Sovereign.”

The point did not move by a hair.

Caleb knew that sword as he knew the weight of his own arm. It had knocked him flat on the sand of the practice yard and hung over his nose afterwards while he lay there and considered his life, and he knew the nick in its edge a hand’s breadth from the point that she had got on a Vesperian raider’s axe at twenty and never let the armourer grind out. He laid his palm on the steel below the point, as a groom lays a hand on the neck of a horse that has been asked to stand.

“Tilda. I am perfectly well.”

“You are in a hole in a mountain with a vampire.”

“It is a very good hole. The shepherds bring their ewes here to lamb. There are drawings of rams on the wall in the first chamber, by somebody who loved them a great deal better than he could draw them, and I shall show them to you as soon as you have stopped pointing that at me.”

“Step away from him.”

“No.”

“Caleb.”

“No,” he repeated, and then, because she had taught him that a man who means to hold a line ought to say so plainly and in as few words as the thing allows, “I shall stand here.”

Matilda went still.

This was the stillness of a soldier who has heard her own name called from a crowd by a voice she cannot place, and turned, and found nobody there. Something moved in her face and went out of it again before he could see what it was, and the light in her skin went down all over her, as a lamp goes down when the wick is turned and nobody has meant to turn it. The round chamber went from blazing gold to a deep warm amber, and at Caleb’s back the hiss of the frost faltered, and stopped.

She did not lower the sword.

“Sovereign,” she said. Her voice had not changed at all. “Have you harmed him?”

“Yes.”

The sword came up off Caleb’s breastbone by the width of two fingers, towards the man behind him, and Caleb’s hand went with it on the steel and held it, and the light went up in her again in a single rush from her collar to her crown, and the steam came off Arian’s shoulders in a cloud.

Caleb heard the skin go. It made a faint wet crackle, like fat catching at the edge of a pan, and when he looked round the red along Arian’s cheekbones had lifted in blisters, and the left one had split, and a dark thread was running from it down the line of his jaw into his collar. Arian did not raise a hand to it. He stood against the rock in the glare and burned, quite still, and looked past Caleb’s shoulder at the woman who was burning him. And on the wall behind him, where her light had thrown it, his shadow had come away from the stone.

It was the only shadow in the chamber she had not pinned. Every other lay flat to the walls, thin as paper. His stood out from the rock as thick as a winter curtain, blacker than any cave could have made it alone, and it leaned towards her, into the light, as no shadow in the world has any business to lean. Its right hand was coming up, slowly, though the hand at Arian’s side had not moved at all.

Caleb had seen that dark go along a ledge two nights ago. An hour since, he had watched the sun take the last of what it left behind. Up on the rim at this moment Sir Hugh Ashby sat his horse in the road with twenty of the Radiant Guard at his back, and here before him stood his sister with her light up and her sword at his breastbone, and the man behind him could barely stand, and Caleb did not think it would take him one breath longer for that.

“He means,” said Caleb, fast and clear, as he had once spoken across a Council table to stop a war over a snowdrift, “that he frightened me once, on the Coast, a great while ago, when I was dying and he was in a hurry, and he has never forgiven himself for it and does not intend to start. I have since frightened him a great deal worse, in this cave, yesterday morning. We are quits. Tilda. We are quits.”

Matilda looked over his shoulder, at the man against the rock and at the thing on the wall behind him. “Is that true?”

Caleb, with his back to it, could not see what Arian’s face did in the pause, and would have given a great deal to.

“It is true,” Arian answered, “that he has frightened me.”

At Caleb’s back the cold drew in. He did not need to look round to know that the shadow had gone down into the stone again, as a hand goes back into a sleeve.

And from down the passage, through the turnings of the rock, high and cracked and furious, came another voice entirely.

“He took a sun-knife for His Highness, Paladin! I saw it from the wheel!” There was a scuffle, and a hiss of pain, and Paul’s voice, low and urgent, and the high voice coming on over it regardless. “A lycan went for His Highness with a singing one, and the Sovereign got in front of it, and it went in him and broke, and he never even —” The voice cracked, and rallied. “And then he put out all the stars.”

There was a silence in the back chamber of *Kolyska*.

“Who said that?” asked Matilda, to the passage.

“Mirek, Paladin.” A breath, through teeth. “Footman. I’ve a broken leg.”

Matilda turned her head back slowly, and Caleb watched her look at the man behind him as she looked at ground, or at weather.

“He put out the stars,” she said flatly.

“Briefly.”

Caleb made a sound. It came up like a hiccup and he shut his mouth on it, hard, and stood holding his sister’s sword with his shoulders shaking, while behind him a Sovereign of the Umbral houses stood against the rock with the steam drying off him and said nothing at all, in a manner Caleb could only call pointed.

Matilda did not laugh. But she took her left hand off the hilt of her sword, and reached out, and took Caleb’s hand off the blade, as she had taken it off a hundred things in the nursery that he was not supposed to touch, and held it.

He saw her feel it. She had never had the gift, and had come out of her seasons in the Harp Vales able to set a bone in the field and nothing more. But she had been holding his hands since he was a year old, and she knew that a healer’s hands are warm. His lay in her gold palm like two stones out of the river.

“Your hands are cold.”

“Yes.”

“You have been spent.”

“Yes.”

“On him.”

“Yes,” said Caleb.

Matilda looked past the cold hand in her own, at the man against the rock.

“Why?”

She asked it of her brother, plainly, as she had asked him, standing over him with the sand of the yard on his back, why he had dropped his guard, as if there might be a reason and she wished to hear it before she put him down again.

“Because he would have died.”

“And?”

“And I did not wish him to.”

Matilda stood with his hand in hers in the amber of her own banked light, and for a long time said nothing.

“The last time your hands were this cold,” she said at last, “you were eight years old, and I carried you down a hill. At Old Meridian. You do not remember. You were lying in the street by that dry fountain with the faceless stone woman in it, and I thought you were dead. You were so cold I could not feel where you stopped and the stone began. I carried you all the way down to the tents inside my cloak, and you did not wake for three days, and Mother said it was a chill in the mist.” She closed her hand on his. “I was not so frightened again for twenty years. Not until the morning they told us you had gone on a pilgrimage.”

Caleb could not speak.

He had remembered a voice in the mist from the glasshouse door, calling his name, and running towards it with his elbows out, and the white street tipping past him sideways, and then nothing. He had never known who came after the nothing.

Behind him, against the rock, Arian had gone still. Caleb knew without any bridge between them that the man at his back had heard that voice too, twenty years ago, from among the roses, and had kept it, as he kept everything.

*She was the one calling*, Caleb thought.

And behind him, in the silence, he felt rather than heard Arian breathe out.

Matilda let go of his hand. She stepped back one pace and sheathed her sword in one long movement that ended with a click of the guard against the throat of the scabbard, like a door shut on a cold night. The amber steadied. In it, along Arian’s shoulders and in his black hair, the frost began to come back a grain at a time, as rime comes back on a windowpane when the fire in the room has been let down.

“Sit down, Sovereign. I do not fight a man who has to hold up a wall to stand.”

“When the High Paladin sits.”

Matilda considered him. Then she unclasped her filthy cloak at the throat and dropped it on the fleece, and sat down on it in her mail with a sound like a sack of horseshoes being set down on a cobbled yard, and laid her sheathed sword across her knees.

Arian sat, slowly, with his hand flat on the rock behind him all the way down. Then the Coast’s Sovereign and Aloria’s High Paladin sat facing each other across two yards of shepherds’ fleece in the back of a cave that smelled powerfully of sheep, both of them bolt upright, each with a hand resting near something sharp, like the two oldest guests at a wedding supper who have been seated together on purpose.

Caleb sat down between them on the fleece, cross-legged, which no prince of the Solar House did on any floor in company, and folded his cold hands in his lap.

“Sovereign, may I present my eldest sister, Her Royal Highness the Princess Matilda, High Paladin of the Radiant Guard, who taught me everything I know about falling down. Tilda, the Sovereign of the Obsidian Coast. He took a blade for me in the Fault the night before last, as Mirek has been good enough to tell the whole cave, and all but died of it, and I have been up to my elbows in him ever since, and you smell, as I always knew you would, of horse.”

“You smell of sheep.”

“Everything here smells of sheep. The Sovereign smells of sheep. You will smell of sheep yourself within the hour. It is a condition of entry.”

Arian said nothing. But the vein of glass above his head, which nobody had addressed, went from brown to honey, briefly, and back.

Matilda looked up at it. Then she looked at Arian, who was regarding the passage as if something there had caught his interest, and at Caleb, whose ears had gone hot again.

“My scouts saw that. At noon yesterday it stood in the north wall of the Fault for an hour like a gold wire. That was how I knew where to come. And last night after dark it came up again, all along its length, bright enough to read a dispatch by from the far rim, Ashby said. Just for a moment.” She looked at the vein, and back at her brother. “As if someone had said something to it.”

Caleb could think of nothing whatever to say.

“It was the lanterns that brought me over the line,” Matilda went on. “The Guard’s lanterns are second-yield stones, and second-yield answers to Aether as a hound answers to the horn. An hour before dawn, the morning before yesterday, every lantern in my camp leaned south at once and burned white. Ashby counted twelve points of light along the southern sky from the high picket. Then, all at once, none, as if someone had shut a book on them.”

“Someone did,” said Caleb.

“So I gathered.” She was looking at Arian. “I marked the bearing, as the standing orders say. I wrote the report and gave it to the fastest rider in the camp, and told him to take the long road to Chalmont and rest his horse often. Then I took twenty of the Guard and Sir Hugh Ashby, and we crossed the line at first light.”

“You crossed the line,” said Arian.

“Twenty is a patrol. Twenty is not a war. I left every banner I have on our own side of the Pas de la Dame, where anyone who cares to may count them. Ashby is holding the road above you, and I would not advise anyone to be in his way if he comes down it.” She turned her head a little towards Caleb without taking her eyes from Arian. “I stood on that line forty-four days. I counted.”

“I know you did,” murmured Caleb.

“Then you know what it cost me to cross it.” She turned back to Arian. “What lit twelve suns in the Fault, Sovereign?”

Arian looked at her across the fleece, and then past her, towards the lambing pens.

“Sergeant,” he called, not loudly.

The sergeant’s voice came back from the far end of the passage. “Sovereign.”

“Bring the rug to the pens.”

\* \* \*

The sergeant’s lamp hung on its peg on the hurdle of Mirek’s pen, shuttered to a glow that lay through the hazel withies in warm gold lozenges on the straw, like the light through a lattice at a cottage window. Mirek lay under it with his face as white as a peeled withy and his chin up. He had dragged himself on his elbows to the head of the passage to shout down it, and Paul had carried him back. When Matilda came through the hurdles he saluted lying down, two fingers to his forehead, as he had seen the lycans of the roofs salute their sergeant.

Matilda stopped at the foot of his bed and looked down at his leg in its two hazel staves and its bands of Alorian lace, like a maypole put away for the winter. She dropped to one knee in the straw and tried the binding at the knee with two fingers, and ran her thumb down the line of the staves from end to end, as Caleb had seen her run it down the foreleg of a horse she was thinking of buying.

“Who set this?” she asked.

“His Highness, Paladin. With his hands. He wouldn’t use the light on me. I told him not to.”

“With lace.”

“It was all I had,” protested Caleb, from the hurdle. “I gave the rest of my shirt to the cause.”

Matilda tried the knee once more, and sat back on her heel.

“It is a good splint.”

Mirek went red to the ears. Caleb, who had been told by his eldest sister on perhaps three occasions in twenty-eight years that something he had done was good, and had kept every one of them, went rather red himself.

“Paladin.” Mirek fumbled the dwarven knife in its case of horn out of his belt and held it up to her in his fist. “Do you know what the ninth is for? The dwarf wouldn’t say.”

Matilda took it with the faint suspicion she kept for every clever folding thing that had not been made for killing anything, and opened the blades one after another with her thumbnail until she came to the ninth. It was short and broad, with a point that stood out from the edge at a right angle, like a thorn on a stem.

“A fleam. For bleeding horses.”

Mirek gazed at it, and then at her. “*Prächtig*,” he breathed.

“It is not,” said Matilda, and closed the knife, and gave it back to him, and stood.

The sergeant came in from the sun with the grey velvet rug held out at arm’s length, and the gold came through the pile in seams and lay along his sleeves and his grey-flecked jaw. Arian had stopped in the passage behind them, at the edge of the lamp’s reach, and came no further. Caleb saw Matilda see where he had stopped, and measure the distance, as she measured a river before she put a horse at it.

“Unroll it, Sergeant,” said Arian.

The sergeant knelt and cut the twine and laid the rug open across the straw of the next pen, as a pedlar lays out his cloth in a market.

The pens went to noon.

The blades lay quiet on the velvet with no hand to draw them, and their light was the light of things asleep. But it was still the light that had pinned every shadow in the Fault to the walls like washing, and it came up off the grey velvet white along their rims and gold within, and the lamp on its peg went pale and foolish beside it. Mirek put his arm over his eyes. Behind them in the dark Caleb heard the frost on Arian’s shoulders begin, faintly, to hiss, and then heard him take a single step back into the passage, and the hiss stopped.

There were ten blades on the velvet and a broken hilt, crossed over one another like spills tipped out of a jar, and the broken one lay a little apart, a black iron hilt with nothing in its claws but a stub of dull grey glass no longer than a thumb. The others were warm. Caleb could feel them on his face from the hurdle. The gold in his skin, which had come back to him so slowly in the sun of the morning, went towards them like a child to a window, and he held it back with the whole of his will.

Matilda knelt and took up the nearest by its hilt, lightly and without ceremony, which was how she held everything with an edge, in case it should be asked to do something at any moment. The light ran up her arm to the elbow. She looked at the blade. Then she looked at the hilt, and was a long time looking.

Caleb had seen the hilts in the Fault, in the frost, and something in the cutting of the runes had snagged in him and stayed snagged. He had not let himself think about them since, which was not the same as not thinking about them. He watched his sister turn the hilt so that the gold ran along the square dwarven runes, and he watched her thumb go to the end of one long stroke. No rune in Dornhöhle had been cut as that one was. The cold-iron there turned up at its tail in a little curl, like the end of a fern before it opens.

Her thumb stayed there.

Then, without letting go of the black hilt, Matilda put her other hand to her own sword and drew it a foot out of the scabbard, and turned it, so that the guard came into the light beside the black iron.

Caleb had looked at that guard all his life. It was plain broad steel, cut along both its arms with a line of square runes in the dwarven style, which he had seen come down on him a thousand times in the practice yard, and had never once thought to ask her to read him. At the end of every long stroke of every rune, the steel turned up at its tail in the same glad little curl.

They lay side by side in the light, the black iron and the bright steel, and the same hand had cut them both.

Nobody in the pens said anything. At the hurdle of the next pen, where he had stood since he carried Mirek back from the passage, Paul had put his hand on the hazel and was holding it, as a man holds a gatepost in a high wind.

“Cecily,” said Matilda.

She said it quite quietly, to nobody.

“She cut them for me when I was given my first sword of the Guard,” she said, to the two hilts in the light. “She was thirteen. She had a book of the dwarven runes out of the Treasury library that she was not supposed to have, and a chisel she was not supposed to have either. She asked me what I wanted on it, and I said my name. She said a sword ought to say something. So she cut my name, and under it she cut the word she said I said most. She was very pleased with the tails. She said the dwarves made their runes look as if they had been told off by somebody, and these were to look glad.” She turned the black hilt in her fingers, and the curls along it caught the gold one after another. “The armourer said they would weaken the guard. I told him the guard would hold. It has.”

“What is the word?” asked Caleb, in a voice he hardly knew.

Matilda did not look up.

“*Again*.”

She slid her own sword home. Then she laid the black hilt down among the others with great care, as one sets down a thing belonging to someone else in a room where one is a guest, and took her hand away from it, and closed the hand, and opened it again.

“Sergeant,” she said, without turning. “Who carried these?”

“Ashward men, Paladin.” The sergeant’s voice was rough. “Rogues out of the hill towns. The Don’s grandson came up through the hill towns in the spring with a purse, and I knew two of the men who drank it.” He looked at the broken hilt. “That one was meant for His Highness, and the Sovereign took it. It sang.”

“And the men?”

“Gone, Paladin. Every one.” The sergeant looked down at his own hands. “The dark went over them on the ledge, and where there was a man there was frost. The sun had their footprints this morning.”

Matilda turned her head and looked into the passage. Arian stood in the dark a step beyond the lamp’s reach, and had not so much as turned his head. He was looking at the broken hilt.

Matilda looked back at the cold grey stub in its black claws, the only thing on the velvet that gave off no light at all.

“Walker.”

Paul came up off the hurdle as if it had burned him. The blades lit his face from below, white and gold, and found as little colour there as they had on the ledge of the Fault.

“Paladin.”

“Where were you?”

“In the last carriage, Paladin. With the baggage. As the Accord says.” His voice was steady as a wall is steady with a great weight of water behind it. “I came as fast as I could. I was too slow.”

In the dark of the passage Arian’s head turned a little towards Paul, as it had turned that morning towards a heart coming down a goat track at a run. He was listening, with his whole stillness, and his face at the edge of the lamplight told Caleb nothing at all.

Matilda was looking at Paul as a commander looks at a man of her own who has come in off a bad patrol.

“You kept him alive.”

Paul’s face did something terrible and brief, and was still.

“His Highness kept himself alive, Paladin. Him and the Sovereign. I carried things.”

“Then you carried them well.” She considered him. “Your mother. Is her cough better?”

“Better, Paladin.” Paul’s eyes went down to the straw at her feet. “So I’m told.”

Matilda nodded once, as if that settled it, and turned away, and did not see his knuckles go white on the hurdle. Caleb saw. He had nursed men in the Vales who would not be praised for a field they had not been quick enough to save, and he made up his mind to make Paul take the second cup of tea every morning from here to the Coast, whether he liked the tea or not.

The sergeant had begun to roll up the rug.

“Those come with me to Castel Nerezza,” said Arian, from the dark.

Matilda turned on her heel. “They are Alorian.”

“They were drawn on me,” Arian answered, “for the coin of a house of the Coast.”

“My sister is not a house of the Coast.”

“No. She is not.”

They looked at each other across the lit straw, the white mail and the black coat, and the blades’ light lay between them on the velvet, gold, with nobody to hold it.

“Let him keep them, Tilda.” She looked round at him. “If you carry one of those north, there is one man in Aloria who will know what you know the moment he sees it in your hand. And he will know what Cecily knows, and what she might say, and to whom. And then he will go down to her laboratory.” He held his sister’s eyes. “Let the Coast keep them, where nobody of ours can see them. Please.”

He saw her hear what he had not said, and whose name he had not said it with, and he saw her decide not to say it either, in front of a vampire and a lycan sergeant and a boy with a broken leg and her own man at the hurdle.

“Roll them up, Sergeant.”

The light went back into the velvet seam by seam, as the light under a door draws back when someone inside has carried the lamp away, and the lamp came back to itself. At a nod from Arian the sergeant carried the bundle away towards the sun with his face turned from it, and the gold went with him round the turn of the rock.

“Walk with me,” Matilda said to Caleb.

“Go into the sun, Caleb,” said Arian.

Matilda looked at him, standing in the mouth of the passage with his face turned a little aside, as if the hazel withies of the lambing pens were of the deepest interest to him. Then she looked at her brother.

Caleb took her by the elbow of her mail and walked her out through the hurdles towards the sun, with his ears burning and Mirek making a noise behind them in the straw that he would deal with later.

\* \* \*

He took her down to the river.

There was a path, if one were generous, and the shepherds had been generous with it for a great many years. It went down from *Kolyska* in a long slant across the north wall, and turned back on itself at a dead thorn tree hung all over with tufts of fleece where the ewes had pushed past it, like a tree at a lycan wedding hung with ribbons, and came down at last onto a shelf of flat grey rock at the water’s edge. The sun lay on the shelf and on the ice along the margin, and the noise of the river filled the bottom of the Fault from wall to wall, so loud that Caleb had to stand close to his sister to be heard at all.

He had chosen it for that. He did not know whether a river was enough against a Sovereign’s hearing, but he thought it might be, if they kept their heads together, and if the man in the cave above them were a gentleman, which Caleb had every reason to believe he was, to his very great inconvenience.

Matilda sat down on a rock in the sun, all at once and without any fuss, with her sword across her knees. The spray came over the edge of the ice in a fine glittering mist and froze as it fell, and settled on her loosened plait in a dust of white, and the gold in her skin had gone down so far that it did not melt.

Caleb sat down beside her.

“He could have killed me,” said Matilda, to the river. “In the cave. And Ashby on the road, and the twenty, and the horses.”

“Yes.”

“I told him to sit down because he could not stand.” She turned the sword a little on her knees, so that the light ran along the nick in its edge. “Why did he not?”

Caleb looked up the long slant of the path to the mouth of *Kolyska*, which was dark, and gave nothing back.

“I was standing in the way.”

Matilda looked at him a long moment, as she had looked at him across the practice yard on the morning he first held his ground and took the blow on the shield instead of the shoulder. Then she looked back at the water.

“Take off your left boot,” he said.

Matilda did not turn her head. “Why?”

“Because you have been walking on it since you came into the cave as if it belonged to somebody else and you had only borrowed it for the morning.”

“It is perfectly well.”

“Then you will not mind showing it to me.”

He held out his hand and waited, and she knew the look, because he had learned it from her.

She unlaced the boot. Two toes on the left foot were the colour of tallow, waxen and cold to the knuckle, with a faint grey bloom at their tips where the frost had gone in deepest. Caleb took the foot into his lap without asking, as he would have done for any soldier off any pass in the world, and Matilda let him, which told him more than the toes did.

“How long?” he asked.

“Since the second week.”

“And you told nobody.”

“There was nobody to tell.” She looked at the river. “You were not there.”

There was so little in his palms that he could have carried it across a room in one of them, like the last water in a cracked bowl. But it was warm, and it was his, and he let it go down into his sister’s foot, slowly, as one breathes on a child’s cold fingers on a winter walk. He felt it find the frozen places and stand in them, and the grey at the tips give a little under it, like ice at the edge of a puddle when the sun first finds it.

Matilda drew in a breath through her teeth. “That hurts.”

“Good. It means they are still yours. I should have had to take them off, otherwise, and I have nothing in my case but a dwarven fleam.”

She laughed.

It came out of her short and sudden, like a bark, and she clapped a hand over her mouth at once as if it had escaped through a door she had left open. He had been the only creature in Aloria who could make her do it since he was old enough to talk, and he had not made her do it in nine weeks, and it went through him like the first mouthful of something hot.

He held the toes until the tallow had gone to pink, and the pink to an angry red, and she had begun under her breath to swear at him in the language of the stables. Then he wrapped the foot in the tail of his own coat, and kept it in his lap.

“Tilda,” he said.

She was still smiling, a little, at the river. She turned her head.

“I lent Andrew my signet.”

The river went by, white and loud between its shelves of ice. Caleb watched the smile go out of her face slowly, as the last of the light goes off a hillside at evening, from the bottom up. The gold in her skin went lower, until there was none left anywhere at all, and she sat on her rock in the full sun of the Fault as grey as the rock itself, with the frost settling on her hair and staying there. Her hand had gone to the hilt of her sword, and it did not close.

“When?”

“In the spring.” He found he could say it quite steadily, now that the first part was said. “He came to supper in my rooms, which he did not often do, and we talked about nothing, and at the end of it he asked if he might borrow my signet. There was a wine merchant in the Marches, he said, who gave better terms to a prince than to a prince’s steward, and he wanted a cask of something for Father’s birthday, and he did not want it known it came from him.” He looked at the river. “I slid it off and dropped it in his hand, and laughed. I had never refused him anything in my life. It never occurred to me that there was anything to refuse.”

Matilda said nothing.

“And then Father came to my rooms,” said Caleb, “which he never did, and stood at the window, and told me my signet had been found at a vineyard in the Marches among the dead. I waited for him to say what came next, and nothing came next, except Mother’s chamberlain with a cup of wine.” He turned his cold hands over on her wrapped foot. “I have spent nine weeks telling myself that Andrew gave it back, and I lost it. I lose everything. Gloves. Hours. The names of minor ambassadors. I told myself so every night in the east wing, and in this cave yesterday, with my hands in a man who had been stabbed with a blade out of the Citadel.” He stopped. “I have never once said it aloud. Not to anyone. Not to myself. I lent Andrew my signet, Tilda, and he went to the Sanctuary with it, and forty-one people died there among the vines, and they found my ring in a dead woman’s hand.”

He made himself go on, because there was more, and he had not said that aloud either.

“Her name was Livia. She kept the Sanctuary. She was the Consigliere’s wife, and when the Sovereign’s mother died bearing him it was she who raised him. There is a room cut in the rock under Castel Nerezza where the Contis keep a flame for each of their dead, and hers is the newest. I have knelt in front of it.” His voice went thin, and he let it. “Her husband told me she liked anyone who ate. He said she would have liked me.”

He had never been within fifty leagues of the Sanctuary, and every night in the east wing he had seen it all the same, with his eyes open on the dark, the vines going up along their wires, each row catching from the last as the leaves of a book catch one from another in a grate, and the grapes splitting on the stem and running down in the heat, and the people in the rows with their hair alight, falling, and the fire going on over them as if they were only more of the vines, and somewhere in the ash a white hand closed hard on his name, which he had slid off his own finger across a supper table and dropped into his brother’s palm.

He had laughed.

It was said. It lay on the rock between them in the sun, and the river went over it, and did not wash it away.

Matilda closed her eyes. The spray went on settling on her lashes and her bare knuckles and the steel across her knees, until the blade went grey with frost from guard to point while he watched, like a window on a winter morning.

“Eleanor said it,” she said. “In my tent, with her ledgers all over the table. *He sent Caleb in his place, Tilda.*” Her voice was level. “I believed her. I have believed her every day since. I stood in the snow and believed her, and did not believe her, both at once, as you believe a scout who comes in and tells you the bridge behind you is down, and go on looking over your shoulder for it all the same.” She opened her eyes. “Now you have said it.”

“Yes.”

“Then it is so.”

She put her hand inside the collar of her mail and felt along the quilted lining over her heart until her fingers found what they were looking for. Then she drew her knife, a plain Guard knife with a horn grip worn pale, and slid its point in under the collar, and cut.

He heard the stitches go, big and square and close, in coarse grey thread, the stitches of a soldier who has mended her own harness by a camp fire since she was fifteen and sees no reason why paper should be treated any better. She drew out a fold of thin sheets, warm from her body, and as she drew them out a narrow strip folded twice and twice again slipped from between them and fell on the rock.

Caleb was nearer. He had it before she did, out of nothing but a lifetime of being nearer, and unfolded it on his knee before he had thought whether he ought.

It was her hand, square and upright, every letter standing to attention, the hand in which she had written him notes since the nursery that said *Again* and *Better* and once, when he was twelve and his wrist was broken, *I am sorry*. The strip had been cut cleanly from the foot of a longer sheet.

*He says do not come. So I shall stand here.*

A grain of frost drifted down and settled on the last word, white on the black ink, and melted in the warmth of his knee, and left a little blur.

He thought of a ledge in the Fault in the white glare of eleven blades, and his own back against a vampire’s, and his own voice saying to a ring of rags and light, *So I shall stand here*. He had thought the words were his. He thought of her face an hour ago, with her sword at his breastbone, when he had said them again.

“You wrote this.”

“It was cut from a report. Nobody read it.” Matilda held out her hand. “Give it back.”

He did not give it back. “You came.”

“I stood forty-four days.”

“And then you came.”

“A letter is a letter.” Her hand was still held out, and it was not steady. “You wrote me a letter that said do not come, and I stood where you could see me, and did not come. That was my letter back. Then twelve suns went up in the Fault at the dead of night, Caleb, and went out again all at once, and you were somewhere under them, and a sun is not a letter. A sun is not anything I know how to answer by standing still.”

He gave the strip back to her. Then he put his arms round her.

She came into them stiffly, all steel and buckles and the edges of things, as if she had been told in theory how it was done and never put it into practice. Her mail was cold through his coat and smelled of horse and frost and the iron of a long road. Her chin came down on his shoulder, hard. Then her arms came up round him and took hold, the arms that had carried him down a white street at eight inside her cloak, and he felt her draw one long breath against his collar, and another, and he held her and let her breathe. The gold came back into her while he held her, as a cold hearthstone takes the new fire’s warmth in the morning, and the frost on her hair went to water and dripped from the end of her plait onto the rock.

When at last she moved, she did not let go. She went still against him in a different way, and her hand came round from his back to his chest, flat over his heart, where a hard round weight lay under silk and lining and a row of black stitches.

She drew back to look at him.

“What is that?”

Caleb looked at his sister’s hand where it lay over the ring, and thought of Master Brandt’s flat clear voice under the cold-iron lamp. *The name of the redeemer withheld from every party, the pledgor included.*

Every party. Not every sister.

“Read the last sheet first.”

It was on different paper from the rest, the heavy cream of the Chancellery, folded in three, in Eleanor’s hand, which leaned forward as if it were late for an appointment with the end of the line.

“That came the night before the lanterns. A rider up from Chalmont, with Eleanor’s seal. I did not understand it. I sewed it in with the rest by my fire, and did not sleep.”

*Notice of the Seventh Chamber of Dornhöhle to the Treasury of the Solar Throne. The portion settled upon His Royal Highness Prince Caleb on the twelfth day of his life, and held by the Chamber since, has this night been drawn in full, in seven drafts, as follows.*

Seven lines followed in Eleanor’s quick black figures, crowns and pence, and a total ruled beneath them. Then, *The account is closed. The Chamber does not discuss the business of its clients.*

At the foot of the sheet, in a hand that had begun to shake, Eleanor had written, *Came in before dawn. Copied by my own hand before the clerks were in. They will not say where it has gone. I do not know what it means. I have not slept. Keep it with the others. E.*

Caleb’s eyes went down the pence.

They went there of themselves, as they had gone down every column he had been sent since he was six years old, and there it was in his sister’s hurried hand, for anyone in the world to see. Twenty-three. Nine. Twelve. Twelve. Nine. Fourteen. Seven. The sun lay so bright on the cream that the black ink stood up off it, and the river’s breath drifted over the figures in a fine cold dust, like flour shaken over a board.

He had written it on the back of NOT YET under a cold-iron lamp, and burned it to a grey curl in an iron dish. It had not gone anywhere. It had gone to the Citadel, and been copied by Eleanor in the dark before dawn, and sewn into Matilda’s mail by a camp fire, and carried into the Fault against her heart.

Matilda had been shown the trick once, in a tent, but she had never in her life read the pence of anything. She read the bottom line, as she read a field from the top of a ridge, and her eyes went to the total and stayed there, and the colour came up under the grey of her face in two hard red patches along the cheekbones, one of them across the branch scratch, which began to bleed again.

“All of it.”

“Yes.”

“Every crown.”

“Every crown and every penny, and what was over went to the Chamber for its trouble, and the account is closed.”

Her hand closed on the sheet. “Who took it?”

“Nobody took it.”

“Did *he* —”

“Nobody took it, Tilda. I gave it. I walked down a corridor of cold-iron at the last hammer of the night, alone, and sat down at a table, and asked the Chamber its price, and paid it.” He put his hand over hers on the sheet. “Feel my coat.”

She put her hand back flat on his breast and pressed, and he felt the ring move against his ribs, heavy and round and cold through everything. The gold had come up a little into the back of her hand, faint as light through a shut eyelid, and the ring took none of it, a stone at the bottom of a sunny stream that the sun goes down to and cannot warm.

“It is a ring. Not mine. The Sovereign’s. Heavy gold, very old, with a stone in it so black that when I first saw it I thought the world had a hole in it there. Every Sovereign of the Coast has worn it for a thousand years. It is what he seals with. Or what he sealed with, until five days ago.”

“What happened five days ago?”

“He pledged it to the Seventh Chamber, to buy the Coast’s debts back from the Carusos before they could burn them on his wedding day and call it a dowry. The notes were bought back while I was standing at an anvil saying *yes* rather too loudly.” He heard his own voice, and went on. “If it is not redeemed by the Long Eclipse, it goes to auction under Holds law, and until then any of his Council may contest his seal and stand up and ask the shadows whether he is still Sovereign. There are men on that Council who have waited his whole life for the chance. He knew it when he did it. And the Coast cannot redeem it. Not by the Eclipse. Not in ten Eclipses.”

“So you did.”

“So I did.”

Matilda stood up, with one boot on and one foot wrapped in the tail of his coat, which came with her, and stood over him in the spray with the Chancellery sheet in her fist.

“You beggared yourself,” she said, “for a vampire.”

“For a ring.”

“For *his* ring.”

“For his throne, then. And for him.”

“Your ship.” Her voice had risen, which it did perhaps once in a year. “Your ship to Nauplion. You told me about it when you were fifteen with your mouth full of cake, and drew me the harbour on the tablecloth in mustard, with the merfolk in it, and the fish. And your house in Lutèce with the balcony over the river, which you were going to buy at twenty and fill with every vulgar novel ever printed and never once invite Father to. And at twenty-five, nothing at all, which you said would cost the most of any of them.” She shook the sheet at him. “Every crown Mother’s clerk laid down for you on your twelfth day. Your door out, Caleb. The only thing you had that did not come to you out of Father’s hand. And you gave it to the Seventh Chamber of Durnhollow in seven drafts in the middle of the night, so that a vampire would not lose his chair.”

“Yes.”

“You have nothing.”

“I have a great many coats.”

“*Caleb.*”

She took him by both shoulders and shook him, once, short and hard, as she shook a man in the line she had found asleep on his watch, and then held him at arm’s length, and her face was doing something he had never seen it do, and he did not think she knew it was doing it.

“Then you are both fools.”

“Very likely.”

She let go of him and sat down again, heavily, with a clash of mail.

“Does he know?”

“No. He thinks it will go to the highest bidder at the Long Eclipse, and that he will stand up before his Council on that day with a bare hand and let them do as they like.” Caleb looked at his own hands, which had gone cold again, having given the last of what was in them to her toes. “He does not know that it is lying against my ribs. And he is not to know.”

“Why not?”

She asked the plain thing at the end, always, after everybody else had finished being clever.

“Because he would not take it. He sat in the dark last night with his hand blistered from holding mine into the sun, and told me that he did not want to be paid. If he knew, he would go back to Durnhollow and sell it back to the Chamber with his own hands, and put my portion in my name to the last penny, and go to the auction with nothing, sooner than owe me. He would do it in an afternoon and never mention it again.” He looked at her. “So he is not to know. Not from me, not from Eleanor’s sums, not from you. I am asking you to promise.”

“I do not lie, Caleb.”

“I am not asking you to lie. I am asking you not to tell him.”

“And if he asks me?”

“He will not ask you. He does not know that there is anything to ask.”

Matilda looked at the sheet a long time. Then she folded it along Eleanor’s folds and slid it inside her mail over her heart, with the strip that said *So I shall stand here*, and laid her hand flat over both of them.

“I shall not tell him,” she said.

She said it in the voice she used for orders of the day, and Caleb, who had been brought up on that voice, knew it for stronger than any promise. Under her hand the gold had come up into her fingers, and the frost on the links round them went to water and ran down in thin bright threads, as the ice goes off a gate in the first warm hour of the spring.

“Thank you.”

“Do not thank me. I think you are a fool.” She took her hand from her mail at last, and held out the rest of the papers.

“Take these.”

They were Eleanor’s copies, the thin ledger taken apart into its leaves, with her column running down every page like a stream finding its way down a hill. He did not need to read past the twelve crates to the Kessel Pass. He had read the hilts.

*Does Citadel machinery sing?*

He saw a lycan with a white scar dragging a boy back from his throat by the scruff. *Not a hair of his head. You heard the Don.*

*There’s people waiting to take you home.*

“He wanted me home,” he said.

“Yes.”

“He sent them to bring me home. Not a hair of my head. Not a scratch. Not a fright, if they could help it. He sat in the Citadel and thought about my hair.” His hands had closed on the papers, and the ring was cold against his ribs like a second heart that would not keep time with the first. “And the rest of them, the boy and the escort and the man I was sitting in a carriage with, he did not think about at all.”

Matilda said nothing.

“Keep those,” she said at last. “Eleanor told me to keep them somewhere Andrew would never think to look. He will never think to look on the Coast.”

“The Sovereign will read them. And his uncle.”

“Let them.” She bent to put her boot back on, and laced it as she laced everything, hard and fast and double-knotted. “The vampire took a blade for you. His uncle may read Eleanor’s sums.”

“What will you do?”

“Go to the Citadel. Tonight I ride for Chalmont. There is a yacht there that will do what I tell it, if I tell it loudly. Ashby takes the twenty back to the line and stands in the snow where everyone can see him, and if anyone asks where the High Paladin is, he will say she is inspecting the positions of the Radiant Guard.” She pulled the lace tight. “It is a very popular errand this year.”

“Tilda.”

Matilda looked at the water, and spoke to that.

“There are no rogues going home from the Fault. Somebody is waiting for them, and they will not come, and sooner or later he will hear of it, the way such men hear things. And when he hears, he will think, as any commander thinks when a plan has failed, about what is left on the field with his name on it.” She tied the knot. “The blades in that rug have no name on them. But there is one person in Aloria who can say who asked for them. And she will open her door to him when he knocks, Caleb, because she always has. I mean to be standing behind it when he does.”

He thought of Cecily at eight on the rim of the nursery bath while he splashed in her sunlight, laughing harder than he was, and of Cecily at thirteen with a stolen chisel, making the dwarven runes look glad.

“She did not know. Tilda. She cannot have known what they were for.”

“No.”

“She would never —”

“No. She would not.” Matilda straightened on her rock. “She thought she was bringing you home.” She was quiet a moment. “So did he. I will not tell her yet. If she knew, she would go up to his rooms and ask him, to his face. Cecily asks. She will not hear it from me until there is someone standing between her and the answer.”

The shadow of the far wall had begun to come across the water towards them, blue and slow, as the sun climbed back up the north face. It came up over Matilda’s boots and her mail and her face, and the gold in her went down a little under it, as a forge fire settles when the bellows stop, and their two breaths went up white together and drifted out over the river and were gone.

Caleb folded Eleanor’s papers tight and buttoned them into his coat, on the right side, away from the ring.

“Then take her a message from me.”

“Tell me.”

“Tell her,” said Caleb, “that I never had my bath.”

Matilda looked at him. He saw her not understand, and then understand all at once, the nursery bathroom of the Citadel with the light pouring off its tiles, and a little girl on the rim of the bath hugging her knees, and a flask of sunlight that a grown man had asked his sister for at his last birthday, for old times’ sake, and been taken away before it was ever poured.

“I will tell her.”

She stood, and stamped the boot down onto her foot, and winced, and pretended she had not. She reached out, as she had once over a sickbed when he was twelve and she thought he was asleep, and rested her hand on his hair for one long breath, and took it away.

“Whatever you need,” she said, “it is yours. The Guard pays me a great deal more than I have ever found a use for, and I spend it on horses.”

“I know.”

“That was not an offer, Caleb.” She turned for the path. “That was the order of the day.”

\* \* \*

In the afternoon the light climbed the north wall again as slowly as it had come down, a stripe at a time, and by the time it had left the road the Fault was full of horses.

Ashby had brought the twenty down off the rim, and they stood on one side of the cleared road in their white cloaks, and the lycans of the escort stood on the other in their Conti black with their wounded on their shoulders, and they looked at each other in the late gold light as dogs of two farms look at each other across a lane. Nobody had told them what to do. Matilda, who might have, was busy with the girth of her horse. The sergeant, who might also have, was lashing the grey velvet bundle to the roof of the second carriage and keeping his eyes on the knots.

The sun went off the rim. The Fault filled with blue.

And up from the cave in the dusk, with the frost thick on his shoulders, came the Sovereign of the Obsidian Coast.

The Guard saw him come. Caleb watched it go along their line as a wind goes over barley, nothing anybody did, only a stiffening, and twenty hands all at once aware of where their sword hilts were. The lycans of the escort struck their fists to their hearts, the wounded too, from where they sat on the carriage steps. The lamps along the road had been lit, and as Arian walked between the two companies the flame in every lamp lay over on its side towards him, and stayed so, and the shadows of the white-cloaked knights drew out long across the road towards his feet, and the knights looked down at their own shadows going, and up at him, and said nothing at all.

At the end of their line a grey mare went back on her haunches with her ears flat to her skull, and the knight at her head hung on the bridle with his whole weight and could not bring her down, and nobody laughed. Caleb thought of the ledge beyond the buttress, where the sun had taken the last of the footprints that morning. There had been more men on that ledge than there were knights on this road, and it had taken the dark less time to go over all of them than it was taking the grey mare’s rider to say her name.

He crossed the road and went to stand at Arian’s side, in the blue. The long shadows let go of the Sovereign’s boots and went back along the stones to the men they belonged to, as children who have strayed down to the river go home up the lane when they hear their names called, and the grey mare came down onto her four feet and stood blowing.

Matilda came across the road and stopped before him, at a distance that Caleb thought the Usher of Distances would have measured and written down in his book with deep content.

“Sovereign.”

“High Paladin.”

Matilda looked at the frost on his shoulders and his white face in the lamplight, and at the right hand at his side, where the skin of the fingers was still roughened and red. “He is my brother, Sovereign.”

“I know.”

“If he comes to harm in your keeping —”

“He will not come to harm,” Arian answered, “while I can stand in front of it.”

Matilda looked at the hand.

“Then see that you can stand.”

She turned for her horse. She had gone two paces when Arian spoke again, and she stopped.

“The prince told my Council that your line was a letter.” He inclined his head to her, not far. “I read it. Thank you.”

Matilda stood quite still in the middle of the road. Then, round the edges of her mouth, something happened that would have been the beginning of a smile on any other face, and on Matilda’s was a great deal rarer and more alarming. The gold came up in her face with it, and for a moment the lamps nearest her stood up straight from where they had leaned, as if they had been reminded that there was more than one way for a flame to go.

“It was not addressed to you, Sovereign.”

“No.”

“That is four,” said Caleb. They both looked at him. “Thank yous. I have been keeping count.” Along the road every lamp that had stood up straight for her lay over again towards the man in black, as if it too had been keeping count, and had lost its place.

“That one was not yours,” said Arian.

“I am counting it all the same.”

Matilda made a sound that in a lesser officer might have been a snort, and swung up into the saddle, stiff in the left foot, and gathered her reins.

“Ashby. The line.”

“Paladin.”

“Walker.”

Paul rose from the carriage step. “Paladin.”

“Keep him alive.”

“Yes, Paladin,” said Paul. His voice scraped on it. Behind Caleb, Arian’s head went towards the sound, and stayed there.

Matilda looked down at Caleb from the height of her horse.

“Again,” she said.

Then she put her horse at the road north, and the twenty went after her in a long white line. Caleb watched her go until she was a white mark on the rim against the last green of the sky.

At the rim, the white mark stopped.

Something went up from it, and caught, and flashed, once. It was a drawn sword held straight up over a rider’s head, and the light along it was her own, the gold of the Solar House sent up the length of the steel from the hand on the hilt and thrown back down into the gorge in a single stroke, so that for one heartbeat the whole north wall of the Fault from the rim to the river was lit like a page under a lamp.

Then she was over the rim, and gone.

The gold of that stroke had gone over Arian with the rest of the wall, where he stood in the blue at Caleb’s side, and at that distance it had been only light, and had not burned him at all.

“She draws on everyone,” said Caleb presently. His voice was not quite his own. “You must not take it personally. She drew a sword on the high priest of the Citadel temple at my naming day. A wooden one. She was eight, and he took me out of her arms to hold me up to the light, and she did not see why he should.”

“Did she get you back?”

“Within the minute, I am told. The high priest never held another child of the Solar House without first looking round to see where she was standing.”

Arian looked up at the rim, where the last green had gone out of the sky and the first star had come.

“I understand her.”

\* \* \*

They took the Fault road south at full dark, in three carriages, and left the first behind under the fallen rock with its engine cold.

At the rim the waystation was a low stone house with iron shutters and a lamp over its door and a young dwarf on the step, who told them with deep satisfaction that the Sovereign’s letter had gone south at noon with his fastest rider. He had looked, Caleb did not doubt, at the black seal with nothing on its face. He said not a word about it, as the Holds did not.

Caleb’s palms were warm again, a little, with the afternoon on the river and the long gold hour on the road, the warmth pooled in each like the last of a cup of tea that has been kept by the fire. Across from him Arian rode with one hand on his knee. The red roughness was still on the backs of his fingers, and along each cheekbone, in the violet that came up through the floor slats, there was still a line of darker colour where Matilda’s light had opened the scald again.

“Give me your hand,” said Caleb. “There is a little in them. Enough for a scald. You may consider it payment for the lemons, again. I am aware that I have already used that one. I find I am short of fresh material.”

Arian gave him his hand.

Caleb laid his two palms over the backs of the fingers, as he had laid one over a fist once in a glowing mist, and let what little there was go down, and felt the skin remember, as it had remembered at the garden doors, where it had been whole, and go back there. When he took his hands away the fingers were smooth and whole, and the frost was already silvering them again, glittering faintly in the violet.

He did not ask about the cheekbones. He reached across and laid two fingers against the line of darker colour along the right one, as a physician lays two fingers on a pulse, and let the last of the warmth go.

Arian held quite still under his hand. He did not close his eyes. He went on looking at Caleb from a hand’s breadth away, with the line fading under Caleb’s fingers from the edges in, and Caleb kept his eyes on the line and his mind on his work, because his tutors in the Vales had been strict about where a healer should look, and because if he had looked anywhere else he did not know what would have become of him.

He did the left side too. Then he sat back on the grey velvet with his two hands empty in his lap and cold again, and found he had to look out of the window for a while.

“Thank you,” murmured Arian.

“That is five.”

“I know.”

The moor went by, high and white under the stars, with the black stands of pine on it like the knots in a board, and somewhere off to the north a white mark was riding hard towards a yacht field and a city where the sun did not go down.

Caleb took Eleanor’s papers out of the right side of his coat and held them out, and in the cold of the carriage, where Arian’s frost had begun to fern the inside of the glass, they gave off a thin ghost of steam.

“These are my sister’s. Eleanor’s, that is. Copies of the Treasury’s books, and some books that are not the Treasury’s. Your uncle will want them. They are about my brother. Andrew.”

Arian looked at Caleb’s face, and whatever he saw in it made him take the papers slowly, and lay them on his knee without unfolding them.

“You knew.”

“Not whose.” Arian’s hand lay flat on the papers. “I did not ask.”

He looked at Caleb across the carriage. In the violet his eyes were as dark as the Fault at midnight, and somewhere far down in them a light was being kept shuttered, like the sergeant’s lamp on its peg in the lambing pens, so that it should not be too much for anyone lying near it.

“We will read them with my uncle. When we are home.”

*When we’re home*, Paul had said, on a stair in Dornhöhle, and on a ledge in the sun. *I am beginning to wonder where you think home is*, Caleb had answered him.

He had stopped wondering at some point in the last nine weeks, while he was looking the other way.

“Yes. When we are home.”

\* \* \*

They came down off the last of the Marches as the second night ended, and the ash cloud of the Coast came over them like a lid.

The stars went out behind it one by one, and the dark under it was a different dark, warmer and closer and smelling of sulphur and old stone, and the violet came up into it from below, from the black valleys and the villages on their ravine sides with their lamps lit, and the slow red rivers moving in the depths. At the foot of the Coast’s long spine the night ferry was waiting with its deck lamps burning, and the black water went by beneath it with the lamps broken up in it.

Caleb stood at the rail.

On the way north Mirek had come out to him here with a message. Tonight Mirek was asleep in the second carriage with the dwarven knife in his fist, and Arian came out instead, without one, and stood beside him with the frost on his shoulders and the violet of the deck lamps running down his black sleeve, and they watched the far shore come, the black cliffs and the lamps of the harbour, and somewhere up the mountain beyond them, too far to see, a fortress in a volcano with a garden in its throat.

Caleb’s right hand was at his coat, over his heart. It went there of itself these days, as it had on every road and in every cave since Dornhöhle, and lay flat over the hard round weight against his ribs, as a traveller’s hand goes to his purse in the press of a market and stays there.

On the rail beside him Arian’s right hand lay on the cold iron. As Caleb watched, its thumb went across to the bare third finger and worried at the root of it, a quarter turn, at nothing.

“You have your hand on your heart again,” said Arian.

He said it to the black water, quite level, as he said everything.

“It is a habit,” admitted Caleb.

“I know.”

The far shore came on. Behind them the Marches went down into the dark, and the night of the Coast closed over the ferry, violet and black and full of the smell of the sea, and under Caleb’s hand the ring lay cold and kept its counsel, within an arm’s reach of the finger it belonged on, all the way home.
