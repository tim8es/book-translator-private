## Chapter 20: Interlude - Vesperia

*Any of the Nine may hold another to answer before the Council. The Sovereign may do more than hold. But let him be sure of the shadows before he does it, for the Nine will be watching to see where they go, and if they do not go to him, he has judged nobody but himself.*

— From *The Oldest Laws of the Nine*, as they are kept in the Consigliere’s office at Castel Nerezza

\* \* \*

The white light went out of the ash above the garden, and the hum went with it, north.

Arian heard it go. He stood on the iron sill with his hand on the frame and listened to the light-engines of a Solar yacht climb out of the throat of his mountain and away over the Coast, a long thinning drone, like a spinning wheel slowing in the next room, until all that was left was the hum of the twelve crystals in their gold and the water on the stair, talking to itself. Under it, a long way up and going further, the prince’s heart went on. It went slow and heavy and sweet, as a heart goes under a song, and his own kept pace with it.

The ash was still coming down.

It came in at the open crown of the dome and fell through the gold in loose slow flurries, turning and taking its time, as the down comes out of a pillow split in a nursery battle, and every flake that passed a crystal took fire at its edges and went grey again and settled. It settled on the roses and the lemon leaves and in the throats of the lilies. It settled on the water of the stair and was carried down. It had never fallen in the garden before. He had built the dome so that it never should.

His boot was smoking.

He looked down at it. The toe lay on the line where the gold began, and the leather had gone brown and then black and was curling back from the welt as a page curls on the bars of a grate, and the smell came up to him, hot and bitter, the smell of a cobbler’s shop with the stove built too high.

He drew the foot back into the dark of the corridor, where he had been asked to keep it. The hand on the frame he left where it was. The backs of the fingers had lifted in a white bloom of blisters from knuckle to nail, and the frost was already coming back over them, grain by grain, white over red, as if nothing had happened that was any concern of frost’s.

Among the roses of the top terrace, Tomas was still smiling.

He stood with the shears open in his fist and his weathered face turned up to the open crown, and the tears ran down into his collar unwiped, because he did not know they were there. His heart came up to the sill slow and broad and content, the heart of a man asleep by his own hearth with his boots off.

He could not go to him. There were twelve suns between the sill and the top terrace, and Tomas was three strides inside them.

“Tomas,” he called.

Nothing. The song had his ears too.

Behind him, a long way down the dark of the corridor, somebody was running. He knew the step. He had heard it in his house twice before, on the night of the hearthstone and on the night he came home from Dornhöhle, and had thought both times that he would not hear it again.

That made three.

Vesna came out of the violet dark with her skirts caught up and flour to the elbow, and stopped at the edge of the light two paces off, and looked down into the garden, at the ash coming down through the gold and her husband among the roses weeping and smiling at nothing. She did not ask what had happened. She looked at the lilies by the water-stair, trodden flat where the sling had come down among them, and at the hole in the dome, and at his hand on the frame.

“Where’s His Highness?”

“North.”

Her hand went to her apron pocket, where she kept her needle case, and closed on it.

“And Walker?”

“With him.”

She looked at his face while a flake of ash fell from the crown to the first crystal. It gave her nothing, as it gave nobody anything, and she kept that too, as Vesna kept everything, for later. Then she went out over the far end of the sill and down the iron stairs to her husband, and took the shears out of his hand, and laid her floury palm against his wet cheek, and said his name low in the Wildmarch tongue, as a woman calls a man in from a field at dusk.

Tomas blinked. He looked at his wife, and at the shears in her hand, and at the grey on the white roses, and his face came down out of wherever the song had put it, slowly, as a man comes down a ladder in the dark, feeling for every rung.

“Tell him to close the crown.”

Vesna told him. Tomas went up the terraces without a word and out by the far door to the masons’ stair, and some while later the round of the dome went dark, and the ash stopped coming.

It did not take the shadow with it.

The long shadow he had left lying on the sill the night he went to Dornhöhle had gone up after the sling, and it was there still. It lay along the vent wall from the doorframe to the height of the second crystal, thin and dark and burned at its tip to a ragged brown fringe, as the lace of a cuff goes that has been held too near a candle.

It stayed there, and he let it stay. It had gone after the breast of the prince’s coat further into that light than any shadow of his had ever been allowed to go, and stopped only where the gold would let it go no further, and he found he had not the heart to tell it that it had been wrong to try.

He sat down on the sill and leaned on the frame, and the gold of the garden lay across the iron beside him and stopped at his knee. Walker had sat here too, on the night of the gallery, with his back to the same iron and a sliver of crystal in his fist. Arian had listened to that heart misbehave for eleven weeks and had never once asked it why.

He knew why now. It had been a heart kept in somebody else’s hand. It had beaten all that while in a drawer in the white of the Citadel, in another man’s time, and gone wrong whenever its own time came up through it and tried to be heard.

He knew something about a heart that kept another man’s time.

Under his ribs the prince’s had gone slower. Far off over the Coast, under a song, it went as hearts go in sleep, and then it steadied, and went even and deliberate, and he knew that evenness. The prince was counting. He had gone up past the doors limp in his own man’s arms, and he was counting, as he had been asked to, to three hundred every night.

Arian counted with him. He could not hear the numbers. He could hear the time they were said in, which was all that had ever mattered about them. Sixty. A hundred. A hundred and eighty, and the evenness began to slip, as the stitches of a woman sewing by the fire go long and loose when she begins to doze over them, and somewhere past two hundred it went soft and slow, and was only sleep, and nobody was counting any longer.

“Two hundred and one,” said Arian, to the garden.

He finished it for him. He said the numbers aloud on the sill, low, to the lilies and the grey on the roses and the burned shadow on the wall, at the pace a boy of eight had set for him once, kneeling in the wet earth of a glasshouse. At three hundred he stopped, because that was what had been asked.

\* \* \*

The fire in the study went on being lit, and the candles with it.

It had been lit every night since the first, on an order he had never troubled to take back, for hands that had been cold in a hall, and the household went on building it high whether there was anybody in the gold chair or not, because nobody had told them to stop and nobody wished to be the one who asked. He came up at the end of each night and sat at his desk with his back to it, and the Council’s papers lay before him with the frost going out over them in white ferns, every one wanting a seal, and he sealed none of them.

The cloak lay where he had put it down, across the arm of the gold chair, sun uppermost.

*Keep it for me.*

*Until when?*

*Until I want it.*

He left it there. It lay in the firelight on the wheat-coloured velvet as it had lain through twelve nights before the prince was taken, and the sunburst at its collar had gone back to sleep, as it had slept in a drawer for twenty years with no light near it to drink. He had been asked to keep it. Keeping did not seem to him to require handling.

The heart was another matter.

It went on under his ribs all through the next day and the day after, a long way north, and he read it as his uncle read a ledger, for whatever it had been built to hide.

On the second day it came up out of the song all at once, a little after the sixth bell of the Coast, and went hard and quick and bright, as it went when the prince came out onto the mezzanine in the morning and the crystals found him, and then quick in another way, which Arian did not like at all. It was glad, and it was not. It went on so for an hour, and then it went quiet and even and stayed even, the evenness of a man keeping his face.

That night the counting began again. It began late, at ninety or so, as if the prince had lain a while in some dark room before he remembered his promise, and it went on, steady, to two hundred and some, and stopped. Under it the heart went strange, and quick, and then very hard, as a heart goes when it is told a thing it was not ready to hear, and for a long while it was only a heart, beating in a room Arian had never seen, with nobody counting it. He sat with his palm on his own chest and waited, until at last the count took up again where it had left off, and went on, even and slow, to three hundred.

He did not know who had stopped it. He found that he minded, unreasonably, and sat with the minding until the crystals had gone once round on their silver chains.

On the third day it knocked hard in the middle of the Citadel’s day, as it knocked when the prince was angry and keeping his voice level, and steadied by main force, as the prince steadied things. That night it walked a long way, quick and wary, through some place that made it uneasy, and stopped, and went very quiet, and then went fast and warm and near to breaking, as he had heard it go once before, at a river in the Fault, with a sister.

A heart was a poor letter. It told him nothing he could have read out to the Nine. But it was the only letter he had, and nobody had read it before him, which could not be said of any other letter the prince had written since he came to the Coast, and he read it every hour, twice.

The flowers in the garden still had their faces turned up to the crown.

They had turned after the prince at his name when the sling went up, and they had not turned back. On the second morning Arian watched from the sill while Tomas went along the top terrace and turned the white heads of the roses gently down to the light again with his big earthy hands, one after another, as a nurse turns a sleeping child off its face. Every head he turned went back up the moment he let it go, to the dark round of the dome where the prince had gone. After the first terrace Tomas gave it up and went on with his deadheading round them.

On the fourth day the *Lantern* came in from Lutèce, damp from the strait and smelling of fae ink, and his uncle did not bring it to the sill.

Arian went down through the violet galleries to look for him.

He was in the lamp room, as Arian had known he would be, on the bench before the niche that had been cut in the spring, with the *Lantern* folded on his knee and the lamplight lying gold along its crease, and his hand on his coat over the place where he carried the broken chain from his wife’s hand. The hundred flames burned upright in their coloured bowls along the walls, each with a softer twin in the black rock beside it, and the air was thick with lamp oil and dark sweet resin, and two niches above Livia’s the flame in Alessandro’s bowl had burned down to its bead of blue.

Enzo did not look up.

“Sit down, Arian,” he said. “You will make the lamps nervous.”

Arian crossed to the other end of the bench, and every flame in the room bent towards him as he passed, as lamps did, and he sat down in the warm dark between two niches.

Enzo held out the paper.

It had the prince on its first sheet. It did not have his face, which the *Lantern* had never yet got right. It had his name across the head of the sheet in crimson ink, under a headline that for once had no question in it at all. *THE PILGRIM PRINCE COMES HOME.* Below it ran the quick light hand of the fae, every word true and none of them in the order anybody would have chosen.

King George had laid down the crown before his court and shut himself in his study. The Crown Prince held the seals, and would be crowned on the eve of the Eclipse. His Royal Highness the Prince Caleb had come home by Solar yacht, *with no one at the step but his brother*, and had been seen since on the white terraces in the Coast’s black.

And at the foot stood a paragraph by itself, set there as a cook sets the one burnt scone at the edge of the plate, so that it will be eaten first and not remarked on.

*The* Lantern *has it from the Crown Prince himself, and records it without comment, that at the crowning His Royal Highness the Prince Caleb will speak before the courts of the world upon what was done to him on the Obsidian Coast. The* Lantern *will be present. It has asked for a seat.*

Arian read the paragraph at the foot again.

“He will not say it,” he said.

“No,” said Enzo. “He will say something.”

Somewhere in the rock behind the lamps the water went on dripping, as it had dripped since before there were Contis to listen to it.

“I am going to the Citadel,” said Arian.

“Yes,” said Enzo.

He said it in the voice he kept for a figure read out of a column, and Arian turned his head.

The master of the *Spaarzaamheid* had been up the switchback to supper twice that week, and had gone down again both times looking like a man who has been sold his own boots. Arian had not asked why.

“Van Houten sails for the Citadel wharves tomorrow night,” said Enzo, “with two casks more than she declares.”

“Two.”

“Two,” said Enzo.

“No,” said Arian.

His uncle folded the *Lantern* along its crease and laid it on the bench between them, and smoothed it flat with one long white hand, and left the hand there.

“The Citadel is noon from wall to wall,” said Arian. “You will burn before you are down the step.”

“Then you may step over me.”

“Zio.”

“Vesna will hold the fortress,” said Enzo, before he could say the rest.

“The Ashward is on the hill.”

“The Ashward is in the guest wing, eating our oranges.” His hand did not move on the paper. “I am coming all the same.”

Arian looked at the bead of blue two niches up.

“Why?” he said.

His uncle was quiet a long while. When he spoke it was to Livia’s lamp, and in the ledger voice, and the voice did not hold.

“I was not with them when your father and aunt died. I have wondered for years if things would have been different had I been.”

The flame in Alessandro’s bowl did not stir.

“You may be the most powerful Sovereign in centuries, Arian, and next to you my power is nothing.” His hand pressed flat on the folded paper. “But I will be with you at every step.”

*If he burns on that step*, thought Arian, *the Coast will want a Consigliere by the next sitting, and there is nobody else alive who can read the Nine.*

“If you burn,” he said, “I shall not stop.”

Enzo turned his head at last. “May I?”

Arian did not know what he was being asked. Then his uncle’s hand came up off the folded paper towards him, and before Arian had decided anything his own had closed on the wrist a hand’s breadth short of his chest, as it had closed on every hand that came within his reach since he was twelve years old, and held it there.

Enzo did not pull against it. He looked at the hand round his wrist, and at the place on the black silk beyond it, on the left side, where a woman had laid hers every night of Arian’s childhood to feel him breathe. His face kept perfectly still, as it had kept still in this room on every month-day since the spring, and that was how Arian knew.

“I have suspected since the gallery,” said Enzo.

Arian let go of his wrist. Under the black silk his heart went on, quick and light as it had been since the *Lantern*, keeping time with another heart far to the north that was beating faster still.

The two vampires sat on in comfortable silence.

Enzo laid his hand back on the paper on his knee and looked at it, as a man looks at his palm after a coin has been taken out of it. Then he rose and smoothed his dove grey and was the Consigliere again from his collar to his shoes.

“Your aunt wrote that I would make a treaty of it,” he said. “I intend to make a very good one.”

She came along the violet corridor a little before the ninth bell, alone, with no lamp, as the Ashward went everywhere.

Her step made no sound. The Ashward’s never had. He heard the cold come before her down the corridor, and the heart that was not in her, and the faint dry whisper of kid gloves being drawn tighter at the wrist, and knew her before she came out of the dark of the turn.

She stopped where the gold of the garden began to reach her. The light came down the corridor as far as her hem and laid a faint warm glow on the black silk of her skirts, which her skirts did not want.

It was eight paces back, further than his uncle stood, for the Ashward’s blood was older than the Contis’ and had grown thinner with every century it had spent waiting. She stood there in the violet dark in her black, which went red in its folds even in that light, with the jet thorn at her throat and a box under her arm, and looked past him into the garden, where every rose and every lily had its face turned up to the shut crown.

“Donna Valentina.”

“Sovereign.” She did not curtsey. She looked at the flowers a while longer. “They have not turned back.”

“No.”

She bent and set the box down on the black glass at her feet. It was lacquer, the length of her forearm, black, with a thorn inlaid in jet on its lid, and she set it down as a jeweller sets a tray on his counter, squarely and without a sound. Then she straightened and drew her gloves tight, the right and then the left. The left had been darned across the knuckle in black silk, with stitches put in by somebody who did not darn often and had done it anyway.

“I keep my grandfather’s papers. He has very beautiful hands, and he has never once spoiled them with ink.” She looked down at the box. “I have come to sell you something.”

Arian did not move from the sill. The silence went down the corridor to her and stood there.

“No. Not tonight. Tonight I have come to give it to you, which you will find a great deal more expensive.”

“What does it cost?”

“My grandfather.”

She said it as she had said *eleven nights* in his hall, without raising her voice and without dropping it, and it went down the corridor and into the garden and nothing in the garden moved.

“On the night you refused me, I told my grandfather that the Sovereign had a weakness at last, and that we should find out what it cost.” Her mouth moved. “We have found out. On the morning of the Eclipse it will cost us the house. I should like to pay before the bill is presented.”

“What bill?”

“The prince’s brother writes a very beautiful letter.” She stooped to the box and lifted its lid a little way, and drew a sheet from under the grey one on top, and held it up in the violet dark. “It came last night.”

He read it from the sill, eight paces off, as he could have read it from the far end of the hall.

*Don Salvatore. My brother is home, and whole, and in his own rooms. The Crown thanks the Ashward for its patience, and for its gifts to him, of which I have heard. I bear the Ashward no ill will. I shall look for it at the Summit, where every house will be given what it is owed. Donna Valentina will remember what I said to you both at the Kessel Pass. I have not forgotten it.*

She put it back under the grey sheet and let the lid down, and drew a breath.

“My grandfather read it twice, and said that the young man wrote prettily enough for an elf, and sent for his purse.”

“And you?”

“I remember the Kessel Pass. If his brother were so much as singed, the Ashward would learn what else the Grand Alchemist could make.” Her hand went up to the thorn and did not close on it. “Since then we have put a knife a finger’s breadth from the prince’s heart, and a flower in his throat. Somebody at your door has told him so.”

“Yes.”

“And now he bears us no ill will.” She smiled. It was the smile she had given the prince across his hall the night she was refused, and it was not meant for Arian. “It is the kindest letter he has ever written to us. That is how I knew.”

The frost had come out from his hand along the sill. He let it.

“The Accord has been loosed for him since the gala, and the inquiry falls on the day of the Eclipse. My grandfather will be at Old Meridian with the five who stood, and a purse for your ring. The prince’s brother will be there a king, with as many of his sister’s knives as she will make him.” She looked at the box at her feet. “There are three copies of what lies in there. The Chamber will never speak of its own. The prince’s brother has his. I have ours. He means there to be nobody left who has read it.” Her voice did not change. “He sent his own brother to this Coast asleep, Sovereign. He will not lose a night’s sleep over me.”

Arian looked at her a long time in the violet dark.

“My grandfather has not been outbid since before your house had a throne.”

“Let him bid.”

She looked once at his right hand, at the bare finger beside the two gold rings, and then at his face again, and whatever she thought of it she did not say.

“What do you want, Donna Valentina?”

“I want there to be an Ashward in the morning.”

“Your grandfather will not be in it.”

“No.” She was quiet. Down in the garden a crystal rang once on its chain, turning, and the note came up the vent and went past her into the dark. “He sat through the vote for the Leaning. He sat through your purge. When the Ashward is burning he will still be in his chair, turning his ring. I should like to stand up. Once. Before I am old.”

Arian rose.

He stepped down off the sill into the dark and came down the corridor, and Valentina gave back before him a pace and then another, as everybody on the Coast did, until the box lay alone on the glass between them. The cold of her came round him, finer than her grandfather’s and with more edge to it, and he crouched on the glass and opened the box.

He could see in that violet dark as well as in any other. The papers lay in it square and flat on a bed of black silk, each folded once and each docketed on its outer fold in a hand that was neat and slanting and plainly hers. On top lay a single long sheet of the Holds’ thick grey paper, and at its foot, in three pools of grey wax, the seven bars of the Seventh Chamber.

He unfolded it.

It was in Master Brandt’s printed hand, every clause read back and agreed aloud, as the Holds wrote everything, and it said what his uncle had pieced together out of ledgers and blades, crate by crate, down to the Ashward recognised first and loudest and the prince found, upon a fuller inquiry, never to have been at the vineyard. At its foot, in two hands, stood *Salvatore, of the house of Caruso, for the Ashward*, and *Andrew, Crown Prince of the Solar Throne*.

He read the first clause last, because it had been written first, above all the others, and he had to turn back three sheets to find it.

*His Royal Highness the Prince Caleb is not to be harmed. Not by the Ashward, nor its weapons, nor any it pays. Whatever is done with what is sent, it is to be done where he is not, and he is to be delivered to the Crown Prince’s people alive and whole and unafraid.*

He read it again.

It was the only line in the instrument that had been written by somebody who was afraid, and the word that gave it away was the last one. Arian knew a little about that word. A boy of eight had knelt beside him once in a glasshouse and told him he was not afraid of him, so there.

Under the instrument were letters. There were three, on good white paper, each sealed in grey wax with a little sun, and the hand on them was careful and even and had not crossed out a word. The first had come before the Fault.

*The road is the Fault, by the Holds’ ledge, out of Dornhöhle at dusk on the sixtieth night. Four carriages, lycans on the roofs and the running boards, the Sovereign in the second. My first clause stands above all the rest. There will be people of mine at the southern rim to bring my brother home.*

The frost went out of his fingers into the paper, white along every line of the ink, and no further than the edge of the sheet.

He had known since the anatomy in his uncle’s office who had told the Ashward his road. He had not held it before in the hand that wrote it. Mirek’s leg had broken on a wheel because of this sheet, and the fifth blade had come undone in Arian’s own blood, and the man who wrote *the Sovereign in the second* in that careful hand had sat at Brandt’s table a fortnight before and had *alive and whole and unafraid* written down above everything else, and meant both.

The second letter he did not need to open. He had heard it quoted at his own table by a man in garnet, word for word, to the Arch-Chancellor of Aloria. The third, the beautiful one, he had read already from the sill.

He closed the box.

“You will want them for the Nine,” said Valentina, above him. “And afterwards for the Citadel.”

Arian stood up with the box in his hands.

“An Ashward in the morning, Sovereign.”

“There will not be.”

She did not move. Down the vent behind him the water went on along its stair, and the cold of her went still round him in the violet dark, as the cold of a temple goes still when the last petitioner has gone home, and her hand went up to the thorn at her throat, and this time it closed there.

“All of it?”

“Everyone who has sat at its table, or given an order in its name.”

“And if I were to take ship tonight?”

“The shadows of this Coast are the Coast’s, wherever they stand.”

“I thought not. I only wished to hear how you would put it.” Her hand came down from the thorn. “And the children?”

“Are not of the table.”

She considered him a long while, his face and the box in his hands, as she had once measured the prince across his hall, to the last decimal.

“Then I have sold you something after all.”

He said nothing to that, and she had not waited for him to. She drew her gloves tighter, though they could not be drawn any tighter. “I shall sit beside him at your table tomorrow, as I always have. I shall not touch his arm.”

She turned to go, and at the edge of the dark she stopped.

“You will see the prince.”

“Yes.”

“Tell him that he dances very well,” said Valentina. “I said it to him once with something after it. I should like it said once without.”

The Nine came at the eleventh bell of the next night in their black and their smoke and their plum, and took their high-backed chairs below the dais, and left one empty.

The lord of the inland vineyards was in the cellar under them, on a stool beneath the old vent, where every word the Nine spoke of him came down the rock to him as they spoke it.

The great hall had been mended where it could be mended. The red frozen hail of the Ashward’s oranges had been swept away and the violet lamps relit on their long chains, but nobody had yet found a way to mend glass, and the star that had opened at the foot of the dais on the night of the purge still ran out between the councillors’ chairs in long white lines, as the veins run in a leaf held up to a lamp. The frost had never quite gone out of them. The glass chair at the right of the high seat stood empty and dark, with no gold thread lit in it.

Don Salvatore came last but one, in garnet, unhurried, with his granddaughter beside him in her black and her thorn, and she sat down at his side and folded her darned glove over the other in her lap.

Arian stood where he had stood to purge his household, on the white star at the foot of the dais, and left the high seat empty behind him, and the steward of the hall brought his staff down three times on the glass, and the Council sat.

Under his ribs, a long way north, the prince was counting.

He had begun some while ago. It came to Arian as it had come on every night since the hearthstone, slow and even and patient, and he stood on the star and let it go on under everything, as a man walking a long road lets a stream go on beside him in the dark.

The steward of the hall took the lacquer box from the step of the dais, where Arian had set it down, and laid it on the black cloth before his uncle, with the jet thorn uppermost.

Don Salvatore looked at it.

Otherwise he was still. He sat with his hands folded and his rings lying on them as they had lain at every Council, and looked at the thorn on the lid of the box for one beat of the prince’s heart, and then his head came round, slowly, as a man’s does at a voice he knows in a crowd, and he looked at his granddaughter.

Valentina looked at the box.

“Consigliere,” said Arian.

Enzo opened it and read.

He read in his ledger voice, dry and level, as he had read to every Council in two centuries, and he read all of it, from the twelve crates to the two names at the foot, and then he unfolded the first letter and read *the Sovereign in the second*, and the cold went down the hall from the dais to the last column.

Nobody spoke. Up in the vaulting the violet lamps swung a little on their chains, though there was no draught to move them.

“The Ashward,” said Don Salvatore, “bought a few knives.”

His voice came across the black cloth as kindly as ever, and as unhurried, the voice of a host at the end of a long supper who sees no reason on earth why anybody should go home.

“The Accord forbids them to Aloria, Sovereign. It is silent, I think, on who may buy them. There is hardly a house at this table that has not at some time bought a thing it ought not to have bought, from somebody it ought not to have met, and been very glad of it afterwards.” He spread his white hands a little on the cloth. “As for the road, the Marches are full of rogues. The Ashward has grieved over it sincerely.”

Arian gave him no answer. Along the walls the candles in their iron stands had begun to lean, all of them, one way, and the Nine watched them do it.

“I do not need a ring to know what you are, Don Salvatore,” said Arian.

“The Sovereign has no ring,” said a voice down the table.

The voice was a woman’s, the same low careful voice that had asked him at the last sitting how he lived through elven blood. She did not say it unkindly. She said it as Enzo would have said it, because it was the law and somebody at the table ought to say it.

“Then whatever the Sovereign does tonight,” she said, “any of the Nine may contest, and lay it upon the table.”

“Let them,” said Arian.

Nobody at the table breathed, which was a thing the Nine did not need to do in any case, and had seldom been so careful not to.

Arian turned back to the man in garnet.

He had made up his mind on the sill the night before, with the box open on the glass and *unafraid* lying white with frost in his hands. He found that he had made it up again, here, on the star, now, and that this time something under his ribs had heard him. It went up. It left the slow even time it had kept for twenty years and went up on its own, high and hard and fast, as a fiddle string goes up a tone when the peg is turned and stays there, singing.

He stood still in the middle of the great hall of Castel Nerezza with a heart going in him as hard as a boy’s who has climbed a wall he was told not to climb, and none of the Nine heard it. Nobody could. It made no sound that carried past his own ribs, and it could be known only by a hand laid flat over it or a head laid on his breast, and it had never occurred to anybody on the Coast to look for a heart in a Conti, and least of all in this one.

“The shadows of this Coast are the Coast’s.”

He spoke to the hall, not loudly, and it went down to the last column as cold goes along a stone floor, without hurrying. Every house at that table had been lent its share of them when the houses were made, and his own had been lent them all to keep, and had kept them a thousand years.

“The Ashward has spent its share on knives and flowers.” He looked at Don Salvatore. “I am calling it in.”

Then he asked them.

He did not lift his hand. He asked as he had asked them all his life, without words, as a man reaches in the dark for a thing on his own desk without looking for it, and they came.

The long shadows of the columns and the candle stands came first, down off the walls and out across the black glass, all one way, towards the star. Then the shadows of the lamps on their chains came down out of the vaulting and lay out with them. And then the Nine felt their own go.

He watched them feel it. Every shadow at that table came out from under its chair by inches, the plum and the smoke and the black, and stretched out across the cracked glass towards the foot of the dais, and lay there with its end at his boots and its other end still fast to whoever it belonged to, as a skein of wool runs out across a parlour floor from the lap of a woman who has let it fall.

While they lay out across the glass, the years they had kept off came up behind the high-backed chairs and stood close, as the cold stands at a man’s back when a door has been opened behind him, and the Nine felt them lean.

The woman with the careful voice looked down at her own, going away from her across the floor, and put her hand flat on the table. A lord of the lesser vineyards gripped the arms of his chair, and while he watched, the skin over his knuckles went loose and fine, as an apple’s skin goes in the loft by the end of winter, and a single white hair slid down from his temple and lay across his cheek. His uncle, at the end of the row, did not look down at all.

Don Salvatore’s did not move.

It lay under his chair in the violet light, dark and close, as it had lain under him for a great many centuries, and it kept to him. Arian looked at it. It was the oldest shadow at the table, older than his own house, and it had been told by a Caruso where to lie since before there had been Contis to tell it anything.

“I have been at a great many Long Eclipses, Sovereign,” said Don Salvatore pleasantly. “I was at the last.”

“So was I.”

“So you were.” The old voice was all gentleness. “You were twelve.”

“Give it back.”

The shadow came.

It came out from under the high-backed chair as a cloth comes off a table when the end of it is drawn, all of a piece and without hurry, and it crossed the cracked glass from the Ashward’s place to the star, and lay down along it at Arian’s feet with the others, and was still.

Don Salvatore sat in his chair with nothing under it.

The violet light lay all round him and there was no dark behind it, anywhere, and the years came to him. They had been waiting under his chair a very long time, however many there had been, and the dark had kept them off him as a cellar keeps the summer off its casks, and now there was no dark. They came all at once, and spoiled what they found.

His hair greyed from the temples back, as a black hearth greys over when the fire under it goes out. His face, which had been as smooth as Arian’s own, went fine with lines, as an old charter goes fine at its folds from being opened too often. His hands thinned on the black cloth, and the rings on them loosened.

He made no sound. He looked down at the garnet ring on his finger and turned it, and it turned easily, too easily, and he regarded it with mild interest, as a man regards a glove that has always fitted and has that moment ceased to. Then he turned his head to his granddaughter.

“You kept the ledger.”

“In my own hand, *nonno*.”

He smiled at her. It was a fond smile, and very old, and quite without surprise.

“Then the house is in good hands,” said Don Salvatore Caruso.

Then the years went on into him. The lines deepened into cracks, and the skin between them dried and drew in, as a grape draws in when it is left on the vine for the late harvest, until the smile stood out of his face all teeth, long and yellow as the keys of a harpsichord in a shut house. His cheek split along one of the cracks with the sound of a seam giving in old silk. What came out was dark and slow, thick as the lees in a cask of blood-wine, and it dried where it ran, to rust and then to powder, before it reached his collar. His eyes clouded in from their rims, as a pane clouds when the frost comes in at its corners, and sank. Inside the garnet silk a bone went with a dry crack, like a stick broken over a knee, and then another, and he leaned a little in the high-backed chair. His left hand slid off the cloth, and the fingers came away from it at the knuckles as it went, and the lesser rings went with them and rang on the glass like coins let fall on a counter.

The smell came down the table after the sound, dust and stale incense and something sweet gone over, and the woman with the careful voice turned her face aside.

He was white now, all through, the white of paper that has lain a hundred years in a window. And then he was ash.

It took fire at its edges as it came, as the flakes had in the gold of the garden, a thin red line running round all of him at once and leaving grey behind it, and he came down into his garnet silk fine and soundless, as if the ash of the Coast had been falling on him all his life and had only now been allowed to land. The silk settled after it on the high-backed chair, slowly, as a tent settles when its pole is drawn, and lay there with nobody in it.

A little of him lifted in the breath of the falling silk and settled on the toe of Arian’s boot. Arian did not look down at it.

The ring fell. It dropped from the cuff of the empty sleeve onto the black glass and rang there, once, and rolled, and came to the edge of one of the white cracks in the floor, and turned on itself there twice, a whole turn and then another, as it had been turned at every Council for longer than anybody at that table had sat on it, and lay down with its stone uppermost and was still.

Nobody moved.

The shadows of the hall lay out across the floor to Arian’s boots, every one, from every column and candle stand and lamp and every chair that still had somebody in it, as the weathercocks of the gnome trading posts in the Marches all swing round together at a change in the wind and stand pointing one way. He let them lie a moment longer, so that the Nine should look at it.

Then he let them go.

They went back. They went as the sea goes back off a causeway at the turn, every one to its own, under its own chair and its own column and its own lamp, until the hall was a hall again and every councillor had his shadow at his feet. The lord of the lesser vineyards looked at the backs of his hands, which had gone smooth again, and went on looking at them, and let the white hair lie where it had fallen.

All but one. Don Salvatore’s lay at Arian’s feet along the star, and it belonged to nobody now, and he did not want it. He did not ask it anywhere. It lay a moment, and then it went away by itself across the glass into the dark under the columns, and lay down there among the others, and was the Coast’s.

The Ashward’s share had not all come home.

He could feel where the rest of it lay. He had always felt it, without troubling to look, as a man feels the far rooms of his own house about him in the dark, and it ran out from the great hall across the Coast in long threads, thin and cold, with a Caruso at the end of every one. One was a pace from him, under the chair beside the empty garnet silk. The others were a long way off, over the harbour and up the hill behind it, where the Ashward had kept its own hall above its own quarter for longer than anybody could remember, and one went further than all of them, north, out over the strait, and was moving.

“Consigliere. The Ashward’s table.”

His uncle looked at him once, along the row. Then he took a paper from inside his coat, folded long and written in several inks, as a list is written that has been kept a great while and added to, and unfolded it on the black cloth and read it out in the ledger voice.

“Ettore Caruso. Brother to the Don. Who has kept the Ashward’s books in his own hand since before the Sovereign’s grandfather was born, and never once blotted a line.”

Arian asked.

Across the harbour, at the top of the hill, a thread went slack. He felt it come loose from whoever had kept it, as a wet knot comes loose all at once after a long pulling.

In the counting room of the Ashward’s hall, Ettore Caruso was writing a figure in the great book of the house by one violet lamp, and the shadow of his hand went along the page beside the pen. Then it lifted off the page. He looked up at the lamp, and down again, because the figure was half made and he was a careful man, and as he went on with it the hand that held the pen went down to the bone. The flesh went off it as the tide goes off the piles of a jetty, and the skin across the knuckles split with the slow sound of a page torn along its binding. The years laid themselves across his back, and he bent lower over the desk under them, and lower, until his spine went with a crack that came back off the ledgers on their shelves, and he lay down across the page with his cheek beside the figure he had not finished. Then he was ash, and the ash went over the open column in a grey drift, and the book of the Ashward had a blot on it at last. The pen lay down by itself, with the ink still wet in its nib.

The shadow was already coming. It came down the hill and over the harbour under the ash, and up the switchback and the black flank of the mountain, and in at the great doors through the crack where the two leaves met, flat and dark and quick, as ink runs along a ruled line when the bottle goes over on a clerk’s desk, and crossed the glass between the columns, and lay down at his boots.

Down the table a chair creaked, once, as a man sat further back in it than chairs are made for.

“Ottavia Caruso,” read Enzo. “Daughter to the Don. Who keeps the Ashward’s gardens at the water’s edge, where the nightbloom is grown, and who sent its seed up the hill to a cellar in the quarter.”

Ottavia Caruso was on her knees in the nightbloom at the foot of the water wall, in her gardening gloves, as she was every night at the hour the white flowers open. Her shadow got up off the bed without her and went away up the walk between the clipped box. She knelt and watched it go. She had kept a garden a great many years, and she knew what the first hard frost does to whatever has been left out in it.

It did it to her. Her face went soft and dark at the edges and fell in upon itself, as a fig falls in that has hung too long on the branch, and split, and a sweetness came off it that brought the moths. She put a gloved hand up to her cheek, and the hand came away from her wrist inside the glove. Her teeth went down among the roots one after another, as a sower lets the seed fall into the drill, and then she was ash on her knees, and the wind off the water took most of her over the wall. What it left lay at the roots of the nightbloom, and every flower in the bed opened over it at once, white in the dark.

That shadow came up from the harbour, from the walled gardens at the foot of the quarter. It came as the dusk comes up a garden walk, bed by bed, and in at the doors, and lay down beside the first.

“Ruggero Caruso. Grandson to the Don. Who carried the Ashward’s purse into the Marches this spring and told the men he paid there what road to watch, and at what hour.” Enzo kept his eyes on the paper. “On the strait tonight, in a yacht of Lowmere, with the Don’s purse for Dornhöhle.”

Out on the strait a yacht of Lowmere was going north under her lamps, and Ruggero Caruso stood at her stern rail with the purse buttoned into his coat. His shadow went over the rail. He leaned out after it and saw it go away south across the swell, long and flat and black, as oil goes out across a harbour from a broken lamp.

He was the youngest of them and had the fewest years waiting, and there were still more than a body is built to carry. The mate, coiling a line amidships, heard him cry out, which none of the others had done, and saw a young man in good black hold the rail with both hands while his hair went white under the stern lamp and blew off his head like the seed off a dandelion clock. His hands held. His arms went thin inside his sleeves, and the bones in them broke under his weight with the sound of green sticks snapped for kindling, and he went down on the planks with his hands still fast on the rail above him. The mate saw the face, and came no nearer. It had gone in to the skull, and the jaw was working at something about the purse until it came loose and hung crooked. Then the wind came over the stern and took him off the deck in one long grey streamer, out over the water after his shadow, and left the two hands on the rail. After a while it took those too.

Nobody on that yacht would touch the purse.

The shadow was a long time coming. Arian felt it start, very far off, out over the black water, and it came back the whole width of the strait to the mountain, and when the head of it came in at the doors the tail of it was still out over the water, longer than any shadow has a right to be, as the wake of a ship lies out behind her on a still night all the way back to the harbour she left. It came in last of all of them, and lay down.

“There are three more,” said Enzo, and read them, and they came. One came from a card table in the quarter, where the other players sat on over their cards a long while afterwards and none of them would be the first to get up. One came from a bath, and one from a bed where a woman slept on beside the ash until morning.

Then he turned the paper over. There was one name on the back of it, by itself, in an ink that had not yet had time to go brown.

“Valentina Caruso,” read Enzo, and the ledger voice held. “Granddaughter to the Don. Who keeps the Ashward’s papers.”

Valentina rose.

She had said on the sill that she should like to stand up, once. She came down the side of the table in her black, past the garnet silk lying empty in its chair, and knelt on the cracked glass in the violet light, and picked her grandfather’s ring up from the crack where it had come to rest, and wiped it once on the darned knuckle of her glove. She did not put it on. She got up and set it on the black cloth of the table of the Nine, before the Consigliere and beside the lacquer box, with its stone uppermost, squarely and without a sound, as she had set the box down on the glass at the sill.

Then she drew her gloves tight, the right and then the left, and looked down at her shadow.

It had begun to go. It went out from under her across the cracked glass, unhurried, as the train of a gown goes out behind a woman crossing a ballroom to claim a dance, and lay down at Arian’s boots with the others, and she let it.

There were fewer years waiting for her than had waited for her grandfather, but there were a great many more than one life can carry, and they came as quickly. Her black hair went grey from the parting outward, as a black step greys under the ash when nobody has swept it, and then white, and then it let go of her and slid down her back.

She turned her face to the great doors, which nobody was going to open for her. The skin of it drew tight, as a drumhead tightens in a dry wind, until the bones stood up under it and it split along the cheek, and she kept her mouth shut. Something gave in her knees with a crack the whole table heard, and then again, and she did not bend. She stood with her chin up and her darned glove folded over the other, as straight as she had stood at her grandfather’s side when the Sovereign refused her, and the glove went loose round the hand inside it, and then hung from her wrist empty to the knuckle. Her throat went in behind the jet thorn, until the thorn stood out from her on its pin over nothing.

Down the table the woman with the careful voice had shut her eyes. Two of the lords had lifted their boots off the glass onto the rungs of their chairs, away from their own shadows, as kitchen maids get up on the stools when a rat goes along the wainscot.

Arian watched her as he had watched her grandfather, with nothing in his face, as a man at a window watches the rain on a night he has no need to go out in.

She was still on her feet when she went.

Her black silk came down onto the glass all at once, as a curtain comes down off its pole. The thorn rang on the glass, once. Last of all the glove came down on top of the silk, with its darn uppermost and its fingers folded, and lay there. By then he was looking at the Nine.

Arian let the Ashward’s shadows go after the Don’s. They went away from his boots one by one into the dark between the columns, and were the Coast’s.

“The house of Caruso is struck from the Nine. Its children and its lesser kin will be brought down off its hill tonight and given to the houses at this table, one to a house. They will answer to whatever name the house gives them. They have no other.”

Down the table the careful voice spoke again. Its owner was looking at the empty garnet silk, and at the black silk on the glass, and at the glove.

“The Sovereign has no ring,” she said, quietly, as one reads out a line that the law requires somebody to read.

“Any of the Nine may contest it.”

Every councillor’s shadow lay under his own chair again, where it had been sent home, and every councillor sat very still on top of it with his boots drawn in close, and nobody contested anything.

His heart had not come down.

It was going still, high and hard and his own, as it had gone when he made up his mind, and it had stayed there through every name on his uncle’s paper, and it would not come down. He stood on the star with the frost white on his shoulders and the silence of the Nine round him as close and still as the dark inside a shut linen press, and under his ribs the heart a boy had counted into him went on beating in nobody’s time but Arian’s, and he did not know how to bring it back. He had never had to. It had come to him already keeping time.

And then, from a long way north, the count reached it.

It came as the water comes in the rock behind the lamp room, patient, one drop and one and one, in no hurry for anybody. It was slower than the prince’s heart had ever gone. It was slower than any heart went on its own, as a count is slow that is meant for somebody else.

Arian understood, standing in the cold before the Nine with the ash of a house on the glass round him, that the prince had felt him go, all the way down the Marches and over the strait and the dark mountains of the Coast, and was lying in the dark of some room behind the lead and counting him home. Out loud, he thought. He could not hear it. He knew it as he would have known the prince’s laugh through a wall.

His heart heard it. It came down out of its own high time as a hawk comes down out of a high wind to a glove that has been held up, patiently, a long while, and found the count, and went into it, and kept it.

He breathed out, which he had no need to do either.

“The Leaning stands,” he said to the Nine. “You set it for this hall, at the hour of the Long Eclipse. At that hour you will be at Old Meridian. Ask the shadows there.” He looked along the table, from face to face. “You have seen what they say when I ask.”

His uncle closed the lacquer box, and the steward carried it back to the step of the dais where it had come from. Arian took it up from there and turned to go, and the steward opened the great doors for him without being told.

Under the floor, in the cellar, on a stool under the old vent, a lord in mulberry velvet had heard all of it. He was Valentina’s cousin, and he had worn his flower to the Sovereign’s table, but his shadow had been lent to the inland vineyards when the houses were made, and not to the Ashward, and Arian had not asked for it. The lord had moved a Leaning. Arian meant him to be standing when it was answered. In the meantime he hoped that he had found the evening instructive.

The study fire was roaring when he came up, as it roared every night, with every candle flame down the long room leaning towards the gold chair as if somebody were still sitting in it, and Vesna was standing in front of the hearth with the cloak in her hands.

She had spread a square of clean linen on the seat of the gold chair and the cloak on the linen, and she was folding it. She did it as she folded everything, briskly, without waste, a fold and a fold and a turn, with the darned hem tucked under and the stained collar turned in, until a child’s grey cloak lay on the linen no larger than a book of hours, with the firelight red on the silver of its stitches and the gold sunburst uppermost.

“His Highness asked me to keep it,” said Arian.

“His Highness said until he wanted it.” She folded the linen over it, corner over corner. “He’ll want it.”

She laid it on the corner of the desk and stepped back from it.

He took it up in both hands. It weighed nothing, as it had weighed nothing on the night it was put round him in the roses. He opened his coat and put it inside, against his chest, over the left side, where his uncle’s hand had stopped short in the lamp room, and buttoned the coat over it, and laid his own hand flat on the place, as a man lays his hand on a purse he means to carry through a crowd.

The prince had slept like that all across the Marches. Arian had thought he was keeping a place.

His thumb moved to the bare place beside his rings and turned there, at nothing.

Vesna was watching it, with her mouth gone into a line, and her hand went to her apron pocket again, to the needle case, and stayed there.

“Sovereign.”

He waited.

She opened her mouth. He watched something come up in her and wait behind her face, as the prince’s questions waited behind his when he had decided not to ask them, and he heard her heart go once, hard, like a fist brought down on a kneading board.

“It’s not mine to say.”

“What is not?”

“Where a thing goes, when it’s not where you left it.” She took her hand out of her pocket and smoothed her apron down over it, twice, as if there were flour on it, though there was not. “His Highness keeps things, Sovereign. He keeps everything. You’ve seen his pockets.”

She held his look and did not give him one inch more, and her face set as it set over a scone that had come out right.

“Then whose is it to say?”

“His.”

“Then I will ask him.”

“You do that.” She pushed a napkin along the desk to him, tied at the corners. It was warm, and it smelled of butter. “Three. For His Highness. They’ll be stale by the Citadel. He’ll eat them.”

The *Spaarzaamheid* stood where she had stood once before, out at the end of the harbour with her lamps lit and her tar shining, and two casks stood on the wet stone at the foot of her gangway, side by side, with their lids off.

Maartje Bosboom stood at the top of the gangway in a fresh cap, with her ledger on its chain held open against her forearm.

“I said Van Houten and Daughters didn’t mean to make a habit of it, Sovereign. I said it on this quay. I want it remembered.”

“It is remembered.”

“Twice isn’t a habit.” She wrote something. “Twice is a custom, and Van Houten keeps customs.” She looked down at the second cask, and at the Consigliere of the Coast standing beside it in his dove grey with a despatch case held under his arm, and her round brown face did something complicated under the starched peak of her cap. “I’ve had them both scrubbed.”

The ash came down past the quay lamps, soft and grey in the violet and without end, and their light lay gold on the wet black stone. Behind them the switchback went up into it to the black flank of the fortress, where the guest wing stood dark in every window. Its people had gone down at the bell in four carriages with the curtains drawn, under the sergeant’s lycans, and there was nobody called Caruso in any of them. Across the harbour, on the hill above the quarter, the Ashward’s own hall stood dark from its gate to its roof, with one lamp going slowly through it from window to window where the Watch was searching the rooms.

Above it all, high on the mountain, the underside of the ash was faintly gold where the garden lay under it, and would go on being gold, Arian supposed, with nobody in it to see.

The sergeant stood under the lamps with his fist to his heart, and Mirek beside him on his hazel crutch.

“The hill’s down, Sovereign,” said the sergeant. “Seven little ones, and the kin that’s under age, and an old nurse who wouldn’t be left. They’re in the Watch house with a fire lit, for what a fire’s worth to them. The littlest keeps asking what she’s called now.” He said the rest to the wet stone of the quay. “There was ash in the chair in the counting room, and the pen lying on the book, and the book gone grey. The last figure wasn’t finished. The ink was still wet on it. The lady in the gardens was only her gloves. There was a hand in one, gone to ash, and it went when my lad picked it up. Every flower in the place had come open round them.”

“The Consigliere has the list.”

“Sovereign.” The sergeant let it lie a moment. “Forty of us to the Marches with the Nine, at the end of the week. They’ll get there.”

“And stay there.”

“They’ll stay there.” The sergeant looked at the casks, and then at Arian, and his heart caught on its old catch, once, as a gate sticks on its latch in the wind. His breath went up white in the cold. “You’ll be in the noon.”

“Yes.”

Mirek opened his mouth, and the sergeant put a hand on his shoulder, and he shut it. Then he opened it again anyway.

“Bring him back, Sovereign.”

“Yes.”

His uncle was climbing into the cask.

He did it as he did everything, with his face composed and his coat-tails gathered in one hand and the despatch case held clear of the oak in the other, one long leg and then the second, as a man steps into a boat he does not trust at a jetty he has never liked. He sat down inside it. He settled the case on his knees, and the broken chain in the breast of his coat, and laid his hand flat over both, and looked up over the rim at his nephew with a face that gave away nothing at all.

“Your aunt,” he said, “would have laughed until she wept.”

“Yes,” said Arian.

“Get in, Arian.”

He got in. The oak closed round him, smelling of the Coast’s best and two scrubbings, and Maartje stood over the cask with the lid held out before her at the full stretch of her arms and looked down at the lacquer box on his knees, and the napkin of scones on the box, and his hand flat on his coat over whatever was folded under it, and did not enter a remark.

“Upright. Not to be broached.” It was the custom. The lid came down, and her knuckles came twice on the oak above his head, and went off to the other cask and came twice there.

The yacht lifted at the bell and turned her tarred stern to the mountain and went north into the ash.

He felt her come out of it a long while after, into the dark of the Marches under stars he could not see, and then much later into the day, when the sun came onto the deck above the leaded planks like a weight set down on a floor, and walked there.

Near the end of that first day, over the high Marches, his uncle’s voice came to him once through two thicknesses of oak.

“The pie man has written,” said Enzo.

Arian turned his head towards the voice.

The paper rustled in the dark of the other cask, and his uncle read it out in the ledger voice, as he read the Nine their accounts.

“*Your gentleman come down the foundry lane the other night in the Guard’s white, with the High Paladin, and the gold coming and going at the edge of his hood, and your black on him under the white. I says mutton, Highness, best in Aloria. He says he is told it is not difficult.*”

Arian said nothing.

“He was quoting me.”

The hold creaked round them. Somewhere above, on the deck, the sun walked from one rail to the other.

“Your aunt had a word for everybody she meant to keep,” said his uncle at length. “I am considering one for him.”

“Which word?”

“I shall tell him first,” said Enzo.

\* \* \*

The prince began to count a little after the dark came again over the Marches.

Arian felt it start, as he had felt it start every night, slow and even under his ribs, and he sat with his back to the cold staves and the box on his knees and his hand at his breast, and counted with him. He could hear his uncle not sleeping in the next cask, and Maartje on the ladder, coming down to look at them and going up again, and under everything the hum of a Lowmere yacht going north in the night towards a white city that had never had one.

It was the eve of the eve.

*Stay in the dark*, the prince had said, going up past the doors in white silk, and Arian had stayed, that once, because he had been asked. Nobody had asked him since. The day after tomorrow, under a black sun, a dwarf in Dornhöhle would lift a hammer over an iron casket with seven bars and a number on its end, and at Old Meridian the Nine would ask the shadows about a bare hand. That was the day after.

Tomorrow was the noon, white from one wall of the Citadel to the other. He had been told all his life that it would kill him. He had never seen it. He had been promised notes on it, and the prince kept his promises, and somewhere at the north end of the thread there would be a pocket with the noon of the Citadel written on the backs of things, in the long untidy hand, waiting for somebody to read them who had never seen it.

He found that he did not want to be read to.

He wanted to look.

The count went on. It went past a hundred, and past two hundred, slow and steady and unhurried, in the time of a boy kneeling in the wet of a broken glasshouse with his hands on a stranger’s, and Arian’s heart kept it, and the prince’s kept it, and they were the same time, and had been these twenty years. At three hundred, close now, somewhere above him in the white city, the prince stopped.

Arian kept his hand over the cloak all the way through the dark and into the morning, and kept the place.
