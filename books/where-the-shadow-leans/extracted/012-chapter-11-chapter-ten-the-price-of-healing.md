## Chapter 11: Chapter Ten - The Price of Healing

*The student will ask why the children of the Umbral houses are not to be touched, even in mercy, even at the point of death. Let him be told that the Grace remembers whatever it has mended, and whatever it has mended remembers the Grace, and that there are some things it is kinder to let forget.*

— From the physicians’ primer of the Harp Vales, *On the Rule of Touch*

\* \* \*

The cave had been kept for sheep, and nobody had ever told it otherwise.

They came to it in the last of the dark, down a goat track that went sliding away down the north wall of the Fault in loose stone and frost, with the sentinel’s lamp shuttered to a slit ahead of them and the stars going out one by one along the ribbon of sky. The lycans who could walk carried the ones who could not, and the frost of the track took their blood in dark coins as they went, for the morning to find. Paul carried Mirek, and last of all, at the pace of the slowest, came Caleb, with the Sovereign of the Obsidian Coast leaning on his shoulder under a travelling cloak that did not suit him.

Arian had not spoken since the road. He set each foot down on the frost as if the track had been laid for him by a careful steward and he did not wish to give offence, and under Caleb’s arm, through the black silk, the warmth went on climbing. It was the wrong warmth. Caleb knew the warmth of a fever from the warmth of a man, and this was neither. It was the warmth of a stone that has lain all day in the sun, borrowed and spreading, and it did not belong to the body it was in. Where Arian’s boots came down the frost sighed and went to water, and behind them the track went back up the mountain in dark wet footprints, each breathing a thread of steam into the starlight.

The sentinel untied the hazel hurdle across the cave mouth as gently as if the shepherds were asleep inside, and they went in. It smelled of a great many sheep over a great many winters, of fleece grease and old straw and, under all of that, cold smoke. A pair of wooden clogs stood side by side on a shelf of rock beside the hearth, as if their owner had stepped out of them one spring evening and meant to step back in at the first snow. From the floor to as high as a tall man could reach, the wall was scratched with the shepherds’ tallies in fives, and beside some of them somebody had drawn a little horned head, with great care and no skill whatever, for a ram that had been loved, or a great deal of trouble, or both. High in one corner, crooked and all on its own, someone had drawn the moon.

“*Kolyska*,” said the sentinel, setting his lamp on the hearthstones. “The cradle, Highness. They bring the ewes down here for the lambing when it comes early.” He turned his grey-flecked head towards the back of the cave. “It goes back a long way.”

Behind them, over the far wall of the Fault, the strip of sky had gone from black to the soft grey of a dove’s breast. Caleb could feel the morning at his back, faint and certain, like the warmth of a hand held near the skin and not yet laid on it.

“Then we go to the back of it.”

The lycans of the escort stayed by the hearth with their wounded, where the sun would find them in an hour and do them nothing but good. In the second chamber the shepherds had built lambing pens of hazel hurdles against the walls, each the size of a cupboard bed, and Paul laid Mirek down in the nearest and stayed on his knees beside him. Caleb went on with Arian on his shoulder, until the sentinel’s lamp stopped, and its shutter closed, and it was dark.

This was the dark of a mountain’s inside, older than the Coast’s and deeper, and nothing in it gave anything back. The only light in the world was his own. It came up faint and gold in his skin, at his wrists and his jaw and in the hand that held Arian round the waist, and lay on the rock for a yard about them and no further, as the light of a lantern lies on the snow about a man walking home across a field.

The back chamber of *Kolyska* was round and low and dry, and the shepherds kept their fleeces there, rolled and tied with twine and smelling powerfully of their late owners. Caleb unrolled two of them on the floor with the air of a footman laying a carpet for a king, and found that he was close to laughing, and stopped, because if he began he did not think he would be able to stop. Arian let himself be helped down onto them, which frightened Caleb more than anything had yet, and sat against the stone with the travelling cloak fallen open about him.

Through the back wall ran a vein of the Fault’s old glass, two fingers wide and as brown as barley sugar. It caught Caleb’s light and gave it back dull and deep, like the eye of an old hound dozing by a fire.

“Show me,” said Caleb.

Arian took his hand from his side, and Caleb put back the black coat and the silk beneath it.

The lines had gone on growing. On the carriage step they had reached the edge of his cuff. Now they had climbed the ribs, a lacework of light under the white skin, and where they ran thickest the skin had begun to cook. It had puckered along their courses and gone the yellow of old wax, and in places it had split, as the skin of an apple splits in the oven, and from the splits a thin dark blood crept out and smoked in the cold and smelled of the forge. The mouth of the wound had curled back from the light inside it, brown at the lip and then black. The topmost line lay against the fourth rib on the left side, a hair’s breadth below the heart, and it pulsed, faintly, and with every pulse it put out another hair of gold and ate a little further into him. Arian’s breath went up white between them in the cold and hung there.

Caleb laid two fingers on the inside of his wrist, out of a habit older than his memory, to count.

There was a pulse.

He had felt it twice before, in the great hall and at the garden doors, and both times he had told himself that it was his own blood beating back at him through the touch. He could not tell himself so now. His own heart was going in his ears like a smith’s hammer on a bad morning. The pulse under his fingers went on beneath it at its own pace, faint and slow, as unhurried as a ferryman’s oar on still water.

He counted twelve beats of it, and took his fingers away.

“Your heart is beating.”

“Yes.”

“Your kind have no heartbeat. It is the second thing the tutors teach us of your kind.” His voice came out level, and he did not know how. “Is it the sliver? Is it the light in you?”

“No.”

“Then —”

“Not now,” said Arian.

He said it with his eyes closed, like a man setting down something heavy on a step to get his breath, meaning to pick it up again, and Caleb heard it so and let it lie.

“How long?” asked Arian.

The Harp Vales had taught Caleb to answer that truthfully to anybody who asked it with their eyes open, and he had always thought it the hardest thing they taught.

“Noon.”

Arian’s eyes opened, and found Caleb’s face in the gold of Caleb’s light.

“My uncle will see to everything. You will want for nothing.”

Then he shut his eyes again, as a notary lays down his pen when the last clause has been read and nothing remains but the seal.

Caleb found a fear in himself that he had not met before. It came up through the body as cold as well water and left the mind perfectly clear behind it, like a room after the guests have gone.

“Hold still. I am going to see to Mirek’s leg. Then I am coming back.”

\* \* \*

Mirek lay in the lambing pen with his head in Paul’s lap, grey to the lips and doing his best not to make a sound about it. The sentinel’s lamp hung from a peg on the hurdle above him and threw the shadow of the hazel withies over the boy like the shadow of a basket over a hen.

Caleb knelt at his feet and cut the boot away with the fourth blade of Mirek’s dwarven knife, which Mirek told him through his teeth was the one for boots, and the leather fell open like a letter that had only been waiting for the knife. Under it the shin bone had gone through in one clean break a hand’s breadth below the knee, and the lower half had turned on the upper, so that the foot lay at an angle that made the eye want to look away. The broken end of the upper bone pressed up under the skin of the shin and stood there, tenting it white and tight as a drumhead, so that one more jolt on the goat track would have brought it through. In another hour the swelling would be too high to set it without more pain than Mirek had in him to bear.

His palms warmed at the sight of it, eager and ready, as they always did at a wound. He closed his hands until the light lay down again.

“Highness,” said Mirek. He had seen Caleb close his hands. “You’re not going to —”

“No. I am not going to spend so much as a candle’s worth of light on you, and you are going to thank me for it when you are older. I am going to do this the way they taught me before they let me do it the other way.” He began to tear the lace from his cuffs. It had survived three weeks in the east wing and a night in the earth of the garden and the Hall of the Chain, and it did not survive Mirek’s shin. “It will hurt a great deal. You may say anything you like to me while I do it, in any language you know. I shall take none of it personally.”

“Save it,” insisted Mirek. His eyes went past Caleb into the dark of the passage. “For him.”

“I know. I heard you the first time.”

Paul cut two straight staves out of the hurdle and sat behind the boy to hold him, and Mirek bit on the leather of Paul’s belt, folded twice. Caleb took the foot in both hands and set his own heel against the boy’s good ankle, and drew breath.

“Paul. Tell him about King Aurel. The dragon. He has never heard it.”

For a moment Paul did not seem to understand. Then he bent his head over Mirek’s and began, in his low voice with the Highland March in every vowel of it, as if they were alone by a guardroom fire. “There was a king of the Solar House, a long time ago. Aurel, his name was. He had a very dull reign —”

Caleb pulled.

He pulled long and slow and steady, as the temple physicians had taught him, leaning back with his whole weight until the leg grew to its proper length and he felt the two broken ends grind against each other through the swollen flesh and find the line. Mirek screamed into the leather. His nails had gone to claws with the pain, as a lycan’s will whether he wishes it or not, and they went into Paul’s forearm and drew four red lines down it to the wrist. Paul held him, and went on with the dull reign of King Aurel without missing a word. Caleb turned the foot, a little and then a little more, against the hot resistance of the swelling, and all at once he felt the ends meet and lock under his fingers, as the two halves of a broken plate go together in the hand when the edges are true.

“— and so the king said,” Paul was saying, “that if he must have a dragon, it had better be one that could keep accounts —”

Mirek had stopped screaming. He was weeping instead, quietly, with the leather still in his teeth, and listening.

Caleb bound the staves with the lace of his cuffs and the silk of his cravat and, when those were gone, with a strip torn from the hem of his own shirt, firm and even from the knee to the ankle. The leg lay straight on the straw between its two hazel staves with the lace of the Solar House wound round it in bands, like a branch of may tied up with ribbons on a lycan feast day.

Mirek let the belt fall out of his mouth. “You didn’t use the light,” he said, hoarsely.

“You told me not to, and for once in your life you were quite right. I mean to tell your mother so the moment we are home. How is it?”

Mirek considered his leg, in its staves and its lace.

“*Prächtig*,” he breathed, and closed his eyes.

Over the boy’s head Paul looked at Caleb at last, with wet eyes, and away again as soon as he had looked.

“Highness.” It came out of Paul like something dragged. “The Sovereign. Will he —”

“Not if I can help it,” promised Caleb, and went back into the dark.

\* \* \*

Arian had not moved. He sat on the shepherds’ fleeces where Caleb had left him, with his eyes closed and the gold going on under his skin. The topmost line had crossed the fourth rib.

Caleb knelt down beside him and pushed back his sleeves.

“No,” said Arian.

“I have not said anything.”

“You have pushed back your sleeves.”

Caleb had no answer to that, so he gave his diagnosis instead, plainly and to the face, as he had been taught.

“It is in you, and it is coming undone, and it will go on coming undone until there is none of it left and none of you either. Light knows light, Sovereign. Mine is the only light in this mountain that you can bear, and the only light the sliver will answer to. I can go in after it with the Star-Grace and gather every thread that has come loose, and draw the rest of it out by the road it came in.”

“And then?”

“And then I expect you will be insufferable about it for a month.”

Arian opened his eyes.

“*A healer who spends beyond his store where the sun cannot refill it may find the Grace will not be recalled*,” he said. He said it quietly, without any weight, in the voice he used for ledgers, and every word was the primer’s. “*Having nothing left to spend, it spends the healer.*”

Caleb stared at him.

“You have read the primer.”

“Yes.”

He thought of a slim water-stained book that had come unasked on his first day in the east wing, which no one would explain. There was no time to ask, and he let it go.

“A sliver of sunlight on a stick,” said Arian.

“I did say that. I was being rhetorical.”

“The sun is fifty paces off.”

The shadows had begun to come out of the walls of the chamber, quietly, the way mist comes up off a river at dusk, and gather about him on the fleece.

“No.”

“Sovereign —”

“*No*.”

The shadows lay round him, deep and still. Caleb’s own light went up a little against them, gold against black, the way a hearth answers the bellows, and neither gave way.

“Why?” said Arian.

He asked it plainly, without any heat in it at all, as Caleb had asked him on the ledge of the Fault. He seemed truly to want to know.

“It would kill you.”

Arian was quiet a long moment in the gold.

Then his hand came up off his knee and closed round Caleb’s wrist, gently, and hard. It was warm, which was wrong, and the warmth went into Caleb’s skin like a wrong note into a song.

Caleb turned his own hand a little in that grip and laid his thumb inside Arian’s wrist, on the place where he had opened a rogue’s knife hand on the Fault road. He laid it there lightly. He did not press.

“I know where the strings of this hand are, Sovereign,” he warned. “I learned them before I learned to dance. Do not make me use them.”

The grip held for three of Caleb’s heartbeats, which were going hard and quick. Then it opened, of itself, before he could touch the place, and lay open on Caleb’s wrist a moment longer, and slid away.

Arian turned his head to the passage.

“Walker.”

Paul came a pace out of the dark of the passage, a great shape beyond the border of Caleb’s light, with his face pale at the edge of it and his hands hanging empty at his sides.

“Sovereign.”

“When it begins to spend him, you will take him into the sun. You will not wait for his leave.”

He looked at Paul in the dark, steadily, for what seemed to Caleb a long time, and Paul looked back at him and did not look away, which Caleb had not seen him do since the Citadel. There was no heat in the look at all. Caleb knew all the same, as one knows the drop beyond a parapet in the dark, what would become of Paul if he waited.

“Yes, Sovereign.”

Arian glanced once at the passage, where Caleb’s light gave out on the rock, and Paul went and stood there.

“You have just forbidden it,” protested Caleb.

“Yes.”

“And now you are arranging it.”

Arian laid his head back against the rock.

“I have met you.”

\* \* \*

He laid both hands flat over the wound, the left above it and the right below, in the warm wrong skin, and found the heart beneath them, and counted, and called.

The Star-Grace came up out of all of him together, the bronze well and the high pass and the long gold mornings of the Marches, all of it banked in his blood, and it rose in him like the river in the Fault when the snow lets go in spring. He felt his eyes go to amber, and the heat come into them. The round chamber went gold to its roof, every grain of the rock standing up in it, and in the back wall the vein of old glass woke and burned the colour of barley sugar held to a lamp. The shadows drew back to the edges of the chamber, slowly and unwillingly, the way a crowd goes back from a fire, and lay there, and watched.

The light went in, and he went with it, feeling the hurt as the body felt it, from the inside. At the garden doors, for a burn across two knuckles, he had not needed to go deep. He went deep now.

It was cold in there.

It was colder than the high passes, colder than iron in a frost, a cold that had never once been warm and had stopped expecting to be. And it was dark, and larger than any body had a right to be from the inside. He had lain at its threshold once, in the black gallery, and felt the loneliness of it come through the bite like a sound through a wall, a great house shut up with every room empty. Now he was inside the house, and the house was on fire.

The sun had got in. It had gone through the rooms of that dark house the way a careless lamplighter goes through a mansion, touching a flame to everything, the curtains and the panelling and the beds, and moving on, and behind it the fine gold lines went running up every wall and along every passage, branching, catching, eating into the dark with little bright mouths. He could see where every one of them had come from, back along the passages to a cellar under the ribs where something lay burning on its side like a candle dropped in straw.

He saw it as a physician too, under the house and the fire, and that was worse. Wherever a gold line ran, the fine dark vessels along it had cooked as brittle as leaves in a drought, and the blood in them, which ought to have gone cold and slow and black, had boiled where it stood and thickened to pitch. The flesh on either side had gone to a grey ash that kept its form only until it was touched, like the ash of a log that keeps the grain of the wood until somebody stirs the fire. And the nearest of the lines was reaching for the heart.

He began at the edges.

He called the threads, and they came. Light knew light, as he had told Arian, and every loose thread of the sliver that had wandered off into the dark of that house knew him and turned at the sound of him and came back down the passages towards his hands, the way bees come home to the skep when the country women of the Vales beat on it with a spoon. He gathered them in. Behind each one he went after the scorch it had left, as a housekeeper goes after the lamplighter, pinching out what he lit and smoothing the burn from the plaster, and where his own light went the walls of that cold house closed over smooth again, and dark, and whole.

It cost, as he had expected. In the Citadel the sun had poured in behind him as fast as he poured out, and in the garden on the Coast the crystals had done the same. Here there was nothing behind him but rock. Every thread he called cost him, and every scorch he smoothed, and he could feel himself going down, as one feels the level going down in a cracked jug held in both hands, slowly, and always, and never once coming back up. His teeth began to chatter. He set his jaw on them and went on, and the cold came up into the roots of them, and his fingertips on Arian’s ribs went from gold to white.

The topmost line had nearly reached the heart. He caught it a hair’s breadth before it touched, and drew it back, and smoothed the burn behind it, and came, because he could not help it, to the heart.

It lay at the middle of the house like the hearth of the one room that is kept, beating, and he was close enough now to feel the whole of it go, slow and warm and patient, lifting the cold about it with every stroke as a sleeper’s breath lifts a blanket.

There was light in it already.

It was old and faint and gold. It was worked through the walls of that heart as gold thread is worked through a veil, so fine and so long settled there that it was part of the weave. It did not burn. It was as unlike the sliver in the cellar as a hand is unlike a brand. And Caleb knew it the way he knew his own hand in the dark, the way a man knows his own voice when it comes back to him across a valley, for nothing he had ever touched had been so plainly his.

Lower down, on the left, where the last of the ribs went round, he found the old wound.

It was long closed. It had been a deep wound once, and a bad one, made by sun-forged silver, and he could feel the faint cold sourness of it still in the scar. And it had been closed with light. The seam of it was gold. It had been closed by someone who did not yet know how it was done, with stitches as large and as earnest as the first row of a child’s sampler, too many of them and none of them quite straight, as if whoever had made them had been afraid that if he stopped putting them in, the whole thing would come undone.

He had felt this flesh know the way at the garden doors, and smelled warm glass and wet earth on an iron mezzanine above the garden on the Coast, and driven through a valley of glowing mist with his palms prickling in his lap and put his hand over a closed fist and felt, for a heartbeat, broken glass above him and a cold hand under his own, and every time, every single time, he had told himself it was the Blight, or the dark, or the mist, and let it go.

He had been here before.

And the bridge opened.

It opened the other way. He had gone in of his own accord this time, carrying the light himself, and the house knew it, and opened from within. Every shut door in it came open at once, down the whole length of every passage, as if the house had been waiting twenty years for a key and heard it in the lock, and through every door, in every room, there was the same thing, and there had always been the same thing, kept, and looked at, and put away, and taken out again, for twenty years.

It was a glasshouse.

\* \* \*

He was eight years old, and the sun was going out, and he was bored.

He was standing among the silk tents of the Solar House on the slope across the valley from Old Meridian, in his good grey cloak, which he had been told on no fewer than four occasions that morning was not to be got muddy, and the whole of his family was looking at the sky. They held up little squares of smoked crystal on silver handles and looked through them, down to the grooms in the horse lines, and the great crystals on their poles at every corner of the tents had begun to dim one by one, as lamps dim at the end of a long party when nobody has thought to trim them. Matilda had put him up on her horse in the morning and led him the whole length of the lines, and he had been the tallest person in the camp for an hour, which had been glorious. Cecily had cried because she was not allowed to come too, and Eleanor had told her that weeping was not an argument, and Cecily had said that it was the only one she had. That had been the morning. Now nobody would talk to him, because they were all looking up.

So he looked up too, through Eleanor’s spare crystal, and the sun was a coin with a bite out of it, and then a coin with most of itself bitten away, and then it was black.

It was black, and round, and it had a ring of white fire about its edge like the rim of a cup held up to a window. The light that came from it was no colour he knew. It was the colour of the inside of a mussel shell, all pale and shifting, and the mist in the valley below had drunk it and was glowing with it, and the white city on the far hill stood up out of the mist like a city in a story his nurse was not allowed to tell him. The shadows had gone strange. He held up his hand and looked for its shadow on the grass, and the grass all round it was the same dim colour the shadow ought to have been. The shadow had spilled everywhere at once, and there was no longer any telling it from the day. A dog in the horse lines lay down and put its nose under its paws. Far off across the valley, somewhere in the white city, a horn was blowing, on and on, and nobody in the tents seemed to hear it. Then it stopped in the middle of its note, as a blackbird stops when the cat has it, and nobody heard that either.

And his hands began to hurt.

It began as a prickling all across the palms, as if he had held them too near the nursery fire and then put them into the snow, and it grew. The tutors in the Harp Vales, where he had gone at six and very proud of it, had told him that a healer’s hands would always know a wound before his head did. He understood it now, because his hands were pulling at him. They were pulling him away from the tents, and down the slope, and across the valley through the mist towards the white city, as a hazel rod pulls at the wrists of a water-finder, and he went where they pulled him, because he was eight, and nobody was looking.

He went down through the mist with his arms and his elbows out, running, the way he ran before anyone had taught him to run like a gentleman. The mist went by him in long slow skeins, cool and soft and full of that pale shell-coloured light, and he came up out of it into the streets. They were white, and worn smooth as the steps of a temple, and they went up the hill between broken walls and arches that held up nothing, and he went up them, past a fountain with no water in it and a stone lady in the middle of it who had lost her face, past doorways with trees growing out of them, up and up, with his hands burning now and his breath coming short.

There was blood on the stones.

He had seen blood, a whole season of it in the Harp Vales, and been sick in a basin and been allowed to stay. This blood was darker, nearly black in that strange light, and it smelled wrong. There was iron in it, as there was in all blood, but over the iron lay something sharp and cold and sour, a bright clean smell like a silver spoon left lying in the rain, and he did not know what it was, and he did not like it, and his hands pulled him on.

It went up a narrow street, on the stones, and then on the foot of a broken column, and then on a doorframe, where a hand had pressed, a hand smaller than a man’s. It went up the last of the hill. It went in at the open door of a glasshouse.

The glasshouse stood all by itself at the top of the hill, in a wild garden gone to briars, and he stood at the edge of the briars and looked at it. It was made of iron so thin and so curved that it looked as if it had been drawn in the air with a pen and left there, and the iron went up into a dome, and the dome had been made all of glass, once. Now most of it was gone. What was left hung here and there from the ribs, clouded and cracked and tilted, and through the gaps the mist came drifting in and drifting out again as it pleased, and high above the dome, through the empty ribs, hung the black sun with its ring of white fire, looking down.

He went through the briars. They caught at his cloak, and he held it up out of their way, because of the mud. He went in at the door.

It was cold inside. It was cold and grey and still, and there were roses growing out of the black earth all over the floor, old ones, gone to briars too, with not one leaf on them anywhere, only thorns. And among the roses, lying on his side in the earth and curled round himself, there was a boy.

He was older than Caleb, a great deal older, older even than Cecily. He wore a black coat with a high collar, as good as anything at home, and it was torn open at the side and wet all down, and the earth under him was dark and soft and wet. There was a smell coming off him that Caleb knew from the kitchens at home, the smell of a roast the cook has let catch, and it came from the side where the fist was. One of his hands was pressed to his side under the torn coat. It was pressed there in a fist, so hard that the bones of it showed white, and between them, slowly, the blood was coming out. His face was whiter than anyone’s face Caleb had ever seen, white as the streets, white as milk in a bowl. His hair was black. His eyes were open, and black too, black all the way across, without any middle to them at all.

They looked at Caleb.

And in the same moment, seeing with a second pair of eyes, Caleb was lying on the earth among the roses, with the cold going out of him through the hole in his side and the sun-forged silver in it singing, and he had been lying there longer than he could count. Before that he had been running, uphill, with his hand in his side. Before that there had been the fountain court, and the mist, and his father stepping between him and it without a word, and then one word, *Corri*, said over his father’s shoulder without turning his head, as he said everything, as a thing that would be done. He had run. He had looked back once from the mouth of the street and seen three men come out of the mist with hoods that held nothing where their faces should have been, and seen the silver go into his father, and into him, and into him again, and heard the sound his father made, which was no sound he had known a Sovereign could make, and seen the dark blood go out of him over the white stones and down into the dry basin of the fountain, more of it than he had known a body could hold. Then a fourth had come at him out of the mist, and the cold had gone in under his ribs, and turned, and he had run again.

Now he was looking at the door.

A light was coming in at it. It was low down, about as high as a child, and it was gold, a faint warm gold that moved, the way a lantern moves when a child is sent across a yard with it and swings it as he goes, and he thought, quite clearly and without any fear at all, that it was death, and that he had not known death would be so short, or so badly dressed, for it had briars on its cloak and mud to the knee. Then the light came nearer, and it had a face. It was a round face, and brown-eyed, with a smudge on the nose and the gold lying all over it like the down on a peach, and it was looking at him. Nobody had ever looked at him like that, and he had no word for it in any language he had been taught.

“Go away,” said the boy among the roses.

“You’re bleeding,” said Caleb.

“Go away.” The boy’s voice was thin and fierce and Vesperian all through, with the Coast rolling under the Alorian like water under ice. “Can you not see what I am?”

Caleb looked at the black coat and the white face and the fist and the blood.

“You’re a boy in a coat.”

The boy drew his lips back from his teeth. It was meant to be terrible. Caleb could see that it was meant to be terrible, and he waited politely for it to be so, but it was mostly just teeth, and rather white, and the boy was shaking too much to hold the face for long.

“I am of the Umbral houses. I am the Sovereign’s son. I am a vampire, and you are an elf, and you are made all through of the only thing in the world that can kill me, and I could kill you, if I wanted. You should be afraid of me.”

Caleb considered this.

“Matilda says vampires eat children who don’t go to bed.”

“We do not eat children.”

“Then I’m not afraid of you,” said Caleb. “So there.”

The boy stared at him. For a moment Caleb thought he might be going to laugh. He shut his eyes instead, and his fist pressed harder into his side, and he said through his teeth, “It is silver. It will not close. Nothing closes silver. You should run.”

“My hands want to,” said Caleb, which was not quite an answer, and knelt down in the roses.

The earth was wet. It came cold through the knees of his breeches at once, soaking, and he thought, *Mother*, and *the cloak*, and then did not think about either of them again. He put out his hand. The boy’s fist was pressed to the wound, white and hard, with the blood coming out slowly between the knuckles, and Caleb laid his own hand over it, flat, as he had seen the physicians lay a hand on a frightened animal.

It was cold. It was the coldest thing he had ever touched, colder than the snow on the high passes in the stories, colder than the inside of the ice house at the Citadel where he was not allowed to go and went, and it ran up into his shoulder and made his teeth hurt, and he kept his hand where it was.

“You have to let go. I can’t get in.”

The fist stayed shut.

“Please.”

And slowly, under his hand, it opened. It opened one finger at a time, as if every finger had to be asked separately and had to think it over, until the boy’s hand lay open and flat against his own side with Caleb’s brown hand, half the size of it, lying over the back of it, and between their fingers the wound, and the blood, and the silver singing in it. The lips of the wound had gone black. The blood came up out of it dark and slow and seething, as if something down in there were on the boil, and the smell came up with it.

Caleb shut his eyes, as the tutors had taught him, and found the place, and called.

It came. It rushed up out of him, quicker than it had ever come at the Vales, quicker than it came for grazes and burns and the paw of the kitchen cat, and it went into the cold hand under his and through it and into the hurt, and he went with it. Nobody had told him yet what silver did, or that it could not be done, and so he did it, and the wound fought him. It was like trying to close a door against a wind with the wind on the other side of it pushing, and he pushed back, and every time he got the door a little way shut the silver sang and the door came open again. He pushed harder. He pushed with everything he had, and when that was not enough, something else in him that he had never felt before, something very deep and very old that lived down at the bottom of him under the light, opened one eye, and looked, and lent him its shoulder.

The door began to close.

He counted while he did it, out loud, because the tutors had told him always to count the heart, and because he was frightened, and counting helped. “One. Two. Three.”

“What are you counting?” asked the boy. His voice had gone a long way off.

“Your heart.”

“I have not got one.”

Caleb opened one eye. “Everybody’s got one.”

“Not us. Not the Umbral houses. It is the first thing they teach us.”

Caleb thought about this, with his hand on the cold hand and his light draining into it.

“Then I’ll count mine,” he offered, “and you can borrow it,” and he shut his eye again, and began again, from one, out loud, slowly, in the even voice the tutors used, one, and two, and three, and four, all the way up, and on.

And the light poured out of him, and the black sun overhead gave none of it back.

He felt that. He had never felt it before, because until that day he had never been anywhere the sun was not. He felt the light going out of him, and out, and nothing coming in behind it, like a pond being drawn off through a sluice with no stream to fill it, and he felt the cold coming up in him where the light had been. Something warm ran out of his nose and over his lip, and he licked it away, and it tasted of pennies. He did not stop. He was eight years old and very frightened, and he was kneeling in the cold wet earth of a broken glasshouse at the top of a dead city with his hands in a vampire’s blood, and he was not going to run away.

And the glasshouse grew warm.

It began at his hands and went out from him over the floor, the way warmth goes out across a cold room from a newly lit stove, and as it went the grey went out of the air and the air turned gold. It was a soft, still, golden air, like the air of a summer afternoon in a walled garden, with the bees gone heavy and the stones giving back the heat. The broken panes still hanging from the ribs went warm in it, all of them, as if the sun were shining on them, and the mist that came drifting in at the gaps came in cold and grey and turned gold the moment it crossed the iron, and lay down over the two of them, and was warm. And all round him, in the black earth, the old roses woke. He saw it happen, under his own light. On every bare thorned stem a bud swelled and split and put out a leaf, crumpled and red at the edges, as new rose leaves are, and then another, and another, unfolding all over the floor of the glasshouse, a whole spring coming up out of the dark in a minute, tender and green and ridiculous, at the top of a ruined hill, on the one day in twenty years when the sun had gone out.

Under his hands, the door shut.

He felt it shut. He felt the silver stop singing, all at once, as a kettle stops when it is lifted off the fire, and the wound close over it from the deepest layer upward, the way he had been taught, and seal at the top in a seam of gold. And under the seam, under the cold hand and his own warm one, under the ribs, a long way down in the dark where there had been nothing, something moved.

He did not notice. He was counting.

“It’s warm,” said the boy.

Caleb opened his eyes. The boy was looking at him. His face was still white, but the gold of the air lay over it now, and over his black hair, and in the black of his eyes, and he was looking at Caleb as if Caleb had climbed up into the sky and taken down the sun and brought it to him in his hands.

“That’s me,” said Caleb.

He took his hands away. They were shaking, and so was the rest of him, and the boy shook too, all over, in the black wet coat, with his teeth knocking. Caleb considered him. Then he undid the clasp at his throat and took off his cloak.

It was grey, and it was very fine, and it had silver thread run all through it, so that it shone a little in any light at all, and at the collar, in gold, the sun of his house was worked with all its rays. He put it round the boy’s shoulders. It was too small. It went round all the same, if he pulled, and he pulled, and tucked it in under the boy’s chin with great care, the way his nurse tucked him in, all round, so that no cold could get in at any edge.

“You’ll be cold.”

“I’m never cold,” said Caleb, who was, now, very.

The boy’s hand came up and closed on the cloak at his throat, and held it.

Then, a long way off, down the hill, in the mist, someone called his name.

“*Caleb!*”

It was Matilda. He knew it at once. It was the voice of the practice yard, the one that said *Again*, only it was not saying *again* now, it was saying his name, and it was high and cracked and frightened in a way he had never heard it, and other voices were calling with hers, a great many of them, nearer.

“That’s me,” said Caleb, scrambling up. “I’ve got to go. They’ll be cross. Matilda’s got a sword.”

The boy’s other hand caught at his sleeve, weakly, and held a little, and let go.

“Stay.”

Caleb looked at him, lying in the roses in the gold, in a grey cloak that would not meet across his chest. He wanted to stay. He wanted it so much that he could feel it in his chest like a stitch from running.

Then he heard the other footsteps. They were coming up the hill on the other side of the glasshouse, from the Vesperian side, fast and light and stumbling, a woman’s, running.

“Somebody’s coming for you. Listen. That’s yours.”

And he ran.

He ran out through the door and through the briars, which tore at him, and at the edge of the briars he stopped and looked back, because he had to know. Through the gold air and the broken panes he saw a woman come in at the far side of the glasshouse, a tall woman in black with her skirts held up in both her fists, and he saw her see the boy, and he saw her go down on her knees in the roses beside him as if her legs had been cut from under her. Then the voices called his name again, closer, and Matilda’s was the loudest, and he turned and ran down the hill towards them with his elbows out.

He was halfway down the white streets when the cold came up and took him.

It came up from his feet, as water comes up into a boat. It came up through his legs and his body and into his arms and his hands, which had been warm all his life and were not warm now, and into his heart, which stumbled, and into his head. The white street tipped up in front of him like a plate being lifted to be dried. He saw the stone lady in the fountain, the one without a face, go by on her side. He thought, quite clearly, as he had thought about the boy, that this was a great deal of trouble to be in over a cloak.

After that there was nothing, for a long time.

And after that there was the yacht.

\* \* \*

He came back to *Kolyska* with his hands still flat on the wound and his face wet.

It had taken no time at all. The topmost gold line still lay a hair’s breadth short of the heart. Twenty years had gone through him between two beats of that slow heart under his hands, and it had come out the other side the same heart, beating, and he knew whose it was, and there was no time to know it.

The sliver was still burning in its cellar.

He went down past the old gold seam, under the last of the ribs, into the dark where the sun lay on its side in Arian, and put his light round it. It had been refined past all reason and broken off short and left to itself, and it was coming apart in every direction at once, as a lump of sugar comes apart in hot tea, and every grain that came away was a grain of noon. And it was singing, faint and high and sweet, the same note that had come down the Fault before the mountain fell. It had never stopped. It had only gone inside.

It did not know him as the threads had. He called it and it would not come, and he called again, and the old thing in the deep of him that had lent him its shoulder in a glasshouse lifted its head and called with him.

The sliver turned.

He wound his own light round it as one wraps a coal from the hearth in a wet cloth to carry it across a room, layer on layer, until the singing came muffled through the wrapping. Then he drew it up by the way it had come in, slantwise under the ribs, a hair at a time, the way the physicians of the Vales draw an arrowhead that has gone in at a bad angle, never pulling, only persuading.

It did not come cleanly. It had cooked the track it lay in, and in places the flesh had fused to it as dough fuses to a hot stone, and at every hair’s breadth something parted with a sound Caleb felt in his own teeth. Black blood welled up round his fingers from the keyhole of the wound and ran down over the ribs and steamed on the fleece. Arian did not cry out. His back came up once off the rock, and his hand closed in the fleece beside him until the old twine of it parted with a crack, and his face, turned up into the gold, had no more in it than a face cut on a tomb.

It came out into his hand, a needle of light from wrist to fingertip, white at the core and gold along its edges, so bright that the chamber went from gold to white and every shadow on the walls went flat. It was hot as noon on a stone terrace of the Citadel in high summer, when there is no shade to stand in anywhere, and it burned even him. He smelled his own palm scorch, faintly, as a feather scorches that has drifted too near a lamp, and the gold in his own skin leapt up at it and wanted it, and he could not keep it.

He turned on his knees and laid it against the vein of old glass in the wall.

The glass took it as a lamp wick takes oil. The brown glass lit, all at once, from the floor up into the roof and away up through the rock, a long gold thread of fire climbing into the mountain, and from somewhere far above them in the stone, faint and sweet and thinning, he heard the last of the singing go up and up, and stop.

The vein glowed on above them, like a seam of honey in a broken comb.

The keyhole of the wound had gone dark. Caleb closed it, because there was light still in his hands and he had never yet left a wound open that he could shut, and under his palms the warm wrong flush went out of the white skin from the edges in, the way the red goes out of a horseshoe laid to cool on the anvil. Behind it the split and yellowed skin along the gold lines closed and went white again, line by line, as a pond skins over where a stone went through. The light did not stop at the wound. It went on of itself to the raw places the blades had left along his cheekbones and the backs of his hands, and smoothed those too, before Caleb had asked it to.

On Arian’s lashes, as he watched, a single grain of frost came out, and then another, white and fine as salt drying on a stone.

Arian breathed, and his breath did not show.

Caleb had never been so glad of a cold thing, and he laughed, or tried to.

Then he went to take his hands away, and his hands would not come.

The light was still going out of them in a steady stream, into the closed wound and the healed ribs and the dark house beyond, and there was nothing more in there for it to mend, and it went on going.

He called it back.

It did not come.

He had nothing left to make heat out of, and so the light had begun to take what it could find. It took the warmth out of his hands, and then out of his arms and his shoulders and his chest, and behind it the cold rose in him from his feet. He watched his own fingers on Arian’s ribs go from white to grey, and the nails go blue at their beds, as the hands of the drowned go when the river in the Fault gives them back in spring.

It was the same cold. He had felt it halfway down a white street with a dead city going by him on its side, and he had forgotten it, and it had not forgotten him.

His heart stumbled. It missed, and caught, and missed. Blood came from his nose as it had come in a glasshouse, and ran warm over his lip, and he could not lift a hand from the wound to wipe it. The gold under his skin went down to silver, and from silver to the flat chalk of the Blight, and the round chamber went down with it to a dim brown glow, like a winter afternoon at four o’clock, and the shadows came off the walls and in.

*Having nothing left to spend, it spends the healer.*

“*Walker!*”

He had never heard that voice raised. It went down the passage through the rock like a great bell struck once in a tower. And he was lifted.

He was held against something cold and steady as a church wall, and the dark went by fast, parting on either side as the sea parts at a ship’s bow, and under his cheek a heart went slow and even, keeping its own time, while his own went missing and catching and missing against it.

He thought, *I know that.* He thought, *one, and two, and three*.

Then there was straw, and the smell of sheep, and Mirek’s voice saying *Highness, Highness* from somewhere far off, and light.

The sun had come down the north wall while he was in the dark, and in at the cave mouth, and it lay across the floor of the first chamber in a long gold bar, straight-edged as a new plank, with the dust going up in it slow and bright.

Arian stopped a hair’s breadth from the gold, as he had stopped at the garden doors. Then there were other arms, big and warm and shaking, and a voice saying *Highness* in the Highland March, and Caleb was given across the line from one pair of arms into the other the way a sleeping child is handed over a stile.

Arian did not let go of his hand.

Paul stepped back into the sun, and Caleb’s hand went with him, and the long white fingers round it came too, into the light as far as the wrist. Through a haze, from a long way off, Caleb saw the light find the backs of those fingers. The white skin reddened, and lifted, and blistered, the blisters rising as fast as bubbles in boiling sugar and bursting and weeping down between the knuckles, and a thin smoke went up off them into the sun, and Arian did not take his hand back. He held on until Paul had carried the whole of Caleb into the sun, to the last finger, and only then did he open his hand and let Caleb’s go, and draw his own back into the dark.

“Stay in the light,” said Arian.

The sun came down on Caleb, low and white and winter-bright, and found his face and his hands and his heart through the silk of his coat, and poured into him. And the Grace, which had been running out of him into the dark like a dog that has slipped its lead in a strange town, heard it, and stopped, and turned its head, and came home.

He felt his heart catch, and hold, and go on, and under his cheek Paul’s heart going like a horse at full gallop, and Paul’s voice over his head saying *Highness, I’ve got you*, as if it were a prayer.

The last thing he saw, before he slept, was the dark of the passage beyond the line of the light, and a pale face in it, watching him lie in the sun.

\* \* \*

He woke on a sheepskin in the cave mouth in the middle of the afternoon, with the sun on his face.

By day the opposite wall of the gorge went up across a mile of air in bands the colour of a loaf’s crust and a chestnut’s shell, and stood in a clear blue shadow, for it faced north and the low winter sun would not look at it. Away along the gorge the sergeant’s men were at the fallen rock with iron bars, so far off that they looked no bigger than rooks.

His hands were cold.

The gold had come back into the rest of him while he slept and lay faint along his wrists. But the warmth that had lain in his palms since he was six, ready for any hurt that came near, was not there. He turned them up to the sun and left them open, and they stayed as they were, like two cups set out in the rain that the rain had not yet found.

Paul sat beside him with his knees drawn up and his big forearms across them, as he had sat outside every door in the east wing. On Caleb’s other side, on straw somebody had carried out into the sun, Mirek was asleep with his splinted leg in its bands of Alorian lace, snoring faintly, as puppies snore.

By the hearth one of the escort was sewing up another’s scalp with a sailmaker’s needle, and the wounded one bit on a hazel stick with the blood dried black down one side of his face and swore round it in the Wildmarch tongue. Caleb, who could have closed that scalp with one hand at any hour before the cave, lay in the sun with nothing in his hands and watched the needle go in.

“You carried me,” murmured Caleb.

Paul’s head turned. Something went across his face, quick and rough, and was put away.

“The Sovereign said to, Highness.”

“And if he had not said to?”

“I’d have carried you anyway.”

“Yes. I know you would.”

They were quiet for a while.

“Paul. You were weeping. In the sun. I heard you.”

Paul’s hands lay open across his knees, and they were not quite steady.

“Those men on the road. What they said. About the Don. And your —” He stopped. His jaw worked. “Highness, I —”

Caleb waited, as he had learned to wait at a sickbed, without moving, so as not to frighten whatever was coming.

It did not come. Paul shut his mouth on it, and Caleb watched him do it, and watched what it cost him.

“When we’re home.”

“You keep saying that,” said Caleb gently. “I am beginning to wonder where you think home is.”

Paul was silent a long time, with his eyes on the blue wall across the gorge.

“So am I, Highness.”

Caleb lay in the sun through the rest of the afternoon and let it fill him, and remembered. The memory lay in him now as plain as a room by daylight. He knew whose lamp burned newest in the lamp room in the west of the fortress, with the stone dust still in the letters of her name, and he thought of a tall woman running with her skirts in her fists, and could not see her face. He thought of a dry fountain under a black sun, and three men with no faces, and the sound a Sovereign of the Umbral houses had made going down, and knew now whose eyes he had seen it with. He thought of *It is warm*, said on a carriage step with the dawn coming, and *it’s warm* in a glasshouse twenty years before it, and his own voice at eight answering, *That’s me*, and he put his cold hands over his face and lay there for some time.

The sun went back up the north wall the way it had come down, until the mouth of *Kolyska* lay in shadow and the gold was only on the rim, and the far wall went from blue to violet. Behind him the lycans had built up the shepherds’ hearth, and the smoke went up past him into the cold, smelling of hazel and sheep and somebody’s idea of supper.

There was a little warmth in his palms. It had come back with the last of the sun, the way the first swallow comes back to an empty barn in spring, and it lay in the hollow of each hand, faint and uncertain, and did not yet know whether to stay.

He got up. His legs took some persuading. Paul gave him his arm as far as the shepherds’ hearth, and there Caleb let go.

“I can manage the rest.”

\* \* \*

The vein of old glass in the back wall was still burning.

It had dimmed since the morning. It lay in the rock above the shepherds’ fleeces like a long gold thread drawn through dark cloth, faint and warm and steady, the only light at the back of *Kolyska* but his own. Arian sat beneath it as he had sat all day, with his long legs out before him and the travelling cloak folded square beside him. The frost had returned. It lay on his shoulders and in his hair and on the backs of his hands, and it did not melt.

His right hand lay on his knee. Across the backs of the fingers, under the frost, the skin was red and blistered from the knuckles to the nails.

Caleb knelt on the fleece in front of him and held out his own hand.

“Give it to me.”

“It is nothing.”

“You said that on the carriage step with the sun coming undone in you. I did not believe you then either.”

Arian gave it to him.

Caleb took it in both of his and laid it in his lap. The blisters had come up in a fine bloom across every finger and broken on most of them, and the frost had settled into the raw places beneath and frozen there in a thin pink crust, and they would have hurt a great deal, on anyone else. He reached down into himself for the Grace, as he had reached ten thousand times without thinking, and found the little warmth in his palms, and it stirred and turned towards the hand, and it would not have closed a scratch. He held the hand anyway, in his two cold palms, as anybody might hold a hand, with no light in it at all.

“I cannot mend it,” he admitted. “Not yet. There is nothing in my hands. I am told it will come back.”

“It will.”

“You sound very sure.”

“I have read the primer.”

Caleb looked up from the hand to the face above it, white and still in the gold of the glass.

“It was you.”

“Yes.”

It was one word, said in the voice that had said *the prince is right* and *no*, flat and clear, like a stone laid on a table.

“In the glasshouse. At Old Meridian, on the day of the Eclipse. You were lying in the roses with your hand in a fist and silver in your side.”

“Yes.”

“I put my cloak round you.”

“Yes.” Arian was silent a moment. “It was too small.”

Caleb made a sound that was meant to be a laugh, and did not come out as one. He put it away and tried again.

“You told me what you were.”

“And you said *so there*.”

“I was eight.”

“I was twelve.”

In the corners of the chamber the shadows stirred, as a curtain stirs when a door has been opened somewhere else in the house, and lay still again.

“I have been considering a reply ever since,” said Arian.

Caleb put his free hand over his eyes.

“You knew me,” he said, from behind it. “In the great hall, on the first night, with the seal. When they brought me in. You knew who I was.”

“Yes.”

“And the garden. The roses. The glass. You built me a glasshouse in the middle of a mountain, and I stood on your mezzanine in my lace and told you it was a hackneyed conceit.”

“You did.”

“Why did you not stop me?”

“I wished to hear what else you would say.”

Caleb took his hand from his eyes.

The light of the glass lay along the edge of Arian’s face and left the rest of it to the dark. He was looking at Caleb as he had across the anvil in the Hall of the Chain, when he had taken such care not to look that Caleb had felt the care like heat, and he had let the care go. He looked the way a man looks at something he has at last been allowed to see, and does not mean to waste a moment of.

“Why did you never tell me?” asked Caleb.

Arian was quiet for a long time.

“You did not remember.”

“That is not a reason.”

“It was the only one I had.”

He looked down at his own hand lying in Caleb’s lap, and for a while that was all. Then he turned the blistered hand over in Caleb’s palms, slowly, so that it lay palm up.

“You would have paid me. I did not want to be paid.”

Against Caleb’s ribs, stitched into the lining of his coat, the Sovereign’s ring lay heavy and cold.

*You have been paid*, he thought. *In seven drafts. You have been paid to the last penny, and the account is closed, and you are never to know it.*

He held the hand, and said nothing at all.

He said instead, when he could, “They called my name. In the mist. Matilda, and a great many others. You heard them.”

“Yes.”

“Then you have known my name since you were twelve years old.”

“Yes.”

He had been *Highness* on the stairs of the east wing and *Your Highness* in the great hall, he had been *the prince* before the Nine and *the sun prince* in the clerks’ whispers and *His Highness* in Salvatore’s mouth, which was worse, and *the hostage* in his own, he had been *malyshka* to a lycan cook and *sun boy* to a blind man with a singing blade, and in nine weeks, across a desk and an anvil and the width of a carriage in the mist of Old Meridian, with his hand over a fist on a ledge of the Fault and his hands on a wound in the dark, the one man in the world who had kept his name the longest without once spending it had never called him by it.

“You have never said it,” said Caleb.

Arian was still a moment.

“Caleb.”

The glass in the wall above them brightened, all along its length, as a coal brightens when someone breathes on it.

Nothing else moved. Only the vein of old glass had gone from honey to gold over their heads, and in Caleb’s two palms, where Arian’s hand lay, the warmth that had come back at sunset stirred, and turned, and rose, until he could feel it lying in each palm like water in a cupped leaf, and it was warm, and it stayed.

He looked down. Faintly, under the frost on the backs of Arian’s fingers, the blisters had begun to go down.

He had filled every silence of his life, and he could find nothing to fill this one, and his eyes were stinging, and he found he did not mind. So he moved, on the shepherds’ fleece, until he was sitting at Arian’s side with his back against the same rock, under the same gold thread of glass, and their shoulders touched. He kept the hand in his lap. After a while he let his head go sideways, as it had in the carriage in the pass, until it rested on a cool shoulder, and this time he did not wait for it to move away.

“I am going to sleep now,” he murmured. “I have been spent, you understand. It is very tiring.”

“Yes,” said Arian.

The frost on his collar was cold against Caleb’s cheek. Under it, under the black silk and the white skin and the healed ribs, he could feel the heart going, slow and steady, unhurried, in a time he almost knew.

\* \* \*

The dark at the back of *Kolyska* was not dark to Arian.

It never had been, anywhere. It had grain, as wood has grain, and he read it as the vault-masons read a slab of stone before they set a chisel to it, every ridge of the rock and every knot of twine on the shepherds’ fleeces, the frost of his own breath settling white on the wool beside his hand. The vein above him had gone back from gold to honey and was going slowly down towards the brown it had been before the prince put a sun into it, and he did not need it. He could have counted the hairs on the prince’s head without it. He had considered it, and decided that it would be ungentlemanly, and that he would in all likelihood lose his place.

He listened instead.

He had listened to hearts all his life. Every child of the Umbral houses did, as every child of the Solar House turned its face to the light, without being taught and without being able to stop. Round the shepherds’ hearth at the cave mouth the lycans of his escort went like the great drums of the hill towns heard from a valley away, and among them went the sergeant’s, older than the rest, with a catch in it like a gate that sticks on its latch, which Arian had never mentioned to him and never would. In the lambing pen the boy’s went quick and light as a linnet’s, fevered a little with the leg, and steady. Vesna’s boy had told the Solar prince to heal his Sovereign before he healed a broken leg, which was either the finest piece of loyalty Arian had met with on the throne or the stupidest. He would double the boy’s wages and leave it to Vesna to say which.

Walker’s heart was wrong.

Walker sat at the edge of the firelight, as near the dark as he could get, with his face to the passage. His heart had been wrong since the night they left the Coast. It went too fast when the prince was kind to him and too slow when the prince was not looking, and on the ledge of the Fault, with the blades lying in the frost, it had stopped for a breath and then come down so hard that Arian had heard it from the carriage door. He did not know what that meant. He meant to find out. On the Coast a heart that went wrong round its master generally meant a purse somewhere, and the Coast had an old answer for it, which was the strait at the turn of the tide, with iron in the man’s pockets so that the tide should not change its mind. Arian had considered it on the ledge in the light of the blades, quite calmly, as a man considers which pen to use. Then the prince had turned on the frost and said *Paul, are you hurt?* and Arian had found that he could see nothing beyond that question but the prince’s face on the morning he was told that Walker had gone home to the Highlands. He had set the thought down unfinished, which he did not do.

He had put the prince into Walker’s arms that morning all the same, because whatever else was wrong with that heart, it would have carried the prince into the sun through a wall of solid rock, and Arian had known it, and had not had the leisure to be nice about it.

Of the Ashward’s rogues he thought once, and only to regret that the dark had been quick with them. It had left none of them able to say who had paid, and he would have liked to ask, slowly, one at a time, in front of the others. The sergeant had known two of the faces, and the road and the hour had been the Ashward’s, and the Nine would want it proved. Arian sat in the dark and considered how little, on the whole, he cared what the Nine would want.

The prince’s heart lay against his arm, through the black silk and the shirt with its hem torn off for a boy’s leg, slow and even and deep in sleep, as it had not gone all day. He had sat in the dark with his hand blistering and listened to it out on the ledge in the sun, under the noise of the river and of a dozen lycans eating whatever the sergeant had chosen to call supper, and he had not lost it once. He had heard it catch, and hold, and go on, in Walker’s arms, in the light, and then he had sat down on the fleeces where the prince had left him and stayed there a long while without moving, because he did not altogether trust what his own face would do if he moved it.

And under all of them, under his own ribs, there was the other.

\* \* \*

It had begun somewhere after seven.

He had been lying in the roses with his life going out of him through the hole the sun-forged silver had made, and a child with mud to his knees had been counting out loud, *one, two, three*, in the even voice of a tutor, lending him a heart because he had none of his own. And somewhere after seven, under the boy’s hands, in a place under Arian’s ribs where nothing had moved in twelve years, something moved.

On eight it moved again. On nine it moved again. By twenty it had found the time the boy was counting in, and it kept to it, and Arian had lain in the warm gold air of the glasshouse with the roses putting out leaves all round him and felt it go, and not known what it was, and known.

He had known that night, lying in his bed in the Vesperian tent with his eyes shut, feeling it in the dark under his own hand while Zio wept in the next room. He knew the beat. He had heard it in every living throat he had ever stood near, and never once felt it in his own. The children of the Umbral houses have no heartbeat, and it is the first thing they are taught about themselves, before their letters, before their own names, and he had lain in the dark at twelve years old, with his father dead since noon and a stranger’s cloak held in his fist, and felt the first thing he had been taught about himself stop being true.

He had not wept for his father. He had lain with his eyes dry and listened to Zio through the silk, and thought, with a clearness that ought to have frightened him, of three hoods with nothing in them, and of what he would do to whatever was inside them when he was grown, and in what order.

He had told no one.

Zia had come in, and sat on the edge of the bed, and put her hand on his chest to feel him breathe, as she had done every night since he could remember. He had felt her hand go still. He had lain with his eyes shut and breathed as a sleeping boy breathes, and she had sat there in the dark with her hand on his heart for a long time, and said nothing, and in the morning she had said nothing either. Nor had he.

They had kept it for nine years. They had kept it from the Council and from the physicians and from Zio, and from each other, and they had sat across from one another at supper every night of those nine years with the same secret laid on the table between them like a salt cellar that neither of them would pass. On the eve of the day he took the throne she had come to his rooms when the house was asleep, and laid her hand on his chest, and said, *I have known since Old Meridian, tesoro*, and he had said, *So have I*, and she had laughed until she wept, and then she had made him eat something.

A Sovereign of the Umbral houses with a heart in him was a Sovereign the Nine might ask the shadows about. Arian had known that at twelve, and he knew it better now.

It was why the prince’s blood had not killed him. Zio had said in the black gallery that it should have, and Zio had been right. It should have burned him from the inside out. But there had been a little of that light in him already, for twenty years, keeping time, and when the prince’s light came into him through the bite it had come into a house where it was known, and it had burned him, a great deal, and let him live. Zio had worked out that much, Arian thought. He had sat across the room from Arian’s bed for ten days while he could not keep blood-wine down, and looked at him, and never once asked.

He had been born by taking. He had drawn the life out of the one who carried him before ever he had a mouth to drink with, month by month, so that she grew thinner and colder about him as he grew, as a candle burns down round its own wick, and on the night he came into the world she had bled out of it without once looking at him. He had known that about himself for as long as he had known anything. He had known what he was. He was the one who takes.

And a boy of eight with mud on his knees had knelt down beside him in the dark and given, and gone on giving, until there was nothing left in him to give, and the one who takes had a heart in him.

\* \* \*

He had felt the prince remember.

He had felt it that morning through the prince’s hands, as the light went through the rooms of him with its lamp. He had kept every door in that house shut for twenty years, and one room at the end of it locked, and gone into that room alone, and come out again, and locked it behind him.

The prince had walked into that room as if it were his. Which it was.

He had felt the glasshouse come back into the prince, and for twenty years in a heartbeat he had not been able to tell which of them was remembering it.

And this evening the prince had come into the dark with his hands empty and said, *It was you.*

Arian turned his head a little, so that his cheek lay against the prince’s hair.

He had heard the name called in the mist, in the voice of a girl with a sword, high and cracked and frightened, and he had lain in the roses in a cloak that was too small for him and said it over without a sound, so as to keep it. For twenty years he had read it in the *Lantern*, in other people’s ink, under headlines that grew less respectable every season, and listened to it spoken across the Council table by men who did not deserve to have it in their mouths. Zia had said it to him once, in the last year of her life, gently, to see what his face would do. He had kept it as the Holds keep a pledge, in the dark, at interest, never once spent, and carried it across the great hall of Castel Nerezza on the night the prince came, and kept it behind his teeth while the prince called him *Don Conti* and told him how a seal was used.

Tonight he had spent it.

It had been worth every grain of the twenty years. He would have said it again, if the prince had been awake, and he doubted he would ever be able to say it without the glass brightening.

\* \* \*

The other word he had said only once, and that once a great many times over.

He had not known he would. He had come down the black gallery with the prince’s light blazing off the walls and the prince’s heart missing and catching and missing in his ears, louder than anything he had ever heard, and he had put his teeth in the prince’s throat because there was no other way and no time to find one, and the light had come into him and begun to burn. It had gone down his throat like lamp oil with the wick already lit, and the hunger had come up behind the burning.

He had never fed from a living throat. Nobody he knew had. It was done only between two who had chosen one another, never between strangers, or in anger, or for hunger, and nobody had chosen him. He had pinned the prince to a floor and been wept at for it, and the hunger did not care. It had come up out of the bottom of him like a flood through a cellar, all at once, and it wanted the whole of the prince, every drop, the gold and the blood and the heart that was stumbling against his mouth. And the light wanted the whole of Arian. And somewhere at the end of the gallery, waiting, something stood with its hands folded and wanted whichever of them went first.

He had one word left that was not his teeth, and he had said it, against the prince’s skin, over and over, as a man sets his shoulder to a door with the storm on the other side of it.

*Mio.*

He had said it to the hunger. *Mine. Not yours. You may not have him. He is mine, and what is mine I keep.* He had said it to the light, which might burn him to the ground if it liked so long as it left the prince standing, and to whatever waited at the end of the gallery with its hands folded, *mine, you shall not have him either*, over and over, until the prince’s heart caught and held under his mouth. Then he had taken his teeth out of the prince’s throat by main force, an inch at a time, and thought that nothing would ever cost him so much again, until this morning, when he had opened his hand in the light at the cave mouth and let the prince’s go.

He had not known if the prince heard it. He knew now that the prince had heard it and not understood it, for he had felt the not-understanding come back to him through the bite, like a question asked in another room.

He was glad of that. It was not a word a man ought to say to a prince of the Solar House on the floor of a corridor. He had not apologised for the floor. He would do it again.

The prince stirred against his shoulder.

He did not wake. He turned his face a little further into the black silk of Arian’s collar, frowning, as if the frost there were an argument he meant to win in the morning, and his hand, which had been lying in his lap over Arian’s, slid up and came to rest flat against the breast of his own coat, over his heart. He had slept like that in the carriage, Arian had noticed, all the way across the Marches, with his hand on his heart as if he were keeping a place there and did not want to lose it.

The shadows of the chamber had come in from the walls while he slept, as they always came to the prince, as cats come to the one warm stone in a yard. They lay over his breast and his folded hand, thick and quiet, and when Arian looked at them they did not move. He had never once been able to make them do anything they did not wish to where the prince was concerned. He let them lie.

His own thumb had gone to the third finger of his right hand, and turned at nothing. In Dornhöhle, behind the door with seven iron bars, the ring that had passed from Sovereign to Sovereign for a thousand years lay on grey velvet in the cold-iron dark, and at the Long Eclipse it would go to whoever bid highest under the law of the Holds. He had known what the Nine would make of a Sovereign whose seals could be contested, and of one with a heart in him, and of one who was both. He had pledged it all the same, so that Salvatore Caruso should not burn the Coast’s debts at a wedding feast and call it a dowry, and he had never been so sure of anything he had sealed in eleven years as he was of that, sitting in a shepherds’ cave with no seal at all.

The prince’s travelling cloak lay folded square beside him, ready to give back. Zia had folded the other one, twenty years ago, and put it away somewhere safe, and it had come to him in the end, and he had not given that one back either.

Twelve suns had been lit in the Fault before dawn, and they would have been seen from every high pass in the Marches, where the Alorians kept a great many eyes. That was for the morning.

The frost on him was his own again, down to the backs of his healing fingers. He had had the sun inside him for the better part of a night and a morning, and he did not, on the whole, think a great deal of it. It had been warm, which he had not expected, and then it had tried to kill him, which he had. He could not see what the elves wrote all their poetry about.

The warm thing had not been the sun.

The prince had promised him on the Fault road to find him something that was not a fire, and asked until the Coast to find it in. Arian sat in the dark with the prince’s head on his shoulder, and the prince’s breath on his collar, and the prince’s hair against his cheek smelling of woodsmoke and sheep and, under those, of what it had always smelled of, warm glass and wet earth after rain, the smell of a glasshouse on the day the sun went out. He had built a garden in the throat of a volcano so that it would smell like that. He had never told anyone so, and he never would.

The prince had found it a long way short of the Coast. He had found it at eight years old, and given it away in a glasshouse, and had not known.

Under Arian’s ribs his heart went on in the dark, faint and slow and warm, in the time a child had counted out for it among the roses, one, and two, and three. It had kept that time for twenty years without anyone to keep it for. It was keeping it now. The prince’s heart beat against his arm, slow with sleep, and his own went with it, beat for beat, as it always had, so near that a physician with two fingers on Arian’s wrist might take it for the beat of his own blood coming back to him, and be right, and never know it.

He did not say the word. There was no storm at the door tonight, and nothing to set his shoulder against, and the prince was asleep.

His heart said it for him.
