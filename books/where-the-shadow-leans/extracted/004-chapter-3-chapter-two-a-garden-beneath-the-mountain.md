## Chapter 3: Chapter Two - A Garden Beneath the Mountain

*The afflicted will sleep long and wake unrested. The cold settles in the hands first, then in the heart, which forgets its measure. Of those taken beyond the reach of the sun, none has been known to live past the tenth week.*

— *Of the Solar Blight*, from the physicians’ primer of the Harp Vales

\* \* \*

On the first day, Caleb asked for books.

He did it chiefly to find out what would happen. He wrote the request out in his most exquisite hand on a sheet of the heavy black paper he found in the escritoire and left it on the silver tray beside the untouched remains of his supper, fully expecting it to be ignored, or mocked, or burned. By the following evening there were forty-three volumes stacked on the table by the window. There was Alorian poetry and Vesperian history, a treatise on dwarven bond-law, three novels of such spectacular vulgarity that he read two of them in one sitting, and a slim, water-stained primer on the healing arts that came unasked and that no one would explain.

On the fourth day, as an experiment, he asked for a harpsichord.

It arrived on the sixth, carried up the stair by four grunting lycans. It was lacquered black and gold, old and fine and slightly out of tune, and he spent a contented afternoon tuning it himself.

On the ninth day he asked for Alorian white tea, which was not exported, and it came on the tenth. On the eleventh, out of pure mischief, he asked for every fae broadsheet printed in the past month, and those came too, the pages still smelling of the Marches. His own face stared up from a good third of them beneath headlines of mounting hysteria. *THE PILGRIM PRINCE: WHERE IS CALEB?* *SOLAR HOUSE SILENT ON YOUNGEST SON.* And on the most recent, in a crimson ink that fairly shrieked off the page, *TRAITOR PRINCE? Palace Sources Hint at Darker Truth.*

He read that one three times. Then he fed it, neatly, to the candle.

*Ask for whatever you require*, the Sovereign had said, and he had meant it with a thoroughness Caleb found more unsettling than cruelty. Cruelty he would have understood. One could brace against it, and quip at it, and endure. Here every door in the east wing stood unlocked save the one that led out of it, every request was granted save the one he did not trouble to make, and the man who had melted his seal on the floor had not come to see him once in three weeks.

Caleb told himself he was relieved.

\* \* \*

The food came three times a day, and it came with Vesna.

She was a lycan woman of perhaps fifty, broad and brown-armed and smelling of woodsmoke and bread, with a voice that could have called a pack home across a valley. She had appeared on his first morning with a tray of Alorian scones and set it down with the air of someone presenting evidence at a trial.

They were a disaster. They were dense as masonry, scorched on one side and raw on the other, and whoever had written the recipe out for her had plainly never met a scone in his life. Caleb ate every one of them, with butter and a great deal of praise, while Vesna stood over him with her arms folded and watched him do it. On the second morning the scones were merely bad. By the second week they were all but good, and Vesna had begun to call him *malyshka*, which he gathered meant something like *little one*, and which no one had called him since he was six.

He asked her how a lycan came to be cooking in a vampire’s fortress. She said the Sovereign had asked her, and that was all she would say, and she said it with a look that closed the subject like a door.

Paul was a different matter. Paul slept across the threshold of Caleb’s bedchamber every night, as he always had, and in three weeks he had said perhaps forty words. Caleb tried everything. He asked after Paul’s mother and sisters in the Highland March, and offered him the vulgar novels. He played the harpsichord badly on purpose, which had always made Paul laugh. Paul thanked him, and looked resolutely at the floor, and went back to the door.

Caleb put it down to shame. It must be hard, for a lycan whose family had kept faith with the Solar House for six generations, to watch the Solar House hand its youngest son to Vesperia. He resolved to be kinder to him, and was.

\* \* \*

The chambers themselves were a study in magnificent melancholy. Tall windows of smoked glass looked out over the jagged black valleys of the Coast, where the twilight never deepened into night or lightened into day but only hung there, violet and charcoal, like a held breath. Hangings of velvet the colour of a bruised plum swallowed what little the candles gave. The bed was a vast, brooding thing of carved ebony, its posts twined with nightshade and serpents, and on the first night Caleb had lain in it and thought, with great clarity, that it resembled nothing so much as a comfortable sarcophagus.

By the third week he no longer thought anything when he lay down in it. He simply slept, and that was how he knew that something was wrong.

He had been trained, as all the children of the Solar House were trained, in the healing arts. Star-Grace was the gift of their blood, and the temple physicians of the Harp Vales had spent a decade of his childhood drilling him in its uses, in the counting of pulses and the reading of breath and the patient, unsentimental study of a body’s betrayals. So when, in the second week, the shimmer on his skin faded from gold to silver and from silver to a flat, chalky pallor, Caleb turned that study on himself. He marked the chill that had settled in his fingertips and stayed there however close he held them to the fire. He counted the fourteen hours of sleep that rested him not at all, and felt the dull weight gather in the tips of his ears. And he listened, at the end of each day, to his heart beginning to stumble over its own rhythm like a dancer who has been too long on the floor.

The primer called it the Solar Blight, and it was plain about its course. Alorians taken beyond the reach of the sun, it observed with scholarly serenity, survived between six and ten weeks.

He found the passage in the water-stained primer that no one would explain, on the evening of the nineteenth day. He closed the book and set it down on the table with great care. Then he went and sat at the harpsichord for a long while, and did not play.

On the twenty-first day, he dressed.

It took more effort than it once had, and he had begun to allow an hour for it. But he dressed with scrupulous care all the same, in a coat of dove-grey silk with silver frogging, a waistcoat embroidered with white stars, and cuffs of Alorian lace so fine they might have been spun by spiders with ambition. There was no one to dress for. He dressed because a prince of the Solar House did not greet his own death in a dressing gown, and because every morning he managed it was a morning on which he had not yet begun to die in earnest.

He was sitting on the chaise by the window, smoothing the lace at his wrists, when the door opened.

\* \* \*

Arian Conti stood on the threshold.

He wore a high-collared coat of black silk that drank the candlelight instead of returning it, and the three heavy rings, and the stillness Caleb remembered from the hall. He offered no greeting. His gaze moved over the room, over the stacked books and the harpsichord and the curl of ash in the candle dish, and came to rest on Caleb.

Caleb saw Arian take in the pallor and the quiet hands and the deliberate uprightness of a spine held straight by will alone, and saw those dark eyes go, as they had in the hall, to the hollow of his own throat.

Whatever Arian heard there, none of it reached his face.

“You look terrible.”

He said it so flatly it might have been a remark about the weather, and to his own surprise Caleb was delighted. It was the first honest thing anyone had said to him since he came. Everyone else had given him *yes, Your Highness* and *at once, Your Highness*, and Vesna had given him scones and *malyshka*, and on one memorable occasion a young lycan footman had given him *please don’t tell the Sovereign I dropped the tea tray, Your Highness.*

He lifted his chin, though his neck protested, and gave the Sovereign of Vesperia a smile of flawless, frosted courtesy.

“And you, Don Conti, look precisely as you did three weeks ago, which I suppose is one of the privileges of your kind. I had begun to wonder whether you remembered you kept a hostage at all, or whether you had simply misplaced me, the way one misplaces an umbrella once the rain has stopped.”

Arian let the barb pass. He came into the room, and the candle on the table bent sideways as he passed, and the air turned cold enough that Caleb could have sworn he saw his own breath.

“You need light. Alorians rot in the dark.”

“What a charmingly unvarnished assessment of my biology.” Caleb rested his chin on his knuckles with every appearance of languid ease. The truth was that they were all that held his head up. “I am obliged to you for the diagnosis, Sovereign, though I made it myself some days ago. If you meant to dispose of the youngest son of Aloria by slow and tasteful attrition, you have my compliments. It is exquisitely done, and far more subtle than I should ever have suspected of you.”

Something moved in the dark of Arian’s eyes, like deep water troubled from beneath, and Caleb could not tell whether it was anger.

“Come with me.”

He turned for the door without waiting to see whether he would be obeyed. Caleb regarded his back for a long moment. Then, because the alternative was to stay on the chaise and go on quietly dying in a very good waistcoat, he rose.

The room tilted. He put a hand to the back of the chaise, casually, as if he had simply wanted to touch the velvet. When the floor agreed to lie flat again, he followed. Paul rose from his post by the door and fell in behind them both.

\* \* \*

They descended.

Caleb had assumed, insofar as he had assumed anything, that the Sovereign meant to take him somewhere high, a tower or a balcony, where the twilight might be less oppressive. Instead Arian led him along the long black corridor of the east wing and down a stair so broad and shallow it might have been cut for giants. They went on through galleries of volcanic glass where the violet lamps grew fewer and the air grew warmer, carrying the faint mineral breath of the mountain’s heart. Lycan sentries straightened as they approached, and Caleb saw their eyes go past their Sovereign to the black iron ring on his own left hand. Two vampire clerks fell silent as the Sovereign passed and began whispering fiercely the moment they thought themselves out of earshot.

Caleb’s ears were Alorian, and sharp. He caught *export* and *third consecutive*, and then *the old Conti ring, on the sun prince*, and then, distinctly, *he has lost his mind*.

At the foot of the last stair, Enzo was waiting.

He stood beside a lectern of black stone, where the last of the violet lamps burned low over an open ledger, and he was angry. It was a vampire’s anger, cold and contained, and it showed only in the bloodless line of his mouth. His gaze went to Caleb, to the chalky skin and the careful walk, and for a moment his composure faltered, as it had in the hall. Then he turned to Arian, and Caleb read him easily, for he had grown up at court and knew what a loyal man looked like at the far end of his patience.

“Sovereign. The Durnhollow debt falls due at the new moon, and our paper is changing hands —”

“Settle it.”

“With *what*, Sovereign? The Alorian ships sailed at half-weight. The third month running. The Council is asking —”

“Then let the Council ask.”

Arian did not break stride. Caleb glanced down as he followed him past the lectern, and in the half second the ledger lay in his sight he saw a column of figures in a neat, old-fashioned hand. Beside the most recent, underscored twice in red, was a single word in the Vesperian script.

*Withheld.*

Caleb kept the word, and walked on.

\* \* \*

At the end of the corridor, the mountain itself stood in the way.

It was a sheer face of raw volcanic stone, and set into it was a pair of doors so enormous that for a moment Caleb’s tired mind would not accept them as doors. They were dark iron, bound and riveted and cut from lintel to threshold with the square dwarven runes he had come to know from the ring on his finger. There was no handle and no lock. At the centre of the ironwork, at the height of a man’s hand, was a round hollow.

Arian stopped before it and turned, and his glance went from the hollow in the iron to the ring.

Caleb looked down at his hand. The black iron had sat there since the first night, snug and warm and immovable. He had tried to pull it off on the second night in a temper, and it would not come. He had assumed with some bitterness that this was the point of a collar.

He stepped forward and pressed the ring into the hollow.

The runes woke from the centre outward, each one kindling forge red and then gold, until the whole face of the doors was written in light. Somewhere inside the iron, vast and patient mechanisms turned over with a sound like a mountain clearing its throat. The doors parted and swung inward, and a wave of warmth rolled out over him, so sudden and so golden that Caleb put a hand to the stone to steady himself, and nearly wept.

He stepped through. Behind him, Paul took up his post beside the doors, with his back to the iron.

He was on a mezzanine of wrought iron, high above a cavern that must once have been the throat of the volcano. Its walls rose sheer and black on every side and fell away beneath him further than he could see. Far above, capping the old vent, was a dome of iron ribs and glass. It was dwarven work, every pane clear and whole, and on the nearest ribs the masons’ chalk marks had not yet been wiped away. Through it the Coast’s eternal twilight showed as a pale disc, like the moon seen from the bottom of a well.

*Tell them I require glass.*

The twilight did not matter. It could not reach him here. Hung from the black vaulting on silver chains, in a great descending spiral that followed the curve of the walls, were twelve Aether-crystals, each taller than a man, each blazing with a clean and roaring gold he had not seen since he left the Citadel.

It was light. It was *sunlight*, and it poured over him and through him and into him.

It struck his skin like warm rain after drought and sank beneath, into the grey, depleted places where the Star-Grace had been dying by inches. He felt it answer. It stirred and warmed and unfolded, like a wilted bloom set back in water. Beneath it, deeper in the blood, an older thing woke a little, as a sleeper turns towards a voice he knows. Every Alorian child was taught that the crystals were the hearts of the old dragons, turned to stone in the southern fires. Caleb had never believed it in any way that mattered. He looked down at his hands on the iron rail and watched a faint gold shimmer creep back into his fingertips, delicate as pollen, and for the first time he wondered.

And below him, where the light fell thickest, there was a garden.

It went down the cavern in terraces that followed the spiral of the crystals, level upon level of dark volcanic soil held back by walls of fitted stone. White roses spilled down the terraces in cascades. These were true Alorian roses, white as a vow, their petals translucent at the edges where the light came through, and a world away from the black, waxen, scentless roses of the Vesperian corridors. There were lemon trees in stone urns, and starflower and sunwort and silverleaf, and a long bed of the little golden lilies that grew wild along the Citadel’s lower terraces and that nobody in Aloria thought beautiful, because they were everywhere. Somewhere out of sight, water was running.

Then the scent reached him on the warm air, roses and damp black earth and green growing things, and under all of it the smell of warm glass.

Caleb went still at the rail.

For one vertiginous moment the mezzanine was gone, and the cavern with it. There was only that smell of warm glass and wet earth, and beneath it a sharp, cold, metallic note that belonged in no garden. Broken panes glittered in half-light. He was kneeling, and the soil was soaking cold through the knees of his breeches, and there was someone’s hand under his own. He was very young and very frightened, and he was not running away.

He reached for the memory, and it went like breath off a mirror. It left behind only an ache under his breastbone that he could not explain, and the garden, and the light.

He was gripping the rail with both hands. He loosened his fingers one at a time and told himself it was the Blight. Three weeks in the dark would addle anyone, and a mind starved of light would reach for phantoms. He only half believed himself, but he let it go all the same, because he was standing in the light and had no strength to spare for ghosts.

\* \* \*

The light was healing him. He could feel his mind clearing, the grey fog of the past weeks lifting from it, and as it lifted, twenty-eight years of court training came back to him. He straightened his cuffs, and began to count.

Twelve crystals. He had grown up beneath Aether-crystals. The spires of the Citadel were crowned with them, and one of the tedious duties of his adolescence had been to walk the annual inspection of the grid at his father’s side. He knew the grades. He could tell the cloudy second-yield stones that lit the lower city from the flawless deep-trench crystals that burned at the Citadel’s heart, and every one of these was deep-trench. This was the finest grade in existence. It came up from the deepest Vesperian trenches so rarely that each stone was counted one by one, and the Solar Throne paid for it in quantities of gold that the Treasury only ever mentioned in whispers.

Twelve of them, here, in a garden.

*Withheld*, the ledger had said, underscored twice in red. *The Alorian ships sailed at half-weight. The third month running.*

He thought of Enzo’s bloodless mouth and the whispering clerks. Every student of statecraft in the Dominion knew that the crystal trade was the single thread by which Vesperia held Aloria’s attention, the only leverage an outcast realm of shadow had over a kingdom of light that would otherwise have gone on pretending it did not exist. Every crystal kept back from those ships was one the Solar Throne would miss. The Sovereign had taken twelve of his best cards out of his own hand and hung them over a garden, where they could buy him nothing.

Twelve of them, over three months.

Caleb turned.

Arian had not come out onto the mezzanine. He stood in the doorway, just beyond the reach of the light, tall and dark and framed in iron and black stone, looking out at the garden with a face as still as a king carved on his own tomb.

“These are deep-trench yield.” Caleb’s voice sounded strange to him. It had lost its fine, cool edge, and he could not find it again. “The finest grade there is. My father pays for these by weight of gold, Sovereign, and I imagine your Council has been in a state of collective apoplexy over the ledgers.”

He stopped there, because the sum had finished itself in his head, and it was not a sum a hostage should be heard working out. Three months. The Sovereign had been keeping the crystals back since before the vineyard burned, before Caleb was ever taken.

It could not be right. The glass was new, and he had heard it ordered on the night he came, but the crystals had been kept back long before that, when there had been no hostage and no blood debt, and no reason on earth for the Sovereign of Vesperia to begin hoarding sunlight in the belly of a mountain. Caleb told himself that Arian might have kept them back for a hundred reasons and simply found a use for them when a dying elf was delivered to his door. That was all it was.

“Why?” he asked instead. “Why have you done this?”

Arian did not answer at once. The golden light lay across the iron threshold at his feet like water on a shore, and he stood just beyond it.

“A dead hostage,” he said at last, “is of no use to me.”

He said it without inflection. Caleb had been raised among diplomats, and he had never heard a less convincing sentence spoken aloud. He opened his mouth to say so, and could not think how.

“The ring will open these doors at any hour,” Arian went on, still to the garden. “No one else’s will. The garden is yours.”

“Mine,” Caleb repeated, with some disbelief.

“Yours,” said Arian.

Caleb looked at him. He looked at the tall figure in the doorway who had melted a seal and then handed him the only key to the only sunlight on the Obsidian Coast, and who stood at the very edge of the light and came not one inch further.

And he understood that Arian could not come in.

Vampires burned in sunlight, and this was deep-trench Aether, the purest solar light that could be quarried from the earth, the same light that crowned the Citadel. To a creature of the Umbral bloodlines this cavern was a furnace. Arian had commissioned a garden and filled it with roses and lit it with a fortune, and he could not set one foot on its paths without burning.

As Caleb watched, the Sovereign put out his right hand to the doorframe, and the light caught the backs of his fingers where they crossed the threshold. It lasted only a moment, but that was long enough for Caleb to see the skin redden and blister, faintly, like paper held too near a candle.

Arian drew his hand back, unhurried, and let the shadow of the corridor fold over it.

For a while Caleb could not speak. His throat had closed, and he had no name for what had closed it.

“A glasshouse, Sovereign.” His voice was nearly steady. “Rather a hackneyed conceit, I’m afraid. Still, I suppose I am obliged to thank you for postponing my expiry, since I was raised, whatever my family may say of me now, to be a courteous guest.”

“You are not obliged.”

Arian turned to go. On the threshold he paused, and for the first time since the doors had opened he looked directly at Caleb, at his face and at the gold returning, slowly, to his skin.

“Stay in the light.”

Then he was gone, and the great doors closed behind him with a sound like a sigh, and Caleb was alone.

He stood on the mezzanine for a long time. Then he went down the iron stairs to the first terrace and walked out among the white roses and turned his face up to the blazing crystals, as the roses did. The light fell warm and golden on his face, and it had cost more than he could bear to think about.

So he did not think about it, not tonight. The ledger and the three months could wait, and so could the redness blooming on the backs of a vampire’s fingers, and warm glass, and wet earth, and a cool hand under his own. He lay down on the warm black earth between the rose beds, under a sun that was not a sun, and slept as he had not slept since the Citadel, deeply and without dreams.
