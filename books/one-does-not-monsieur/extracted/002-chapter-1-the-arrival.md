## Chapter 1: The Arrival

\*   \*   \*

Cecily de Blanchemer was eleven minutes older than her brother, and she had not let one day of the seventeen years since go by without finding some use for them.

“You will take a chill,” she told him, somewhere over the Scottish border, when he unlatched the carriage window for the fourth time and put his head out into the night.

“Scotland first,” Caleb returned. “Afterwards I shall come in and warm myself at your disapproval, which has never yet failed me on a cold night.”

The wind took the words out of his mouth and flung them back along the flank of the carriage and down into the dark.

He did not mind. The carriage was the size of a house and the soft blue of a duck’s egg, and twelve winged palominos drew it, each as big as a carthorse and half as big again, with wings that rose and fell over the moors with a sound like sails filling in a harbour. Below them the hills lay silver with frost. A river ran between them like a dropped ribbon, and here and there on the black slopes a scatter of sheep lay pale as pearls off a broken string.

He had been told that Scotland was grey, and wet, and full of people who would not trouble to be pleasant about either, and he had looked forward to it for a month. Nobody had told him that it would be silver. He leaned out a little further, so that the cold took him by the ears, and watched a lake slide beneath the wheels with the moon lying in it whole, and thought that if the rest of the country was half so well arranged he would have to write and apologise to it.

Behind him the carriage was warm and full of the Beauxbatons seniors in pale blue silk under every shawl they owned, because the Headmistress had told them Scotland would be cold and none of them had believed her. Four boys by the stove were playing Exploding Snap. They had asked Caleb twice since Carlisle to make a fifth, but had been refused so charmingly both times that they were content to play with just the four of them.

Cecily caught him by the back of the collar and pulled him back in.

“*Tiens*, Andrew has written again,” he announced, allowing himself to be pulled and settling beside her on the silk bench.

“He wrote on Tuesday.”

“That was the Tuesday letter. This is the Thursday letter.” He drew it from inside his coat. It ran to three sheets in their eldest brother’s hand, which was elegant on the first page and less so on every page after, as it always was when he had a great deal to say, and to Caleb he always had. It smelled of Ministry ink, which smelled of nothing so much as a committee. “‘My dear Caleb and Cecily, you may be assured that the Tournament has been made perfectly safe. I have seen to it personally. Matilda will be at the castle with the security of the French delegation, and you will both have the goodness to do as she tells you. Caleb will wear his flannel.’” He lowered the page. “Why only Caleb, I wonder?”

“Because I have no need to be told.”

“Nor have I. I simply do not listen, which is far more restful for everyone.” He turned to the second sheet, where the hand had begun to run downhill. “There is a great deal more. ‘The castle is old and its staircases are not to be trusted. The lake is deep. The Forest is forbidden, and I have had it confirmed in writing that it is forbidden to the guests as well. Caleb is not to feed anything.’”

“He knows you.”

“He knows a boy of eight and a swan at Chantilly, which is not at all the same. The swan was very rude to me first.” Caleb folded the letter in three, with great care, and put it back over his heart, where it crackled. “I shall answer him from Hogwarts. I shall tell him that I am wearing the flannel and have fed nothing, and that the staircases have behaved beautifully, and he will believe the first and know better than the other two, and we shall both be content.”

Cecily laid her hand on the pocket of her robe, where a square of parchment had lain folded since August, written in the green ink she kept for examinations and for letters to people she meant to defeat.

“I am going to win it.”

“*Bien sûr.* I do not doubt it.”

Nobody did. Cecily had come first in every lesson they had shared since they were seven, and Caleb, right beside her in all of them, had never troubled to try, and would usually do well enough to satisfy their parents but not well enough to draw any attention, which was just as he preferred.

“Will you put in yours?”

“I had not thought of it.”

“You never do.” She tucked her feet up under her shawl. “You do not decide on most things, *mon frère*. Things happen to you, and then you smile at them, and afterwards they are persuaded it was your idea from the beginning. So I shall decide this one for both of us, and win it for Blanchemer. You shall sit in the stands in your flannel and tell me afterwards that I was magnificent.”

He considered this, and found it exceedingly in his favour, and nodded solemnly.

“I shall practise,” he promised. “*Magnifique.* *Superbe.* I shall have a new word for every task, so that you do not grow tired of them.”

“You will forget them all and say *bravo*, as you do at the opera.”

“Only when the soprano is very bad. When she is good I say nothing, and weep into Maman’s sleeve.”

“The Contis will be there,” Cecily added, more quietly.

The carriage went on through the dark, and for a while neither of them said anything, and the boys by the stove shrieked as their cards went up in a puff of green smoke.

At home, in the long gallery at Blanchemer, there hung a portrait of their great-grandfather, and whenever the name of Conti was spoken in its hearing it turned its face to the wall. Once, at a Christmas dinner, when an uncle had drunk more elf-made wine than was wise and told the story of the Siege of Lyon at length, it had climbed out of its frame altogether and gone to sulk in a still life of pears, and it had taken two house-elves and a ladder to coax it back. The painter had been kind to him. *Grand-mère*, who had dressed that face every morning until the old man died, said that the Contis had made a fire at Lyon which no healer had ever learned to put out of a wound. That was the Contis, so far as Caleb had ever known them. They were a turned back in a gilt frame, and a silence at dinner, and the reason his mother would not keep black roses in the house.

“How many?” he wondered. He had heard it all at last Sunday’s luncheon at their estate in Sologne, but had been occupied throughout with thoughts about the upcoming hunt.

“Three at Durmstrang, four if you include their uncle.” Cecily gave him a look, knowing he had been thinking of the hunt instead of listening to Papa and Andrew discuss the Tournament, despite being seated right next to them. She counted them off on her fingers. “Rocco, a cousin, who is cruel. Vittorio, another cousin, a year younger, who follows Rocco about like his shadow and laughs whenever Rocco is cruel. And the younger son.” Her mouth thinned. “The elder son, Dario, is long done with Durmstrang, *Dieu merci*, but it is said that the cousins still do whatever he writes to tell them. Andrew says we are not to speak to any of them. They are taught the Dark Arts on that ship, Caleb, and not the defence against them, as we are. They are set the arts themselves, twice a week, out of a book, and the Contis have been first in them since before the Institute kept lists.”

“And the younger son? You have told me what the cousins are. You have not told me what he is.”

“Nobody seems to know. He has been first in the Dark Arts every year since he was eleven. Andrew has seen the Institute’s lists, and went quite white over them, and would not tell me the margin. And nobody has ever once seen him use them. That is what I like least about him.”

“I have never met a Conti, you understand.” Caleb laid his hand over Andrew’s letter, through his coat, and it crackled reproachfully. “I shall form my own opinion. It is the privilege of the youngest. Everyone else formed theirs before I was born, and nothing remains for me but to be fair.”

“Be fair from the other side of the Hall, then.”

“I am always fair from the other side of the Hall, Cecy. It is the only place from which one can be.”

She glanced at him sidelong, because that was truer than he had meant it to be, and he smiled at her until she looked away. It was the first thing either of them had been taught, before their letters and long before their wands. One was gracious to everybody, and close to nobody who did not bear one’s name. Their grandmother had put it more plainly, in the stillroom, with her sleeves rolled up. *The world may have your manners, chéri. Keep the rest at home.*

At the front of the carriage the Headmistress called for them all to sit, and to put away their Snap.

The palominos folded their wings.

The carriage dropped. It went down in a long swooping glide with its wheels spinning on the empty air and a girl near the front squealing with joy, and Caleb watched it all come up to meet them, the black mountains and the black lake, and on its crag above the water, with every window lit, the castle. It went up in tower upon tower and turret upon turret, with bridges and battlements climbing one above another into the night, like a wedding cake built by a family that had never once in its history been told no. Caleb had grown up in a house with a hundred and twelve rooms, and still he put his hand flat to the glass and laughed under his breath.

He tried to count the towers and lost his place at nine, because a staircase he could see through a lit window had begun, quite slowly, to move.

“*Regarde*, Cecy. The stairs.”

“I see them.”

“They are going somewhere.”

“So are we.” Cecily braced her feet. “Hold on to something.”

The hooves struck the lawn like twelve cannons fired at a christening. The great wheels touched and bounced and touched again, and the carriage settled on its springs with a long sigh.

Caleb took his hand from the glass. There was a print of it left behind on the pane, five fingers in the mist, pointing up at the castle, and he left it there.

The cold met him at the foot of the steps like a creditor.

It went straight through his silk robes and through the flannel, which he was in fact wearing, and he breathed it in with relish. The Hogwarts students stood in rows on the castle steps in black robes and scarves of four colours, to greet their guests, their breath going up over their heads like the smoke over a village. Many of the girls were excited to see the Beauxbatons convoy, especially with two Blanchemers among them. The de Blanchemers had been painted by every portraitist in France for six generations, and not one of them had ever needed flattering. They were among the oldest pureblood houses in Europe, and their magic was as famous as their faces. Caleb and Cecily both had the aristocratic nose of the Blanchemers, and their high brows and cheekbones, but unlike their siblings they had also inherited their mother’s warm enchanting beauty with curly chestnut hair and twinkling hazel eyes. He smiled charmingly towards the steps, and two first-year girls at the end of the row went pink to the ears and clutched one another.

“Stop that,” Cecily hissed at his shoulder.

“I am simply smiling, *ma chère sœur*.”

“You are shining at them, *mon petit frère*.”

“It is bitterly cold, Cecy. Someone must.”

A tall old wizard came down the steps to meet the Headmistress, with a silver beard tucked into his belt and half-moon spectacles that caught the carriage lamps, and kissed her hand with the air of a man who had been looking forward to it since the summer. That was Professor Dumbledore. Caleb had seen his picture on a Chocolate Frog card when he was six, and had kept it in the frame of his mirror for a year, and was a little disappointed to find that the real one did not wave.

At the top of the steps the Headmistress gathered her seniors with one sweep of her arm, as a swan gathers in her cygnets, and took them in out of the cold. Behind them Hogwarts had begun to turn and peer out at the lake, but Beauxbatons climbed up the steps two by two without hurrying and without a glance back at the water, and Caleb, who had been taught at seven to walk into a strange house as if it had been expecting him, went in with his sister on his arm and did not look back.

He wanted to. Something was happening on the lake, he could hear it, a deep sucking murmur like a bath the size of a parish beginning to be let out, and every Hogwarts head on the steps had turned to it. But one did not turn around on the steps of a house where one was a guest, to stare at another guest arriving, as if one had never seen water move.

“The lake has begun to turn,” Cecily informed him out of the corner of her mouth. “Something is coming out of it.”

“How do you know?”

“I looked in the brass of the door handle.”

“*Tricheuse.* That is cheating.”

“It is a great deal better than craning one’s neck like a tourist on the Pont Neuf.” Cecily swept him in.

The ship rose out of the lake bow first, like something drowned that would not stay down, with the black water pouring off it in sheets and the weed hanging from its yards. On its deck the Durmstrang seniors stood in their furs in the freezing spray and let it run down their faces.

Arian stood at the rail with the water running out of his hair.

They had come all the way under the sea, with the portholes black and the lamps lit day and night and the hull groaning around them in the cold, and he had not minded it. Under the sea nobody could see the ship and nobody wrote to it. Now the lake broke over the rail and fell back, and the night came in at once, sharp and clean, smelling of pine and frost, and above the water the castle stood up on its crag with every window lit, as if somebody had poured gold into it and left it to cool.

“*Smotri*,” Vittorio said in Russian, at his elbow. “Look at it. Like a cake. Like a cake at a peasant wedding.”

“Look at the lawn,” said Rocco.

There was a carriage on the lawn. It stood at the edge of the Forest, pale blue and ridiculous, the size of a house, with a dozen great golden horses being led away from it with their wings folded, steaming in the cold. Lamps burned in all its windows. One of them still had a handprint on it, pale in the mist on the glass, as if somebody had pressed his palm there to look out.

“Beauxbatons,” Rocco murmured. He said it as he said most things, pleasantly, as if he had been asked the time. “And in it, I am told, two of the white house. The two youngest. Dario wrote to me about them.” He smiled out at the carriage. “He sends them his regards.”

Vittorio laughed. He had a laugh like a tray of cups going over, and he used it whenever Rocco paused.

Arian said nothing. He had said nothing to either of them since the Baltic, and they had stopped expecting it somewhere off the coast of Norway, and had gone on talking to each other across him, which was what they preferred.

Behind them, on the wet deck, a first-year in a fur cap three sizes too large was being sick into a bucket, as quietly as he could. Vittorio saw, and nudged Rocco, and Rocco looked down at the child with great kindness.

“Mirek. You are dripping on my boots.”

The boy lifted his head. He was eleven, and white to the lips. Durmstrang had sailed its ship with its own youngest since before anybody could remember, and every year a handful of first-years were chosen to go to sea with the seniors, to coil rope and scrub decks and learn the ship from the keel up. Mirek had begged for it, and been chosen, and had been sick from the first day out. Since the second week of term he had followed Arian about the ship with the patient devotion of a hound, which Arian had never asked for and did not know how to send away.

“Sorry,” Mirek whispered. “Sorry, Rocco.”

“Wipe them.”

Arian stepped between them, without looking at either of his cousins, and took the bucket from the child, and handed it to another student coming off watch, and stood where he was. He did not say anything, and did not need to. Rocco looked at him, and smiled, and after a moment turned away to the rail as if the boots had never been mentioned. He never laid a hand on Arian. None of them ever did. Arian was the younger son of the head of the house, and that was a thing one did not touch, any more than one touched the ikon in the great hall at Chernoyar. There were other ways.

“Come,” said Enzo, behind them all.

His uncle stood by the gangway in his black coat, buttoning his gloves. He was tall and thin and grey at the temples, and he looked at the castle once, as he looked at everything, and did not trouble to look at it again.

“They are waiting for us. We will not keep them.”

They went ashore in the order the Headmaster liked, which was the order of the house. The Headmaster and Enzo went first, then Rocco and Vittorio, then Arian, alone, then Mirek and the rest of them in a long red file up the frozen lawn. The castle came nearer, and grew, and its great doors opened as they climbed the steps, and warm air came out to meet them, and noise, and the smell of a great many candles.

Arian went in after his cousins thinking of nothing at all.

The Great Hall had been told it was to have guests, and it had risen to the occasion.

Its ceiling had been enchanted to look like the sky, and the sky that night was doing its best for the visitors, with a high white moon and a few clouds drawn out across it as thin as gauze, and the stars come out as thick as salt on a dark tablecloth. Beneath it floated candles in their hundreds, without a holder among them, and their flames stood straight up in the still air and turned the long tables to gold.

The Beauxbatons party had been seated at the far end of the Ravenclaw table, under the tall windows, while the rest of the Hall was still empty. In the dark glass at Caleb’s shoulder the lake opened, and out of it the whole ship rose into the moonlight with the water pouring off its sides. A Beauxbatons girl further down the table half stood to see, and sat down again under one look from the Headmistress. Caleb glanced at the window as if it were only the weather, and unfolded his napkin.

The girl was Odile Marchand, from Rouen, who had sung Cherubino in the school opera in the spring and made the Headmistress cry. She sat now with her hands in her lap and her face scarlet and her soup untouched before her, and Caleb, three places along, saw that she would not lift her eyes from it all evening if somebody did not do something.

Then the great doors opened again, and he forgot her for a while.

The Durmstrang party came up the Hall in a body, in blood-red robes under heavy cloaks of fur. At their head walked their Headmaster, lean and silver-furred, and beside him walked a tall, thin dark man in black, with grey at his temples and black gloves on his hands, who looked at everything once and did not trouble to look at it again.

“The uncle,” Cecily murmured, to the gold jug in front of her.

She did not turn her head. Their governess had held that a de Blanchemer turned around in public for a fire and for nothing less, and Cecily watched them in the curve of the jug, where the whole Hall lay bent and gilded and nobody could catch her at it, while Caleb looked at the candles.

He did not need to be told which of the students were the Contis. There were clearly three of them, walking ahead of the other students, who kept a careful distance behind them, as one keeps back from a hearth that throws sparks. They were all tall and all dark, with black hair and black eyes and harsh, handsome faces that seemed to have been carved by the weather out of frigid ice. The one right in front was broad in the shoulder, handsome in the way of a carved door, with arrogant features. The one at his right elbow was slim and light-footed, and a year younger, with a laugh like a dropped tray, and he put his feet where the first one had put his.

“Rocco,” Cecily told the jug, “and Vittorio.”

The third Conti came after them, alone, with an empty yard of floor before him and another behind.

He wore no hat. His hair was black and swept back from a high pale brow, and the moon in the enchanted ceiling lay on it like frost on a slate roof. He was the tallest of the three and the handsomest, with a sharp nose, sharp cheekbones and a jawline that could cut. He walked with a slow, deliberate stride, his cloak open over his red robes as if he did not feel the cold. Behind him followed the rest of Durmstrang, and at their head a first-year in a fur cap three sizes too large, who was trotting to keep up with his eyes fixed on the third Conti’s back.

Halfway up the far side of the Hall, the Conti turned to look at Caleb.

He did not slow. There was nothing in his face at all, and his eyes were as black as his hair, and the look came across two empty tables and the whole width of the floor and settled on Caleb as if it had been sent for him and had found him at the first attempt.

Caleb met the look levelly, as he would have met any guest’s in his mother’s house, and gave him the faintest courteous inclination of the head, which was all that civility required and not a hair more. The look did not move on, as looks did. It stayed on him like the heat from an open oven door, and Caleb, who had never yet been the first to look away from anybody, dropped his eyes to the cloth. Then Rocco glanced back and spoke a word, and the boy turned his face away and went on to the Slytherin table with the others.

Caleb found that he had folded the corner of his napkin into a neat triangle, and then into a smaller one, and he unfolded it again and laid it flat.

He had been looked at all his life, in Paris and in Sologne, at the opera and at every ball since he was six, by duchesses and by footmen and by a great many young men and women who had then gone home and written poetry about it, some of which had been sent to him and some of which had been sent, by mistake, to Andrew. He knew every kind of look there was, the ones that admired and the ones that hoped and the ones that measured the Blanchemer pearls. He did not know this one. It had not wanted to be noticed, and it had not cared whether it was. It had simply been there, and taken what it came for, and gone.

He did not like it. He told himself so, firmly, and then, because he had been raised to be honest at least with himself, he admitted that he did not entirely dislike it either, and that this was a good deal more alarming.

The Durmstrang party took its places at the far end of the Slytherin table, by the opposite wall. There were two house tables between them, Gryffindor and Hufflepuff, and given the good deal of history between the Blanchemers and the Contis, Caleb suspected that someone had carefully measured that distance.

“Arian,” Cecily informed the jug, low and cool, because a Conti had looked at her brother before the whole Hall as if he had a right to, and she had not cared for it. “The younger son. Nobody knows anything of him for certain, only what is whispered. It is said that at fourteen he disarmed his entire year with a single charm, and gave the wands back a week later without a word to anyone. That is whispered. His marks in the Dark Arts are not. And he has just looked at you, Caleb, before three schools. I should very much like to know why.”

“He must not feel the cold,” Caleb observed. “The cloak must be decorative, then, since it is not properly worn. How incredibly casual!”

Cecily sniffed in disdainful agreement. “He is from Durmstrang, *voyons*. They are used to freezing weather. This is probably mild for them.” She turned the jug a quarter turn, so that the Slytherin table went out of it. “You acknowledged him.”

“I inclined my head, as civility requires. One inclines one’s head to anybody who passes one’s table, and you would have done as much yourself if you had not been so occupied with the jug.”

“Not to a Conti.”

“To anybody, Cecy. Need I remind you of our manners, *ma chère sœur*? I am courteous to everyone.”

“Yes,” Cecily retorted, “and that has always been the trouble with you.”

Hogwarts, which had stood out on the steps to see both its guests in, came in last, with cheeks stung red from the cold. The students quickly filled the benches in a rush of scarves and chatter, which quieted and became whispers the closer they came to the Durmstrang end of the Slytherin table. Two Gryffindor boys who had been shoving each other down the aisle stopped shoving as they passed it. A little Hufflepuff girl sat down, saw who was sitting at the next table, and stood up again and went to sit somewhere else.

Then the plates filled themselves in honour of the guests, with a bouillabaisse made by someone who had heard of bouillabaisse but never tried one, and *coq au vin* beside the steak-and-kidney pie, and for Durmstrang a borscht the colour of garnets, and pelmeni, and a dish of pickled herring that no one from Hogwarts or Beauxbatons would go near. All down the Beauxbatons end the seniors bent their heads over the bouillabaisse and began, with great politeness, to be disappointed in it.

Odile Marchand had not moved. The soup in front of her had stopped steaming.

Caleb took up the basket of bread, which was nearer to him than to anyone, and instead of helping himself he leaned past the two boys between them and held it out to her first.

“*Tenez*, Mademoiselle Marchand. You were quite right to stand.”

She lifted her head, startled, and went redder still.

“It is not every evening that a ship comes up out of a lake to dine with us,” Caleb went on, low enough that only their end of the table could hear, “and one of us ought to have risen to greet it. I would have done it myself, only I was afraid it would ask me to dance, and I had not been introduced.”

Odile laughed. It burst out of her before she could stop it, a high clear peal from the top of Cherubino’s register, and half the Beauxbatons end looked up, and at the head of the table the Headmistress turned her great head down the length of the cloth to see who had laughed at her table in a strange house.

She found Caleb.

He smiled at her. The smile he had given the steps had been for everybody and had cost him nothing. This was the one he kept for his mother when she came down late to breakfast, and it went up the table to the Headmistress with nothing held back, warm and glad and a little apologetic, as if they had shared a joke that the rest of the room had missed. The Headmistress considered him for a long moment. Then her mouth, which had not unbent since the Pyrenees, unbent, and she smiled back at him, and shook her head as one shakes it over a favourite nephew, and turned to the Hogwarts staff again.

All down the Beauxbatons end the shoulders came down. Somebody passed the bread. Odile took a roll from the basket and ate her cold soup with every sign of enjoying it, and the noise came back up the table louder and warmer than before, and spilled over the gap onto the Ravenclaws, until they too were laughing, though not one of them had heard the joke.

“You are impossible,” Cecily told him.

“I am hungry, Cecy. One cannot eat with a soprano in mourning three places away. It is bad for the digestion.”

Everyone in the Hall knew of the feud between the de Blanchemers and the von Contis. Half the Hall had read the piece in the *Prophet*, and the other half had been told about it by the first half at breakfast, with improvements. The nearest Ravenclaw was a lanky seventh-year with ink on his fingers, who sat across a yard of empty bench that nobody had told him to keep and that he kept all the same, and who had brought the cutting folded into the back of a book on goblin wars. Before the pie had gone round he leaned over the gap, very red, to ask whether it was true that Caleb’s grandfather had once turned the Conti grandfather into a hatstand at Lyon.

“An umbrella stand, monsieur,” Caleb corrected him gently, “and only for an afternoon.”

He said nothing more about it, and the Ravenclaw, despite being very curious, found that he was perfectly content with the answer, charmed as he was by Caleb’s smile. He was called Ambrose Fennick, and he told Caleb so, and told Cecily so as well, and then told her a great deal about the goblin rebellion of 1612, to which she listened with the expression of a woman who has been offered a dish she does not care for and is too well brought up to refuse.

After that it went as it generally went. By the time the plates were cleared, half the Ravenclaw table had found some reason to lean down towards the Beauxbatons end, and Caleb had been charming to every one of them. He asked after their houses and their lessons and their owls, and remembered the answers, and made each of them laugh at least once, and when they went back to their own end of the table not one of them could have said what Caleb de Blanchemer had told them in return. He was sunlight through a window, warm on everybody and the same on everybody, and nobody who sat in it ever quite found the latch. He was a de Blanchemer, and he was friendly to all the world and friends with none of it, because that was how a de Blanchemer was raised.

Cecily ate her bouillabaisse in silence and watched the Slytherin table.

The Durmstrang end was not quiet. Rocco sat with his chin on his clasped hands, chewing slowly and watching nothing but the two de Blanchemers, first at one and then at the other, as a man looks over a pair of horses he has been told are not for sale, and Vittorio turned his knife in the candlelight, idly, so that the light ran down the blade towards the Ravenclaw table.

Caleb continued with his supper, and ignored the poor attempt at intimidation. After a while Vittorio grew tired of it and put the knife down.

“And the uncle?” Caleb asked. “What is he called?”

Among the Hogwarts professors sat the Headmistress of Beauxbatons, and the Headmaster of Durmstrang in his silver furs, and at the end, between a witch in a green hat and a tiny wizard on three cushions, the thin dark man in black. He ate his soup with the patience of someone who had been served worse and would be served worse again. In the centre of the table, presiding over it all in robes the colour of a ripe plum, Professor Dumbledore was laughing at something the Headmistress had said.

“Enzo von Conti,” Cecily muttered. “Andrew says he is the only one of them with any sense.”

“That is a great deal of sense for one man to keep on behalf of a whole family.”

He glanced at the Durmstrang end of the Hall, at the last Conti, and found that the last Conti had not stopped looking at him, and had not, apparently, any intention of stopping. There was nothing cold in it. The face was all winter, but the look came across two house tables as warm as a hand laid on the back of his neck.

Caleb studied the bats in the rafters instead, and at the stars, and at the moon on the enchanted ceiling, and resolved that he would not look across the Hall again before the pudding. He had looked twice more before the pudding came.

The Durmstrang end of the Slytherin table was not quiet, and Arian had known it would not be.

It was never quiet where Rocco sat. Rocco talked. He talked low and easily in Russian, in the reasonable voice Dario had taught him, and everything he said was pleasant, and none of it was kind. He talked about the Hogwarts students as they filed in, which of them were Mudbloods by the look of their shoes and which of them would cry first if one spoke to them in the corridor. He talked about the food, and the old man in the plum robes, and the Headmistress of Beauxbatons, whom he called the giantess. He talked about the two de Blanchemers under the windows, and what Dario had said of their family in his last letter, and what their great-grandfather had looked like when he came out of Lyon with half his face burnt off by the fire their own house had made, which was in the family book with a drawing, and which Rocco had been trying to cast since he was fourteen, and he said it all smiling, with his chin on his hands, as if he were telling a story he was fond of.

He sat on Arian’s left, a little nearer than there was any need for, which was how Dario used to sit when he wished Arian to remember that he could. Vittorio sat across from them with his back to the wall and a heel of bread in his fist, and Mirek sat on Arian’s right, because Mirek always did.

Vittorio had found a use for the bread. He was tearing it into crumbs and flicking them, one at a time, at the first-year, and every time one landed in the boy’s hair or his ear or his soup, he laughed, and looked at Rocco to see whether Rocco had seen. Mirek went on eating with his face turned down and red. When the salt came round, Vittorio took it and emptied half of it into the boy’s borscht, carefully, while Mirek watched him do it and did not dare to stop him, and then he passed it on.

“*Kushay*,” Rocco suggested. “Eat it. It would be rude to the English to leave it.”

Mirek lifted his spoon.

Arian took the bowl from in front of him and set it down in front of himself, and put his own untouched bowl before the boy, and went on looking down the Hall.

Vittorio laughed, a little too loud. Rocco said nothing at all for a while, and then he began to talk about something else, in the same pleasant voice. That was how it was done in their family. One said a great deal, and all of it was meant to hurt, and what was not said was left to the silence, which had been in the family longer than any of them and knew its business.

Arian had eaten nothing. Everyone around him had fallen on the food as if they had been at sea for a month, which they had, and Arian sat with his hands under the table and watched the windows across the Hall.

A boy and a girl were seated under the windows among the Beauxbatons students, and despite never having seen them before, Arian recognised the refined, fine-boned features of aristocracy and knew them to be Blanchemers.

He would have known it without Rocco. He had grown up among the old houses of the north, where blood was a thing one could see across a ballroom, in the set of a head and the length of a hand, and these two had it as plainly as if it had been written over them in gold. They had the same face, the two of them, high-browed and clean and finely made, and the same curls, the brown of chestnuts when the husk has just split, and they sat side by side with the same straight backs. But they did not sit in the same way.

The girl was on guard. He saw it in the first minute. She held herself very still, and she did not turn her head towards the Slytherin table once, and yet she had not stopped watching it since the Durmstrang party came in. She watched it in the gold jug in front of her. He had seen her turn the jug a quarter turn, so that he went out of it. She would make a good enemy, and he thought she meant to be one.

The boy was not on guard at all.

He sat in the middle of the candlelight as if it had been lit for him, and perhaps it had. The candles had drifted that way, it seemed to Arian, as candles drift towards a door left open on a summer night, until they hung thicker over the Beauxbatons end than anywhere else in the Hall and the gold plates there shone like the inside of a church at Easter. The noise was louder there too, and kinder, and all of it was turned one way, towards him. People leaned in to talk to him. Ravenclaws came down their own table on one pretext after another and stood at his shoulder, and he turned to each of them as if he had been waiting all evening for that one face, and they went away again, lit up, like lamps that had been carried past a fire.

He had beautiful hands. Arian saw them when he broke his bread, long and quick and certain, and when he laid them still on the cloth they did not fidget, as the hands of the Hogwarts boys fidgeted. His curls had come a little loose from the wind on the steps and he had not troubled to put them right. He laughed, now and then, at something somebody said, but only a little, and with his mouth closed, as if the real laugh were being kept somewhere else, for somebody else, and Arian found that he wanted, with a want that went through him like a blade, to know where it was kept, and for whom.

Arian had seen him from the doors.

He had come up the Hall behind his cousins and looked once towards the windows, and for half a second his step had faltered on the flags. The boy had met his look and inclined his head to him, gravely, as one great house greets another. It was only manners, and meant for anybody, and Arian had carried it the rest of the way up the Hall all the same, like a coal carried across a winter yard in a cupped hand, and before he reached his own table he knew that he would not set it down again.

Now it had gone down under his ribs and caught, where nothing had burned for twelve years. His heart was going as it went before a duel, and under the table his hands had shut until the nails cut his palms. In front of him the water in his goblet began to skin over with ice from the rim inwards, in fine white feathers. He looked at it and made it stop, as he had been making things stop since he was six, and the ice went back into the water.

Then the boy smiled at the Headmistress.

It had been over in a moment. A girl down the Beauxbatons table had been sitting over her soup with her face scarlet, over something that had happened before Durmstrang came in, and the boy had leaned across with the bread and said something to her, and she had laughed. And the Headmistress had turned to see who dared, and the boy had smiled at her.

Arian knew every smile that was given to a Conti. There was the one that was afraid of him and the one that wanted something, and the one his father gave to men who were about to be sorry, and the one Dario gave him across the breakfast table on the mornings Dario had arranged something. He had been given all of them since he could walk, and he could tell them apart across a room as other men told wines apart, and he had believed there were no others.

He had never in all his life seen a smile that could light up a room.

The boy had smiled up the table at that great severe woman, and she had smiled back as if she could not help it. Then it had spread down the Beauxbatons end and across the gap and along the Ravenclaw table, like daylight coming down a valley one farm at a time, until half the Hall was warmer for it. He had gone back to his soup without the least idea that he had done anything.

“Look at him,” Rocco murmured in Russian.

Arian said nothing.

“The little prince of the white house holding court,” Vittorio sang under his breath, and laughed his tray-dropping laugh.

A Hogwarts girl had stopped at the boy’s shoulder to say something, a fair girl in a yellow scarf, and the boy looked up and smiled at her, and Arian watched her go scarlet from the collar to the hair. Then a Beauxbatons girl across the table from him, and another a little further down, and a Ravenclaw at the end with her book held up before her face, one after another, every time he turned that smile on them, as poppies open in a field when the sun comes out. Not one of them came an inch nearer for it. He gave it away to every one of them, and kept something back from every one of them, and Arian, who had kept everything back all his life because he had been obliged to, saw it, and understood it, and wanted with the whole of himself to be the one he did not keep it from.

After a while Rocco leaned in until his mouth was by Arian’s ear.

“*Bratets.* You are staring.”

Arian looked at the light.

Rocco smiled, and it was not kind. “You have always liked beautiful, useless things. Tell me,” he chuckled, “how do you think a Blanchemer breaks?”

He had said the first part of it before, in the stable yard at Chernoyar, when they were six. Arian had been kneeling in the straw with something warm cupped in his two hands, and Dario had found him there and taken it from him, and Rocco had stood at Dario’s elbow and said it, and laughed, and had been saying it in one way or another ever since.

Arian did not remember what had happened to the bird. He remembered his hands afterwards, empty in the straw. He remembered that he had not cried, because Dario had been watching to see whether he would. And he remembered that after that day he had never again let any of them see him care for anything, because whatever he cared for, Dario took, and whatever Dario took was not given back.

So he did not answer. He had learned in the stable yard what came of answering. He sat with his hands under the table through the rest of the meat and the clearing of the plates, and looked at the light, and let Rocco watch him do it, until the pudding rose up out of the gold plates and Rocco turned away to eat.

But the question stayed where Rocco had put it, by his ear, and went on asking itself in Rocco’s pleasant voice, and Arian heard it, and understood that Rocco had seen where he was looking, and that Rocco wrote to Dario.

There were towers of treacle tart and trifle, and at the far end of the Ravenclaw table a pyramid of macarons, and before the Durmstrang party a dark honey cake in a hundred thin layers that any cook at Chernoyar would have sniffed at. Arian cut his slice in two and slid the larger half onto Mirek’s plate, and Mirek looked at it, and at him, and ate it in four bites before Vittorio could see.

Then Professor Dumbledore got to his feet, and the Hall went quiet for him. He spoke of the Tournament, revived after long and well-deserved neglect, of three champions and three tasks and eternal glory, and of the danger, which he did not make light of, and which made the whole of Hogwarts lean forward on its benches as one. Then a caretaker came shuffling up the Hall with a jewelled casket, and Professor Dumbledore tapped its lid three times with his wand.

The Goblet of Fire was a plain thing. It was a great roughly hewn cup of wood, brimming over with flames of a white so blue and so cold that at the Ravenclaw table somebody said *oh* out loud. It would stand in the Entrance Hall from tonight, Professor Dumbledore explained, and any student who was of age might put a name into it until the Halloween feast, a week tomorrow, when it would choose. There would be no withdrawing once it had spoken. He would draw an Age Line around it himself, so that nobody under seventeen need trouble to try.

Every face in the Hall was turned up the room to the blue-white fire.

Arian looked at the windows.

The boy under them was studying the Goblet with his chin on his hand and his head a little on one side, as if it were a painting somebody had told him was very fine and he had not yet made up his mind about it. Then, as if he had felt it, he turned his head and met Arian’s eyes straight across the Hall, and for a moment neither of them looked away.

It was the boy who looked away first, down at his plate, and the colour came up along his cheekbones as Arian watched, faint and quick, like the first light on snow.

Arian stared down at his own hands.

At Durmstrang a boy was taught to go after what he wanted in the open, or not at all, and to be ashamed of nothing but failing to get it. It was the first thing they taught, before the curses and the drills, and Arian had believed it, as one believes what one has never had to test. He tested it now, sitting at that table, and found that it was true, and that it frightened him more than anything had frightened him since the stable yard.

The Beauxbatons party left the Hall first, as it had come in first, and Caleb went out with his sister on his arm and felt the look all the way down the Hall between his shoulders, like a draught from a door that somebody has left open behind one.

He did not turn around. He walked as he had been taught to walk out of a ballroom, without hurrying and without looking back, and he smiled at the Hufflepuffs as he passed, and they smiled back. On the steps the cold came up to meet him again, and he breathed it in gratefully, as if he had been too long in an overheated room.

“He is still looking,” Cecily remarked.

“How can you possibly know that?”

“The door knocker.”

“*Mais non*, Cecy, you cannot see the Slytherin table in a door knocker. It is a lion. It is facing the wrong way.”

“I do not need to see him. I can feel it on you. You are walking as you walked at the Duchesse de Morny’s, the night the Comte followed you around the conservatory with a camellia.”

“The Comte was twice my age.”

“And you walked just like this.” She steered him down the steps onto the frozen lawn, where the carriage glowed at the edge of the Forest with all its windows lit. “Andrew says we are not to speak to any of them.”

“I have not spoken to any of them. I inclined my head. One of them looked at me for the rest of the evening, which I could not prevent and did not invite, and which I shall mention to nobody, least of all Andrew, who would come up from Paris tonight in his dressing gown.”

Cecily was silent for a few steps, her breath going up white.

“Why was he looking at you?”

“I have not the least idea.”

That was not altogether true, and he did not like it. He had spent the whole of supper not liking it, in between the bouillabaisse and the Ravenclaws. Everything about the boy was winter, the still face and the stiller hands, the cloak worn open as if the cold were nothing to him, the slow deliberate stride. His gaze was not. It had lain on him all through supper as steady and as close as heat from a banked fire, and once or twice, when he had been unwise enough to meet it, it had burned. He had a notion what a look like that meant, from anybody else. From a von Conti it made no sense at all.

“If he means to look at me like that all year,” he decided, “I shall grow used to it, as one grows used to a portrait in a guest bedroom. By Christmas I shall not notice him at all.”

“You have never once in your life failed to notice a portrait in a guest bedroom. You talk to them.”

“Only to the ones who are lonely.”

The carriage door stood open at the top of its little steps, and warm light came down them onto the frost, and inside somebody had started the Snap game again. Caleb stopped at the bottom and gazed up at the castle.

It stood over them black against the stars, enormous, with its lit windows going up and up into the dark and its towers lost among the clouds, and somewhere inside it a staircase was still moving, because he could hear it, a long grinding groan of stone, very far off, like an old man turning over in his sleep. He thought of a hundred and twelve rooms at Blanchemer and how he had known every one of them by the time he was nine, and every cupboard, and every secret stair, and the loose stone behind the tapestry in the north gallery where the peacocks hid their eggs.

“How long will your bath be?”

Cecily eyed him.

“An hour and a half,” she replied. “Perhaps a little more. I have been in a carriage for three days, Caleb, and I intend to boil.”

“Then I shall not wait up. *Bon bain*, Cecy.”

“You will not be *here* to wait up,” said Cecily, and went up the steps ahead of him into the light.

\*   \*   \*

Arian walked back down the lawn to the ship at his uncle’s side.

The rest of Durmstrang had gone on ahead in a long red line, with Rocco at the head of it and Vittorio at his elbow and Mirek trotting at the back, and the two of them came after it slowly, without lanterns, over the white grass. The frost was coming down hard. The lake lay black and still with the ship in it, and the stars stood in the water upside down around the hull, and away to the left the Beauxbatons carriage shone pale blue at the edge of the Forest with every one of its windows lit.

Enzo did not speak until they were halfway to the shore. When he did, it was in Russian, as everything between them was.

“You looked at the de Blanchemer boy for two hours.”

Arian did not answer. There was nothing to answer. It was true.

“Your cousin saw it,” Enzo went on, in the voice of a man reading out an inventory. “So did Vittorio, and the Headmaster, and in all likelihood the boy’s sister, who has, I am told, the eyes of a customs officer. Your cousin will write to your brother. I expect he has begun the letter in his head already.”

“Yes, Dyadya.”

“I do not say that you must not look.” Enzo walked on a few paces. His breath went up white in the starlight and was gone. “I am not your father, and in any case I have never in my life been able to stop you doing anything you had decided to do. I say only this. Your brother has never yet seen a thing you cared for without putting his hand on it. Be careful what you let him see.”

Arian looked across the lawn at the carriage.

One window had gone dim near the back, low down, where steam had begun to go up from under the eaves into the cold, white and slow. Another, near the front, still burned. As he watched, a shadow crossed it, somebody tall and slight, with a head of curls, who stopped at the glass for a moment and looked out at the castle, and then moved away.

Everything in Arian went towards that window. He felt it go, as a compass needle goes north when the box is opened, and he stood on the frozen grass and did not follow it.

“I will do nothing.”

His uncle regarded him for a long time in the dark.

“Good,” Enzo said at last, and turned for the ship. “That will be a great deal harder.”
