Summary

A Triwizard AU: Durmstrang Arian × Beauxbatons Caleb.

Rule one of the de Blanchemers: never trust a Conti.
Rule two: never let anyone close.
Rule three, which nobody thought to write down: never let a Durmstrang boy take a leaf out of your hair. He will only take it as encouragement.

Caleb de Blanchemer is charming to everyone and close to no one. Arian von Conti has no words for courting, no patience and no sense of personal space. What he has is a stare like a lit hearth, and a habit of leaving dead animals on doorsteps.

“One does not, monsieur,” Caleb tells him. Repeatedly.

Arian is not listening. He is watching Caleb’s mouth.

*   *   *

Cecily de Blanchemer was eleven minutes older than her brother, and she had not let one day of the seventeen years since go by without finding some use for them.

“You will take a chill,” she told him, somewhere over the Scottish border, when he unlatched the carriage window for the fourth time and put his head out into the night.

“Scotland first,” Caleb returned. “Afterwards I shall come in and warm myself at your disapproval, which has never yet failed me on a cold night.”

The wind took the words out of his mouth and flung them back along the flank of the carriage and down into the dark.

He did not mind. The carriage was the size of a house and the soft blue of a duck’s egg, and twelve winged palominos drew it, each as big as a carthorse and half as big again, with wings that rose and fell over the moors with a sound like sails filling in a harbour. Below them the hills lay silver with frost. A river ran between them like a dropped ribbon, and here and there on the black slopes a scatter of sheep lay pale as pearls off a broken string.

He had been told that Scotland was grey, and wet, and full of people who would not trouble to be pleasant about either, and he had looked forward to it for a month. Nobody had told him that it would be silver. He leaned out a little further, so that the cold took him by the ears, and watched a lake slide beneath the wheels with the moon lying in it whole, and thought that if the rest of the country was half so well arranged he would have to write and apologise to it.

Behind him the carriage was warm and full of the Beauxbatons seniors in pale blue silk under every shawl they owned, because the Headmistress had told them Scotland would be cold and none of them had believed her. Four boys by the stove were playing Exploding Snap. They had asked Caleb twice since Carlisle to make a fifth, but had been refused so charmingly both times that they were content to play with just the four of them.

Cecily caught him by the back of the collar and pulled him back in.

“Tiens, Andrew has written again,” he announced, allowing himself to be pulled and settling beside her on the silk bench.

“He wrote on Tuesday.”

“That was the Tuesday letter. This is the Thursday letter.” He drew it from inside his coat. It ran to three sheets in their eldest brother’s hand, which was elegant on the first page and less so on every page after, as it always was when he had a great deal to say, and to Caleb he always had. It smelled of Ministry ink, which smelled of nothing so much as a committee. “‘My dear Caleb and Cecily, you may be assured that the Tournament has been made perfectly safe. I have seen to it personally. Matilda will be at the castle with the security of the French delegation, and you will both have the goodness to do as she tells you. Caleb will wear his flannel.’” He lowered the page. “Why only Caleb, I wonder?”

“Because I have no need to be told.”

“Nor have I. I simply do not listen, which is far more restful for everyone.” He turned to the second sheet, where the hand had begun to run downhill. “There is a great deal more. ‘The castle is old and its staircases are not to be trusted. The lake is deep. The Forest is forbidden, and I have had it confirmed in writing that it is forbidden to the guests as well. Caleb is not to feed anything.’”

“He knows you.”

“He knows a boy of eight and a swan at Chantilly, which is not at all the same. The swan was very rude to me first.” Caleb folded the letter in three, with great care, and put it back over his heart, where it crackled. “I shall answer him from Hogwarts. I shall tell him that I am wearing the flannel and have fed nothing, and that the staircases have behaved beautifully, and he will believe the first and know better than the other two, and we shall both be content.”

Cecily laid her hand on the pocket of her robe, where a square of parchment had lain folded since August, written in the green ink she kept for examinations and for letters to people she meant to defeat.

“I am going to win it.”

“Bien sûr. I do not doubt it.”

Nobody did. Cecily had come first in every lesson they had shared since they were seven, and Caleb, right beside her in all of them, had never troubled to try, and would usually do well enough to satisfy their parents but not well enough to draw any attention, which was just as he preferred.

“Will you put in yours?”

“I had not thought of it.”

“You never do.” She tucked her feet up under her shawl. “You do not decide on most things, mon frère. Things happen to you, and then you smile at them, and afterwards they are persuaded it was your idea from the beginning. So I shall decide this one for both of us, and win it for Blanchemer. You shall sit in the stands in your flannel and tell me afterwards that I was magnificent.”

He considered this, and found it exceedingly in his favour, and nodded solemnly.

“I shall practise,” he promised. “Magnifique. Superbe. I shall have a new word for every task, so that you do not grow tired of them.”

“You will forget them all and say bravo, as you do at the opera.”

“Only when the soprano is very bad. When she is good I say nothing, and weep into Maman’s sleeve.”

“The Contis will be there,” Cecily added, more quietly.

The carriage went on through the dark, and for a while neither of them said anything, and the boys by the stove shrieked as their cards went up in a puff of green smoke.

At home, in the long gallery at Blanchemer, there hung a portrait of their great-grandfather, and whenever the name of Conti was spoken in its hearing it turned its face to the wall. Once, at a Christmas dinner, when an uncle had drunk more elf-made wine than was wise and told the story of the Siege of Lyon at length, it had climbed out of its frame altogether and gone to sulk in a still life of pears, and it had taken two house-elves and a ladder to coax it back. The painter had been kind to him. Grand-mère, who had dressed that face every morning until the old man died, said that the Contis had made a fire at Lyon which no healer had ever learned to put out of a wound. That was the Contis, so far as Caleb had ever known them. They were a turned back in a gilt frame, and a silence at dinner, and the reason his mother would not keep black roses in the house.

“How many?” he wondered. He had heard it all at last Sunday’s luncheon at their estate in Sologne, but had been occupied throughout with thoughts about the upcoming hunt.

“Three at Durmstrang, four if you include their uncle.” Cecily gave him a look, knowing he had been thinking of the hunt instead of listening to Papa and Andrew discuss the Tournament, despite being seated right next to them. She counted them off on her fingers. “Rocco, a cousin, who is cruel. Vittorio, another cousin, a year younger, who follows Rocco about like his shadow and laughs whenever Rocco is cruel. And the younger son.” Her mouth thinned. “The elder son, Dario, is long done with Durmstrang, Dieu merci, but it is said that the cousins still do whatever he writes to tell them. Andrew says we are not to speak to any of them. They are taught the Dark Arts on that ship, Caleb, and not the defence against them, as we are. They are set the arts themselves, twice a week, out of a book, and the Contis have been first in them since before the Institute kept lists.”

“And the younger son? You have told me what the cousins are. You have not told me what he is.”

“Nobody seems to know. He has been first in the Dark Arts every year since he was eleven. Andrew has seen the Institute’s lists, and went quite white over them, and would not tell me the margin. And nobody has ever once seen him use them. That is what I like least about him.”

“I have never met a Conti, you understand.” Caleb laid his hand over Andrew’s letter, through his coat, and it crackled reproachfully. “I shall form my own opinion. It is the privilege of the youngest. Everyone else formed theirs before I was born, and nothing remains for me but to be fair.”

“Be fair from the other side of the Hall, then.”

“I am always fair from the other side of the Hall, Cecy. It is the only place from which one can be.”

She glanced at him sidelong, because that was truer than he had meant it to be, and he smiled at her until she looked away. It was the first thing either of them had been taught, before their letters and long before their wands. One was gracious to everybody, and close to nobody who did not bear one’s name. Their grandmother had put it more plainly, in the stillroom, with her sleeves rolled up. The world may have your manners, chéri. Keep the rest at home.

At the front of the carriage the Headmistress called for them all to sit, and to put away their Snap.

The palominos folded their wings.

The carriage dropped. It went down in a long swooping glide with its wheels spinning on the empty air and a girl near the front squealing with joy, and Caleb watched it all come up to meet them, the black mountains and the black lake, and on its crag above the water, with every window lit, the castle. It went up in tower upon tower and turret upon turret, with bridges and battlements climbing one above another into the night, like a wedding cake built by a family that had never once in its history been told no. Caleb had grown up in a house with a hundred and twelve rooms, and still he put his hand flat to the glass and laughed under his breath.

He tried to count the towers and lost his place at nine, because a staircase he could see through a lit window had begun, quite slowly, to move.

“Regarde, Cecy. The stairs.”

“I see them.”

“They are going somewhere.”

“So are we.” Cecily braced her feet. “Hold on to something.”

The hooves struck the lawn like twelve cannons fired at a christening. The great wheels touched and bounced and touched again, and the carriage settled on its springs with a long sigh.

Caleb took his hand from the glass. There was a print of it left behind on the pane, five fingers in the mist, pointing up at the castle, and he left it there.

*   *   *

The cold met him at the foot of the steps like a creditor.

It went straight through his silk robes and through the flannel, which he was in fact wearing, and he breathed it in with relish. The Hogwarts students stood in rows on the castle steps in black robes and scarves of four colours, to greet their guests, their breath going up over their heads like the smoke over a village. Many of the girls were excited to see the Beauxbatons convoy, especially with two Blanchemers among them. The de Blanchemers had been painted by every portraitist in France for six generations, and not one of them had ever needed flattering. They were among the oldest pureblood houses in Europe, and their magic was as famous as their faces. Caleb and Cecily both had the aristocratic nose of the Blanchemers, and their high brows and cheekbones, but unlike their siblings they had also inherited their mother’s warm enchanting beauty with curly chestnut hair and twinkling hazel eyes. He smiled charmingly towards the steps, and two first-year girls at the end of the row went pink to the ears and clutched one another.

“Stop that,” Cecily hissed at his shoulder.

“I am simply smiling, ma chère sœur.”

“You are shining at them, mon petit frère.”

“It is bitterly cold, Cecy. Someone must.”

A tall old wizard came down the steps to meet the Headmistress, with a silver beard tucked into his belt and half-moon spectacles that caught the carriage lamps, and kissed her hand with the air of a man who had been looking forward to it since the summer. That was Professor Dumbledore. Caleb had seen his picture on a Chocolate Frog card when he was six, and had kept it in the frame of his mirror for a year, and was a little disappointed to find that the real one did not wave.

At the top of the steps the Headmistress gathered her seniors with one sweep of her arm, as a swan gathers in her cygnets, and took them in out of the cold. Behind them Hogwarts had begun to turn and peer out at the lake, but Beauxbatons climbed up the steps two by two without hurrying and without a glance back at the water, and Caleb, who had been taught at seven to walk into a strange house as if it had been expecting him, went in with his sister on his arm and did not look back.

He wanted to. Something was happening on the lake, he could hear it, a deep sucking murmur like a bath the size of a parish beginning to be let out, and every Hogwarts head on the steps had turned to it. But one did not turn around on the steps of a house where one was a guest, to stare at another guest arriving, as if one had never seen water move.

“The lake has begun to turn,” Cecily informed him out of the corner of her mouth. “Something is coming out of it.”

“How do you know?”

“I looked in the brass of the door handle.”

“Tricheuse. That is cheating.”

“It is a great deal better than craning one’s neck like a tourist on the Pont Neuf.” Cecily swept him in.

*   *   *

The ship rose out of the lake bow first, like something drowned that would not stay down, with the black water pouring off it in sheets and the weed hanging from its yards. On its deck the Durmstrang seniors stood in their furs in the freezing spray and let it run down their faces.

Arian stood at the rail with the water running out of his hair.

They had come all the way under the sea, with the portholes black and the lamps lit day and night and the hull groaning around them in the cold, and he had not minded it. Under the sea nobody could see the ship and nobody wrote to it. Now the lake broke over the rail and fell back, and the night came in at once, sharp and clean, smelling of pine and frost, and above the water the castle stood up on its crag with every window lit, as if somebody had poured gold into it and left it to cool.

“Smotri,” Vittorio said in Russian, at his elbow. “Look at it. Like a cake. Like a cake at a peasant wedding.”

“Look at the lawn,” said Rocco.

There was a carriage on the lawn. It stood at the edge of the Forest, pale blue and ridiculous, the size of a house, with a dozen great golden horses being led away from it with their wings folded, steaming in the cold. Lamps burned in all its windows. One of them still had a handprint on it, pale in the mist on the glass, as if somebody had pressed his palm there to look out.

“Beauxbatons,” Rocco murmured. He said it as he said most things, pleasantly, as if he had been asked the time. “And in it, I am told, two of the white house. The two youngest. Dario wrote to me about them.” He smiled out at the carriage. “He sends them his regards.”

Vittorio laughed. He had a laugh like a tray of cups going over, and he used it whenever Rocco paused.

Arian said nothing. He had said nothing to either of them since the Baltic, and they had stopped expecting it somewhere off the coast of Norway, and had gone on talking to each other across him, which was what they preferred.

Behind them, on the wet deck, a first-year in a fur cap three sizes too large was being sick into a bucket, as quietly as he could. Vittorio saw, and nudged Rocco, and Rocco looked down at the child with great kindness.

“Mirek. You are dripping on my boots.”

The boy lifted his head. He was eleven, and white to the lips. Durmstrang had sailed its ship with its own youngest since before anybody could remember, and every year a handful of first-years were chosen to go to sea with the seniors, to coil rope and scrub decks and learn the ship from the keel up. Mirek had begged for it, and been chosen, and had been sick from the first day out. Since the second week of term he had followed Arian about the ship with the patient devotion of a hound, which Arian had never asked for and did not know how to send away.

“Sorry,” Mirek whispered. “Sorry, Rocco.”

“Wipe them.”

Arian stepped between them, without looking at either of his cousins, and took the bucket from the child, and handed it to another student coming off watch, and stood where he was. He did not say anything, and did not need to. Rocco looked at him, and smiled, and after a moment turned away to the rail as if the boots had never been mentioned. He never laid a hand on Arian. None of them ever did. Arian was the younger son of the head of the house, and that was a thing one did not touch, any more than one touched the ikon in the great hall at Chernoyar. There were other ways.

“Come,” said Enzo, behind them all.

His uncle stood by the gangway in his black coat, buttoning his gloves. He was tall and thin and grey at the temples, and he looked at the castle once, as he looked at everything, and did not trouble to look at it again.

“They are waiting for us. We will not keep them.”

They went ashore in the order the Headmaster liked, which was the order of the house. The Headmaster and Enzo went first, then Rocco and Vittorio, then Arian, alone, then Mirek and the rest of them in a long red file up the frozen lawn. The castle came nearer, and grew, and its great doors opened as they climbed the steps, and warm air came out to meet them, and noise, and the smell of a great many candles.

Arian went in after his cousins thinking of nothing at all.

*   *   *

The Great Hall had been told it was to have guests, and it had risen to the occasion.

Its ceiling had been enchanted to look like the sky, and the sky that night was doing its best for the visitors, with a high white moon and a few clouds drawn out across it as thin as gauze, and the stars come out as thick as salt on a dark tablecloth. Beneath it floated candles in their hundreds, without a holder among them, and their flames stood straight up in the still air and turned the long tables to gold.

The Beauxbatons party had been seated at the far end of the Ravenclaw table, under the tall windows, while the rest of the Hall was still empty. In the dark glass at Caleb’s shoulder the lake opened, and out of it the whole ship rose into the moonlight with the water pouring off its sides. A Beauxbatons girl further down the table half stood to see, and sat down again under one look from the Headmistress. Caleb glanced at the window as if it were only the weather, and unfolded his napkin.

The girl was Odile Marchand, from Rouen, who had sung Cherubino in the school opera in the spring and made the Headmistress cry. She sat now with her hands in her lap and her face scarlet and her soup untouched before her, and Caleb, three places along, saw that she would not lift her eyes from it all evening if somebody did not do something.

Then the great doors opened again, and he forgot her for a while.

The Durmstrang party came up the Hall in a body, in blood-red robes under heavy cloaks of fur. At their head walked their Headmaster, lean and silver-furred, and beside him walked a tall, thin dark man in black, with grey at his temples and black gloves on his hands, who looked at everything once and did not trouble to look at it again.

“The uncle,” Cecily murmured, to the gold jug in front of her.

She did not turn her head. Their governess had held that a de Blanchemer turned around in public for a fire and for nothing less, and Cecily watched them in the curve of the jug, where the whole Hall lay bent and gilded and nobody could catch her at it, while Caleb looked at the candles.

He did not need to be told which of the students were the Contis. There were clearly three of them, walking ahead of the other students, who kept a careful distance behind them, as one keeps back from a hearth that throws sparks. They were all tall and all dark, with black hair and black eyes and harsh, handsome faces that seemed to have been carved by the weather out of frigid ice. The one right in front was broad in the shoulder, handsome in the way of a carved door, with arrogant features. The one at his right elbow was slim and light-footed, and a year younger, with a laugh like a dropped tray, and he put his feet where the first one had put his.

“Rocco,” Cecily told the jug, “and Vittorio.”

The third Conti came after them, alone, with an empty yard of floor before him and another behind.

He wore no hat. His hair was black and swept back from a high pale brow, and the moon in the enchanted ceiling lay on it like frost on a slate roof. He was the tallest of the three and the handsomest, with a sharp nose, sharp cheekbones and a jawline that could cut. He walked with a slow, deliberate stride, his cloak open over his red robes as if he did not feel the cold. Behind him followed the rest of Durmstrang, and at their head a first-year in a fur cap three sizes too large, who was trotting to keep up with his eyes fixed on the third Conti’s back.

Halfway up the far side of the Hall, the Conti turned to look at Caleb.

He did not slow. There was nothing in his face at all, and his eyes were as black as his hair, and the look came across two empty tables and the whole width of the floor and settled on Caleb as if it had been sent for him and had found him at the first attempt.

Caleb met the look levelly, as he would have met any guest’s in his mother’s house, and gave him the faintest courteous inclination of the head, which was all that civility required and not a hair more. The look did not move on, as looks did. It stayed on him like the heat from an open oven door, and Caleb, who had never yet been the first to look away from anybody, dropped his eyes to the cloth. Then Rocco glanced back and spoke a word, and the boy turned his face away and went on to the Slytherin table with the others.

Caleb found that he had folded the corner of his napkin into a neat triangle, and then into a smaller one, and he unfolded it again and laid it flat.

He had been looked at all his life, in Paris and in Sologne, at the opera and at every ball since he was six, by duchesses and by footmen and by a great many young men and women who had then gone home and written poetry about it, some of which had been sent to him and some of which had been sent, by mistake, to Andrew. He knew every kind of look there was, the ones that admired and the ones that hoped and the ones that measured the Blanchemer pearls. He did not know this one. It had not wanted to be noticed, and it had not cared whether it was. It had simply been there, and taken what it came for, and gone.

He did not like it. He told himself so, firmly, and then, because he had been raised to be honest at least with himself, he admitted that he did not entirely dislike it either, and that this was a good deal more alarming.

The Durmstrang party took its places at the far end of the Slytherin table, by the opposite wall. There were two house tables between them, Gryffindor and Hufflepuff, and given the good deal of history between the Blanchemers and the Contis, Caleb suspected that someone had carefully measured that distance.

“Arian,” Cecily informed the jug, low and cool, because a Conti had looked at her brother before the whole Hall as if he had a right to, and she had not cared for it. “The younger son. Nobody knows anything of him for certain, only what is whispered. It is said that at fourteen he disarmed his entire year with a single charm, and gave the wands back a week later without a word to anyone. That is whispered. His marks in the Dark Arts are not. And he has just looked at you, Caleb, before three schools. I should very much like to know why.”

“He must not feel the cold,” Caleb observed. “The cloak must be decorative, then, since it is not properly worn. How incredibly casual!”

Cecily sniffed in disdainful agreement. “He is from Durmstrang, voyons. They are used to freezing weather. This is probably mild for them.” She turned the jug a quarter turn, so that the Slytherin table went out of it. “You acknowledged him.”

“I inclined my head, as civility requires. One inclines one’s head to anybody who passes one’s table, and you would have done as much yourself if you had not been so occupied with the jug.”

“Not to a Conti.”

“To anybody, Cecy. Need I remind you of our manners, ma chère sœur? I am courteous to everyone.”

“Yes,” Cecily retorted, “and that has always been the trouble with you.”

Hogwarts, which had stood out on the steps to see both its guests in, came in last, with cheeks stung red from the cold. The students quickly filled the benches in a rush of scarves and chatter, which quieted and became whispers the closer they came to the Durmstrang end of the Slytherin table. Two Gryffindor boys who had been shoving each other down the aisle stopped shoving as they passed it. A little Hufflepuff girl sat down, saw who was sitting at the next table, and stood up again and went to sit somewhere else.

Then the plates filled themselves in honour of the guests, with a bouillabaisse made by someone who had heard of bouillabaisse but never tried one, and coq au vin beside the steak-and-kidney pie, and for Durmstrang a borscht the colour of garnets, and pelmeni, and a dish of pickled herring that no one from Hogwarts or Beauxbatons would go near. All down the Beauxbatons end the seniors bent their heads over the bouillabaisse and began, with great politeness, to be disappointed in it.

Odile Marchand had not moved. The soup in front of her had stopped steaming.

Caleb took up the basket of bread, which was nearer to him than to anyone, and instead of helping himself he leaned past the two boys between them and held it out to her first.

“Tenez, Mademoiselle Marchand. You were quite right to stand.”

She lifted her head, startled, and went redder still.

“It is not every evening that a ship comes up out of a lake to dine with us,” Caleb went on, low enough that only their end of the table could hear, “and one of us ought to have risen to greet it. I would have done it myself, only I was afraid it would ask me to dance, and I had not been introduced.”

Odile laughed. It burst out of her before she could stop it, a high clear peal from the top of Cherubino’s register, and half the Beauxbatons end looked up, and at the head of the table the Headmistress turned her great head down the length of the cloth to see who had laughed at her table in a strange house.

She found Caleb.

He smiled at her. The smile he had given the steps had been for everybody and had cost him nothing. This was the one he kept for his mother when she came down late to breakfast, and it went up the table to the Headmistress with nothing held back, warm and glad and a little apologetic, as if they had shared a joke that the rest of the room had missed. The Headmistress considered him for a long moment. Then her mouth, which had not unbent since the Pyrenees, unbent, and she smiled back at him, and shook her head as one shakes it over a favourite nephew, and turned to the Hogwarts staff again.

All down the Beauxbatons end the shoulders came down. Somebody passed the bread. Odile took a roll from the basket and ate her cold soup with every sign of enjoying it, and the noise came back up the table louder and warmer than before, and spilled over the gap onto the Ravenclaws, until they too were laughing, though not one of them had heard the joke.

“You are impossible,” Cecily told him.

“I am hungry, Cecy. One cannot eat with a soprano in mourning three places away. It is bad for the digestion.”

Everyone in the Hall knew of the feud between the de Blanchemers and the von Contis. Half the Hall had read the piece in the Prophet, and the other half had been told about it by the first half at breakfast, with improvements. The nearest Ravenclaw was a lanky seventh-year with ink on his fingers, who sat across a yard of empty bench that nobody had told him to keep and that he kept all the same, and who had brought the cutting folded into the back of a book on goblin wars. Before the pie had gone round he leaned over the gap, very red, to ask whether it was true that Caleb’s grandfather had once turned the Conti grandfather into a hatstand at Lyon.

“An umbrella stand, monsieur,” Caleb corrected him gently, “and only for an afternoon.”

He said nothing more about it, and the Ravenclaw, despite being very curious, found that he was perfectly content with the answer, charmed as he was by Caleb’s smile. He was called Ambrose Fennick, and he told Caleb so, and told Cecily so as well, and then told her a great deal about the goblin rebellion of 1612, to which she listened with the expression of a woman who has been offered a dish she does not care for and is too well brought up to refuse.

After that it went as it generally went. By the time the plates were cleared, half the Ravenclaw table had found some reason to lean down towards the Beauxbatons end, and Caleb had been charming to every one of them. He asked after their houses and their lessons and their owls, and remembered the answers, and made each of them laugh at least once, and when they went back to their own end of the table not one of them could have said what Caleb de Blanchemer had told them in return. He was sunlight through a window, warm on everybody and the same on everybody, and nobody who sat in it ever quite found the latch. He was a de Blanchemer, and he was friendly to all the world and friends with none of it, because that was how a de Blanchemer was raised.

Cecily ate her bouillabaisse in silence and watched the Slytherin table.

The Durmstrang end was not quiet. Rocco sat with his chin on his clasped hands, chewing slowly and watching nothing but the two de Blanchemers, first at one and then at the other, as a man looks over a pair of horses he has been told are not for sale, and Vittorio turned his knife in the candlelight, idly, so that the light ran down the blade towards the Ravenclaw table.

Caleb continued with his supper, and ignored the poor attempt at intimidation. After a while Vittorio grew tired of it and put the knife down.

“And the uncle?” Caleb asked. “What is he called?”

Among the Hogwarts professors sat the Headmistress of Beauxbatons, and the Headmaster of Durmstrang in his silver furs, and at the end, between a witch in a green hat and a tiny wizard on three cushions, the thin dark man in black. He ate his soup with the patience of someone who had been served worse and would be served worse again. In the centre of the table, presiding over it all in robes the colour of a ripe plum, Professor Dumbledore was laughing at something the Headmistress had said.

“Enzo von Conti,” Cecily muttered. “Andrew says he is the only one of them with any sense.”

“That is a great deal of sense for one man to keep on behalf of a whole family.”

He glanced at the Durmstrang end of the Hall, at the last Conti, and found that the last Conti had not stopped looking at him, and had not, apparently, any intention of stopping. There was nothing cold in it. The face was all winter, but the look came across two house tables as warm as a hand laid on the back of his neck.

Caleb studied the bats in the rafters instead, and at the stars, and at the moon on the enchanted ceiling, and resolved that he would not look across the Hall again before the pudding. He had looked twice more before the pudding came.

*   *   *

The Durmstrang end of the Slytherin table was not quiet, and Arian had known it would not be.

It was never quiet where Rocco sat. Rocco talked. He talked low and easily in Russian, in the reasonable voice Dario had taught him, and everything he said was pleasant, and none of it was kind. He talked about the Hogwarts students as they filed in, which of them were Mudbloods by the look of their shoes and which of them would cry first if one spoke to them in the corridor. He talked about the food, and the old man in the plum robes, and the Headmistress of Beauxbatons, whom he called the giantess. He talked about the two de Blanchemers under the windows, and what Dario had said of their family in his last letter, and what their great-grandfather had looked like when he came out of Lyon with half his face burnt off by the fire their own house had made, which was in the family book with a drawing, and which Rocco had been trying to cast since he was fourteen, and he said it all smiling, with his chin on his hands, as if he were telling a story he was fond of.

He sat on Arian’s left, a little nearer than there was any need for, which was how Dario used to sit when he wished Arian to remember that he could. Vittorio sat across from them with his back to the wall and a heel of bread in his fist, and Mirek sat on Arian’s right, because Mirek always did.

Vittorio had found a use for the bread. He was tearing it into crumbs and flicking them, one at a time, at the first-year, and every time one landed in the boy’s hair or his ear or his soup, he laughed, and looked at Rocco to see whether Rocco had seen. Mirek went on eating with his face turned down and red. When the salt came round, Vittorio took it and emptied half of it into the boy’s borscht, carefully, while Mirek watched him do it and did not dare to stop him, and then he passed it on.

“Kushay,” Rocco suggested. “Eat it. It would be rude to the English to leave it.”

Mirek lifted his spoon.

Arian took the bowl from in front of him and set it down in front of himself, and put his own untouched bowl before the boy, and went on looking down the Hall.

Vittorio laughed, a little too loud. Rocco said nothing at all for a while, and then he began to talk about something else, in the same pleasant voice. That was how it was done in their family. One said a great deal, and all of it was meant to hurt, and what was not said was left to the silence, which had been in the family longer than any of them and knew its business.

Arian had eaten nothing. Everyone around him had fallen on the food as if they had been at sea for a month, which they had, and Arian sat with his hands under the table and watched the windows across the Hall.

A boy and a girl were seated under the windows among the Beauxbatons students, and despite never having seen them before, Arian recognised the refined, fine-boned features of aristocracy and knew them to be Blanchemers.

He would have known it without Rocco. He had grown up among the old houses of the north, where blood was a thing one could see across a ballroom, in the set of a head and the length of a hand, and these two had it as plainly as if it had been written over them in gold. They had the same face, the two of them, high-browed and clean and finely made, and the same curls, the brown of chestnuts when the husk has just split, and they sat side by side with the same straight backs. But they did not sit in the same way.

The girl was on guard. He saw it in the first minute. She held herself very still, and she did not turn her head towards the Slytherin table once, and yet she had not stopped watching it since the Durmstrang party came in. She watched it in the gold jug in front of her. He had seen her turn the jug a quarter turn, so that he went out of it. She would make a good enemy, and he thought she meant to be one.

The boy was not on guard at all.

He sat in the middle of the candlelight as if it had been lit for him, and perhaps it had. The candles had drifted that way, it seemed to Arian, as candles drift towards a door left open on a summer night, until they hung thicker over the Beauxbatons end than anywhere else in the Hall and the gold plates there shone like the inside of a church at Easter. The noise was louder there too, and kinder, and all of it was turned one way, towards him. People leaned in to talk to him. Ravenclaws came down their own table on one pretext after another and stood at his shoulder, and he turned to each of them as if he had been waiting all evening for that one face, and they went away again, lit up, like lamps that had been carried past a fire.

He had beautiful hands. Arian saw them when he broke his bread, long and quick and certain, and when he laid them still on the cloth they did not fidget, as the hands of the Hogwarts boys fidgeted. His curls had come a little loose from the wind on the steps and he had not troubled to put them right. He laughed, now and then, at something somebody said, but only a little, and with his mouth closed, as if the real laugh were being kept somewhere else, for somebody else, and Arian found that he wanted, with a want that went through him like a blade, to know where it was kept, and for whom.

Arian had seen him from the doors.

He had come up the Hall behind his cousins and looked once towards the windows, and for half a second his step had faltered on the flags. The boy had met his look and inclined his head to him, gravely, as one great house greets another. It was only manners, and meant for anybody, and Arian had carried it the rest of the way up the Hall all the same, like a coal carried across a winter yard in a cupped hand, and before he reached his own table he knew that he would not set it down again.

Now it had gone down under his ribs and caught, where nothing had burned for twelve years. His heart was going as it went before a duel, and under the table his hands had shut until the nails cut his palms. In front of him the water in his goblet began to skin over with ice from the rim inwards, in fine white feathers. He looked at it and made it stop, as he had been making things stop since he was six, and the ice went back into the water.

Then the boy smiled at the Headmistress.

It had been over in a moment. A girl down the Beauxbatons table had been sitting over her soup with her face scarlet, over something that had happened before Durmstrang came in, and the boy had leaned across with the bread and said something to her, and she had laughed. And the Headmistress had turned to see who dared, and the boy had smiled at her.

Arian knew every smile that was given to a Conti. There was the one that was afraid of him and the one that wanted something, and the one his father gave to men who were about to be sorry, and the one Dario gave him across the breakfast table on the mornings Dario had arranged something. He had been given all of them since he could walk, and he could tell them apart across a room as other men told wines apart, and he had believed there were no others.

He had never in all his life seen a smile that could light up a room.

The boy had smiled up the table at that great severe woman, and she had smiled back as if she could not help it. Then it had spread down the Beauxbatons end and across the gap and along the Ravenclaw table, like daylight coming down a valley one farm at a time, until half the Hall was warmer for it. He had gone back to his soup without the least idea that he had done anything.

“Look at him,” Rocco murmured in Russian.

Arian said nothing.

“The little prince of the white house holding court,” Vittorio sang under his breath, and laughed his tray-dropping laugh.

A Hogwarts girl had stopped at the boy’s shoulder to say something, a fair girl in a yellow scarf, and the boy looked up and smiled at her, and Arian watched her go scarlet from the collar to the hair. Then a Beauxbatons girl across the table from him, and another a little further down, and a Ravenclaw at the end with her book held up before her face, one after another, every time he turned that smile on them, as poppies open in a field when the sun comes out. Not one of them came an inch nearer for it. He gave it away to every one of them, and kept something back from every one of them, and Arian, who had kept everything back all his life because he had been obliged to, saw it, and understood it, and wanted with the whole of himself to be the one he did not keep it from.

After a while Rocco leaned in until his mouth was by Arian’s ear.

“Bratets. You are staring.”

Arian looked at the light.

Rocco smiled, and it was not kind. “You have always liked beautiful, useless things. Tell me,” he chuckled, “how do you think a Blanchemer breaks?”

He had said the first part of it before, in the stable yard at Chernoyar, when they were six. Arian had been kneeling in the straw with something warm cupped in his two hands, and Dario had found him there and taken it from him, and Rocco had stood at Dario’s elbow and said it, and laughed, and had been saying it in one way or another ever since.

Arian did not remember what had happened to the bird. He remembered his hands afterwards, empty in the straw. He remembered that he had not cried, because Dario had been watching to see whether he would. And he remembered that after that day he had never again let any of them see him care for anything, because whatever he cared for, Dario took, and whatever Dario took was not given back.

So he did not answer. He had learned in the stable yard what came of answering. He sat with his hands under the table through the rest of the meat and the clearing of the plates, and looked at the light, and let Rocco watch him do it, until the pudding rose up out of the gold plates and Rocco turned away to eat.

But the question stayed where Rocco had put it, by his ear, and went on asking itself in Rocco’s pleasant voice, and Arian heard it, and understood that Rocco had seen where he was looking, and that Rocco wrote to Dario.

There were towers of treacle tart and trifle, and at the far end of the Ravenclaw table a pyramid of macarons, and before the Durmstrang party a dark honey cake in a hundred thin layers that any cook at Chernoyar would have sniffed at. Arian cut his slice in two and slid the larger half onto Mirek’s plate, and Mirek looked at it, and at him, and ate it in four bites before Vittorio could see.

Then Professor Dumbledore got to his feet, and the Hall went quiet for him. He spoke of the Tournament, revived after long and well-deserved neglect, of three champions and three tasks and eternal glory, and of the danger, which he did not make light of, and which made the whole of Hogwarts lean forward on its benches as one. Then a caretaker came shuffling up the Hall with a jewelled casket, and Professor Dumbledore tapped its lid three times with his wand.

The Goblet of Fire was a plain thing. It was a great roughly hewn cup of wood, brimming over with flames of a white so blue and so cold that at the Ravenclaw table somebody said oh out loud. It would stand in the Entrance Hall from tonight, Professor Dumbledore explained, and any student who was of age might put a name into it until the Halloween feast, a week tomorrow, when it would choose. There would be no withdrawing once it had spoken. He would draw an Age Line around it himself, so that nobody under seventeen need trouble to try.

Every face in the Hall was turned up the room to the blue-white fire.

Arian looked at the windows.

The boy under them was studying the Goblet with his chin on his hand and his head a little on one side, as if it were a painting somebody had told him was very fine and he had not yet made up his mind about it. Then, as if he had felt it, he turned his head and met Arian’s eyes straight across the Hall, and for a moment neither of them looked away.

It was the boy who looked away first, down at his plate, and the colour came up along his cheekbones as Arian watched, faint and quick, like the first light on snow.

Arian stared down at his own hands.

At Durmstrang a boy was taught to go after what he wanted in the open, or not at all, and to be ashamed of nothing but failing to get it. It was the first thing they taught, before the curses and the drills, and Arian had believed it, as one believes what one has never had to test. He tested it now, sitting at that table, and found that it was true, and that it frightened him more than anything had frightened him since the stable yard.

*   *   *

The Beauxbatons party left the Hall first, as it had come in first, and Caleb went out with his sister on his arm and felt the look all the way down the Hall between his shoulders, like a draught from a door that somebody has left open behind one.

He did not turn around. He walked as he had been taught to walk out of a ballroom, without hurrying and without looking back, and he smiled at the Hufflepuffs as he passed, and they smiled back. On the steps the cold came up to meet him again, and he breathed it in gratefully, as if he had been too long in an overheated room.

“He is still looking,” Cecily remarked.

“How can you possibly know that?”

“The door knocker.”

“Mais non, Cecy, you cannot see the Slytherin table in a door knocker. It is a lion. It is facing the wrong way.”

“I do not need to see him. I can feel it on you. You are walking as you walked at the Duchesse de Morny’s, the night the Comte followed you around the conservatory with a camellia.”

“The Comte was twice my age.”

“And you walked just like this.” She steered him down the steps onto the frozen lawn, where the carriage glowed at the edge of the Forest with all its windows lit. “Andrew says we are not to speak to any of them.”

“I have not spoken to any of them. I inclined my head. One of them looked at me for the rest of the evening, which I could not prevent and did not invite, and which I shall mention to nobody, least of all Andrew, who would come up from Paris tonight in his dressing gown.”

Cecily was silent for a few steps, her breath going up white.

“Why was he looking at you?”

“I have not the least idea.”

That was not altogether true, and he did not like it. He had spent the whole of supper not liking it, in between the bouillabaisse and the Ravenclaws. Everything about the boy was winter, the still face and the stiller hands, the cloak worn open as if the cold were nothing to him, the slow deliberate stride. His gaze was not. It had lain on him all through supper as steady and as close as heat from a banked fire, and once or twice, when he had been unwise enough to meet it, it had burned. He had a notion what a look like that meant, from anybody else. From a von Conti it made no sense at all.

“If he means to look at me like that all year,” he decided, “I shall grow used to it, as one grows used to a portrait in a guest bedroom. By Christmas I shall not notice him at all.”

“You have never once in your life failed to notice a portrait in a guest bedroom. You talk to them.”

“Only to the ones who are lonely.”

The carriage door stood open at the top of its little steps, and warm light came down them onto the frost, and inside somebody had started the Snap game again. Caleb stopped at the bottom and gazed up at the castle.

It stood over them black against the stars, enormous, with its lit windows going up and up into the dark and its towers lost among the clouds, and somewhere inside it a staircase was still moving, because he could hear it, a long grinding groan of stone, very far off, like an old man turning over in his sleep. He thought of a hundred and twelve rooms at Blanchemer and how he had known every one of them by the time he was nine, and every cupboard, and every secret stair, and the loose stone behind the tapestry in the north gallery where the peacocks hid their eggs.

“How long will your bath be?”

Cecily eyed him.

“An hour and a half,” she replied. “Perhaps a little more. I have been in a carriage for three days, Caleb, and I intend to boil.”

“Then I shall not wait up. Bon bain, Cecy.”

“You will not be here to wait up,” said Cecily, and went up the steps ahead of him into the light.

*   *   *

Arian walked back down the lawn to the ship at his uncle’s side.

The rest of Durmstrang had gone on ahead in a long red line, with Rocco at the head of it and Vittorio at his elbow and Mirek trotting at the back, and the two of them came after it slowly, without lanterns, over the white grass. The frost was coming down hard. The lake lay black and still with the ship in it, and the stars stood in the water upside down around the hull, and away to the left the Beauxbatons carriage shone pale blue at the edge of the Forest with every one of its windows lit.

Enzo did not speak until they were halfway to the shore. When he did, it was in Russian, as everything between them was.

“You looked at the de Blanchemer boy for two hours.”

Arian did not answer. There was nothing to answer. It was true.

“Your cousin saw it,” Enzo went on, in the voice of a man reading out an inventory. “So did Vittorio, and the Headmaster, and in all likelihood the boy’s sister, who has, I am told, the eyes of a customs officer. Your cousin will write to your brother. I expect he has begun the letter in his head already.”

“Yes, Dyadya.”

“I do not say that you must not look.” Enzo walked on a few paces. His breath went up white in the starlight and was gone. “I am not your father, and in any case I have never in my life been able to stop you doing anything you had decided to do. I say only this. Your brother has never yet seen a thing you cared for without putting his hand on it. Be careful what you let him see.”

Arian looked across the lawn at the carriage.

One window had gone dim near the back, low down, where steam had begun to go up from under the eaves into the cold, white and slow. Another, near the front, still burned. As he watched, a shadow crossed it, somebody tall and slight, with a head of curls, who stopped at the glass for a moment and looked out at the castle, and then moved away.

Everything in Arian went towards that window. He felt it go, as a compass needle goes north when the box is opened, and he stood on the frozen grass and did not follow it.

“I will do nothing.”

His uncle regarded him for a long time in the dark.

“Good,” Enzo said at last, and turned for the ship. “That will be a great deal harder.”

My dear Caleb,

I have it from the Headmistress that the carriage stands within forty yards of the Forbidden Forest. I have written to her about it. You will not go into the Forest. You will not go down to the lake after dark, nor up the towers, nor into any part of that castle which is not lit, and I am informed that a great deal of it is not. You will keep to the carriage after supper with your sister, and you will both be in bed by ten. You will not speak to the Durmstrang party. As to the younger Conti, you are not to be in the same room as him, the Great Hall excepted, and I do not write that on account of Lyon. I have read the Institute’s returns. I come up with the judges on the thirty-first, and I shall expect to find you just as I left you.

You will wear your flannel.

— From a letter of M. Andrew de Blanchemer to his brother, Monday 24 October

*   *   *

The bath at the back of the Beauxbatons carriage had been charmed by a famous witch of the last century to be as deep as a pond and as hot as one could bear, and to smell, according to the taste of whoever was in it, of lime flowers or orange blossom or the sea. Cecily had been looking forward to it since the Pyrenees. She went in at a quarter to midnight with three towels, a book on the properties of moonstone and a cup of chocolate, and shut the door on the rest of the carriage in a manner that said it would not be opened again for anything less than a fire.

Caleb gave it a minute, then took his cloak from its peg and his wand from under his pillow, and let himself out.

The night had gone very still. The frost lay white over the whole lawn, and his boots left dark prints in it, and the castle went up in front of him into the stars with most of its windows dark now, and a few still lit, here and there, very high up, like candles left burning in a house where everyone else has gone to bed. He lit his wand and held it up, and stood a moment on the grass, looking up at it.

Andrew had forbidden the Forest and warned him about the staircases, but had said nothing whatsoever about exploring the castle after dark, which Caleb considered an oversight on his part. Not even Andrew could call it disobedience.

The great front doors had been left a hand’s breadth open. He slipped through them sideways, into the Entrance Hall, and stopped.

The Goblet was there. It stood alone on its stool in the middle of the flagstones, within the thin gold ring of the Age Line, and it burned blue-white and soundless in the dark, and its light went up the marble staircase and lay on the walls, so that the whole cold Hall seemed to be under water. Nobody was there. Nobody had yet put a name in it, or if they had, it had swallowed them without a sound.

Caleb went to the edge of the gold line and looked into the fire.

It was not like any fire he knew. It gave no heat and made no noise, and it moved slowly, as weed moves in a deep pool, and when he looked down into it long enough it seemed to him that it was looking back. He thought of Cecily’s square of parchment in its green ink, folded in her pocket since August, and of how she would walk across the line on Halloween morning with her chin up and drop it into this, and he cared for the thought a good deal less than he had in the carriage.

He was seventeen. The line would let him across. He put the toe of his boot to the gold, as one tries the water at the edge of a lake, and felt it prickle, warm and willing, and took the boot away again.

“Not tonight, madame,” he told the Goblet. “I have only come to pay my respects.”

The flames went on moving in their slow way and gave no sign that they had heard, and he left them to it and went up the marble staircase with the light going up the walls ahead of him.

The portraits slept in their frames with their chins on their chests and woke grumbling as the light went by, and Caleb stopped to read the little brass plates under each of them, so that he should know whom he had woken. Sir Gawen told him he was a puppy, and Lady Morwenna told him he had very good manners for a Frenchman and asked whether he was married, and a bald wizard with a quill behind his ear demanded to know whether he had a pass. Caleb said that he had not, and that he would be grateful to be told where one was obtained, and the wizard, who had plainly not been asked anything since the reign of the last king but one, was so delighted that he forgot to take his name.

On the second floor a step that had been perfectly solid a moment before was not there when he put his weight on it, and he went through it to the thigh and sat down hard on the step above, with one leg swallowed by the stair and his wand rolling away down the flight below him, lighting up the banisters as it went. He sat there in the dark for a while, quite stuck, and considered his position. Andrew had written that the staircases were not to be trusted. He would never tell Andrew that he had been right, because it would make his brother very happy and Caleb would have to live for the rest of his life being reminded of it.

“Madame,” he told the stair at last, with great civility, “I have the honour to be a guest in this house.”

The stair let him go. It did it with a kind of sulky reluctance, as a dog lets go of a slipper, and Caleb retrieved his wand from the landing below and went on up, keeping to the edges, and laughing quietly to himself all the way to the fourth floor. There a corridor ended in a door that would not open however politely he asked it, and when he turned around he found that the corridor behind him now went somewhere else.

“Bien,” Caleb conceded to the door, and went to find another.

He never found the towers that night. What he found instead was a corridor full of suits of armour that turned their helmets to watch him go by, and a classroom where somebody had left a cage of mice that had been turned into snuffboxes and not entirely turned back, and a window on the fifth floor from which he could see the whole of the lake with the moon on it and the black ship lying in the middle like an inkblot on a letter. He stood at that window a long while. He thought that he had never seen anything quite so lonely as that ship, and then he thought that it was a strange thing to think about a ship, and went on his way.

When he came back across the lawn, the frost had thickened on his footprints. The carriage was dark except for the long window at the back where the steam was still rising into the cold.

*   *   *

At a little before midnight, Arian had given up his hammock and gone up on deck.

He had not slept. He had lain in the dark with Mirek snoring in the next hammock and the hull creaking around him in the cold, and he had stared at the planks over his head and seen, with great clarity, a Hall full of candles and a boy under the windows smiling radiantly at the Headmistress of Beauxbatons. He saw the image even when he closed his eyes, so he had got up and climbed on to the deck.

Across the water the Hogwarts castle stood up black against the stars with a few windows still lit despite the late hour. At the edge of the Forest he saw the Beauxbatons carriage lamps being put out one by one, until only a single window still burned near the back, steaming as if a kettle had been left on.

Then the carriage door opened.

Somebody came down the steps with a lit wand held up, stood on the frozen grass a moment, looking up at the castle, before setting off across the lawn at a determined pace towards Hogwarts. The wandlight swung with the movement, and it lit up a head full of chestnut curls.

Arian’s hands closed tightly on the rail.

The gangway lay ten feet to his left. The light was going away across the lawn, getting smaller and further off, and in a minute it would be gone.

He stayed where he was.

Behind him, low down near the stern, Vittorio’s porthole stood open, as it always did, whatever the weather.

He did not know what he would do if he caught up. At Chernoyar there had always been food, clothes, and tutors, and a new sword on the rack before the old one was blunt, and he had never had to want any of it, not even the bird in the stable yard, which he had only been trying to keep alive. He wanted now, so hard that his hands ached on the rail, and he held on to it.

Under his hands the frost began to spread. It crept out from his fingers along the wet wood in fine white feathers, as it had gone into the water of his goblet at supper, down the rail on either side, then over the iron of the stanchions, until the whole of the rail was white and furred and glittering. He took his hands off the rail and put them inside his cloak. The frost stopped where it was, and stayed.

Then he watched the castle.

He would not follow the light tonight, but he could see it. It showed in the windows as it passed them, one and then the next, a moving spark in the black stone, going up. It went up the great staircase, which he could see through the high windows over the doors, and across, and up again, and then it was lost for a long time somewhere in the middle of the castle, and Arian stood on the frozen deck with his hands under his cloak and waited for it as he had been taught to wait for deer, without moving, for as long as it took. At a quarter past twelve it came out again on the fifth floor, in a long window that looked over the lake, and stopped there.

It remained in that window for a while. He could see the light, and the shadow of somebody standing behind it at the glass, looking out over the water towards the ship.

Whether the boy could see him he had no way to tell, and thought probably not. He stood on the deck in the dark with the frost on the rail beside him, and the boy stood in the window with the light in his hand, and for as long as it lasted there was nothing between them but a mile of black water and the cold.

Then the light moved away from the window and was gone.

He saw it again a little after one, coming down the great staircase, and across the Entrance Hall, and out through the doors onto the lawn, swinging. It went back across the grass the way it had come, slower now, and at the carriage steps it stopped. Then the carriage door opened, and the light went in, and was put out.

A little after that, the steam stopped rising from the back window.

Arian stood on the deck until his hands had gone numb inside his cloak and the stars had turned a little further around the sky over the Forest. Then he went below deck to his hammock, and lay there with his eyes open.

Andrew’s letter came down with the owls at breakfast, in the middle of the porridge, and landed in Caleb’s plate.

He read it with great attention, and then with his eyebrows raised, and then aloud, to his sister, in a low voice, so that the Ravenclaws should not hear.

“‘You will not go down to the lake after dark, nor up the towers, nor into any part of that castle which is not lit.’” He looked up. “He has been here before, I think. He knows a great deal about it.”

“He has a plan of the castle grounds,” replied Cecily, “from the Ministry archive. He showed it to Papa. It is on his desk in Paris with all the dark parts shaded in.” She buttered her toast. “You were in one of the dark parts last night.”

“I was in several. They were very well kept. I shall write and tell him so.”

“You will do no such thing.”

“Mais non,” Caleb agreed, “I shall tell him I was in bed by ten, which is a kindness, and costs me nothing, and he will sleep tonight for the first time since we left.” Cecily scoffed at her brother, knowing that Andrew would in fact be kept up at night instead because Caleb never did as he was told.

He folded the letter, and reached for the toast, and could not quite attend to it, since across the Hall, at the Durmstrang end of the Slytherin table, Arian von Conti was staring right at him.

The night before he had looked over a long empty floor, from the middle of a procession. This morning he sat at his table with a cup of something black in his hand and his elbows on the cloth, and watched Caleb down the Hall over the rim of it, openly, as if there were nothing between them but air. When their eyes met he held Caleb’s without so much as a blink, and he was still holding them when Caleb gave up first. Caleb turned to his toast, to his sister, to the enchanted ceiling, which was grey that morning and spitting with ghostly rain that never came down, and each time he glanced back across the Hall, the Conti was still staring right at him.

Caleb found that he had buttered the same piece of toast three times.

“He is doing it again,” Cecily informed the jug.

“I am aware of it.”

“You are buttering your toast as if it had wronged you.”

“I am buttering it as if it had been insufficiently buttered, which it was.” He laid down his knife. “He might at least blink, Cecy. It is very wearing, to be looked at by somebody who does not blink. One begins to feel like a painting in a gallery with a very dull guard.”

His sister was quiet for a moment, turning the jug. In the curve of it the Slytherin table lay bent and gilded, and in the middle of the red at the end of it there was one figure that did not move.

“I do not know what he means by it,” she admitted at last, and he could hear that she disliked admitting it. “Perhaps he wishes to frighten us. They are said to be fond of it. The cousin has not taken his eyes off us since we sat down either, and I like his looking at us a great deal less.” She set the jug straight. “Or perhaps he is simply rude. They have no manners in the north. Grand-mère says they eat with their knives.”

“He did not eat with his knife. He did not eat at all.”

“You were watching him eat?”

“I was watching him not eat. It is not at all the same.”

She gave him a long look over her toast.

“He is first in the Dark Arts, Caleb,” she said. “Andrew did not put that in a letter for his own amusement. Whatever that boy is looking at you for, it is not your conversation, and I do not care to find out by accident what it is. I am going to write to Matilda.”

“You will bring an Auror across the Channel for a boy who does not blink?”

“I shall send her a letter. She may decide about the Channel herself.”

At the far end of the Ravenclaw table two seventh-years had got up and were walking, with great casualness, out of the Hall towards the Goblet, with slips of parchment in their hands. A little crowd went after them to see. Cecily watched them go, and her hand went to the pocket of her robe, where the green ink was.

“You are not going to put yours in now?” Caleb asked.

“Certainly not, voyons. Anybody may put a name into a cup on a Monday morning between the porridge and the post.” She stood and shook out her skirts. “I shall put mine in on Halloween morning, before the feast, when everyone is there to see it done. If one is going to do a thing, Caleb, one may as well be seen to do it properly.”

She left, having finished her breakfast. Caleb sat for a moment over his toast and Andrew’s letter, and then, because he could not help it, glanced across the Hall once more.

Arian von Conti had not moved. He was still staring, and as Caleb watched him he raised the black cup a little towards him, unhurried, as a man raises his glass in a toast to someone across a crowded room, and drank.

Caleb stared at the ceiling for some time after that.

He knew very well what it meant when a young man lifted his glass to one across a room. It had been done to him at a dozen suppers in Paris, by a dozen young men who had afterwards sent flowers, and he had smiled at every one of them and sent the flowers on to Maman. It had never before been done by a von Conti, in a cup of black coffee, at a quarter past eight in the morning, in front of three schools. And it had never once before made him forget to drink his chocolate.

He could have drawn that face with his eyes shut, down to the way the black hair was pushed back from the brow.

The boy had come in to breakfast with his sister on his arm and a letter in his hand, and Arian had looked up from his coffee and not looked anywhere else since.

He had told Enzo on the lawn that he would do nothing, and he had done nothing all night at a frozen rail. This morning he looked, and did not trouble to pretend that he was doing anything else.

“Nu. I thought I would not wait for the end of the week,” Rocco remarked pleasantly, and dipped his pen. “He does so like to hear things while they are fresh.”

Vittorio laughed.

He heard Rocco’s pen stop. Then it went on again, a little faster.

Arian went up the marble staircase.

He kept the length of a ballroom between them, because the stair was wide and the morning was full of people and he did not yet know what else to do with the distance. The boy went up to the first floor, and along, and up again. He walked as he had walked out of the Hall the night before, without hurrying and without looking back. He kept his eyes ahead on the first flight, and on the second.

At the turn of the third he stopped, and leaned on the banister, and looked down.

Arian stopped too, on the landing below, in the white light from a high window, and looked up.

For a moment neither of them did anything at all. Arian went on looking. He held the look, and went on holding it, up the whole height of the stairwell, until the boy’s colour rose and it was the boy who broke it. He turned and went up the stairs, a little slower than before.

What it meant, Arian had no idea, but he followed it all the same.

By Tuesday the whole of Beauxbatons knew, and by Tuesday luncheon so did a good part of Hogwarts, that Arian von Conti, second son of the Conti head, was following Caleb de Blanchemer, second son of the Blanchemer head.

He was simply there, at the foot of the library stair when Caleb went up it and at the top when Caleb came down, at the end of the corridor of armour, across the fountain courtyard, and at the doors of the Great Hall whenever Beauxbatons went in to a meal or came out of one. On Tuesday, at seven in the morning, Caleb opened the carriage door and found him standing on the frozen grass at the foot of the steps, where no von Conti had ever set foot, as if the lawn were his own. He never spoke. He kept the length of a ballroom between them, mostly, and when he chose to close it he did not ask leave. He made no secret of it either. In broad daylight, with half the school going by, he followed Caleb de Blanchemer about Hogwarts and watched him, as calmly as another boy might walk to his lessons. Each afternoon he vanished for two hours, which Ambrose Fennick said were the hours the Durmstrang ship kept its lessons, and at three he was back without fail, at the end of whatever corridor Caleb happened to be in.

Andrew said they were not to speak to any of them. Caleb had decided that he would obey Andrew in this one thing.

On Tuesday morning he found a window seat on the fifth floor with a view of the lake, and a book of Ronsard in his pocket, and a portrait on the wall beside the window of two old sisters in lace caps taking tea, who were delighted to have company. Arian von Conti was at the far end of the corridor. Caleb gave him not so much as a glance. He settled himself in the window with his back against the stone and his boots up on the cushion, and inclined his head to the two old ladies, and asked whether they cared for poetry.

They declared that they doted on it.

“Mignonne, allons voir si la rose,” Caleb read to them, low and clear, in the voice in which his mother read aloud in the long gallery at Blanchemer on wet afternoons, “qui ce matin avait déclose sa robe de pourpre au soleil…”

He read the whole of it. He read it well, which he knew, because he had been reading it to Maman since he was nine, and he gave every line its weight and let the last one go quietly, as one sets down a glass. He kept his eyes on the page, and had no need to lift them. At the end of the first stanza the step at the far end of the corridor began to come down it, unhurried, and at the end of the second it was halfway, and by the last line it had stopped on the other side of the window, against the wall, an arm’s length from Caleb’s boots, where nobody had asked it to be. The two old ladies in lace caps sat with their teacups halfway to their mouths. At the end the elder put hers down altogether.

“Oh, my dear,” she breathed. “Do you know what it is about?”

“A rose, madame.” Caleb did not lower his voice. “The poet takes a young lady into the garden to see it. This morning it was in full bloom, and by evening it has dropped every petal, and he tells her that her beauty is the same, and that she had better gather her youth while she has it.” He turned the page. “He is asking her, very prettily, not to keep him waiting.”

“And does he know what it is about?” The younger one tipped her lace cap at the red robes standing against the wall on the other side of the window. “Your young man?”

“He is not mine, madame. And I do not believe he has a word of French.”

“He has a great deal of English,” the elder one said, with enormous satisfaction, “and you have just given him every word of it. You are a very wicked boy.”

Caleb did not answer, because she was perfectly right, and he had known it before he opened the book. He had known it with every line. He had felt the look come down the corridor towards him a stanza at a time, and he had read the poem into it as one might sing into a well, to hear what echoed back.

But nothing did. The boy against the wall said nothing, and kept his eyes on Caleb through every breath. When Caleb looked up at last, only for a moment, over the top of the page, he found that Arian von Conti was standing near enough to touch, and that he was looking at Caleb’s mouth.

Caleb shut the book.

He had gone hot to the ears, which was absurd, since it was his own fault, and the old ladies in lace caps had seen it, and the younger one had begun to giggle into her tea.

“Mesdames,” he said with dignity, “I thank you for your company,” and he went off down the corridor in the other direction, and down two flights of stairs, at a pace that was very nearly a run, and spared no glance behind him to see whether he was followed, because he knew.

On Tuesday night Arian did not stay on the deck.

He was at the rail at half past eleven with his cloak on, and at a quarter to twelve the carriage door opened and the light came down onto the frost and stood a moment looking up at the castle, and set off across the lawn. Arian went down the gangway after it.

He did not creep. He walked up the white lawn in the open, a long way behind, with the frost loud under his boots, and halfway up the lawn the light stopped and turned around, and held still, and Arian stopped too, and for a moment they stood like that, the two of them, with the whole frozen width of the grass between them and the castle going up into the stars. Then the light turned back to the castle and went on, not hurrying, and Arian went after it.

The great front doors stood a little open, as the boy had left them. Inside, the Entrance Hall was dark except for the Goblet. It stood alone on its stool within the gold ring of the Age Line, burning blue-white and soundless, and the boy from Beauxbatons was standing before it with his wand lowered, looking into the fire.

Arian stopped in the doors.

The boy knew he was there. He kept his eyes on the cup, but he knew, and he stood there for a long time all the same. He stayed outside the gold line, and put nothing into the cup. He only gazed into it, and the blue light lay over his face and his curls and his still hands, and made him seem like something carved out of ice and set there to keep watch, and Arian, in the doors, watched him and did not breathe. He had never seen anybody look at a fire like that. The boy looked at it as if it had asked him a question, and he was not yet sure of the answer, and did not mean to be hurried.

Then the boy bowed to the Goblet. He did it gravely, from the waist, as one bows to a great-aunt at the end of a long dinner, who may or may not be awake. Then he turned and went up the marble staircase two steps at a time, with his light running up the marble before him, and Arian went after him.

The portraits woke grumbling again as the light went by, and the boy apologised to each of them in turn, by name. Forgive me, Lady Morwenna. A thousand pardons, Sir Gawen. Good evening again, monsieur, I have still not obtained a pass. A bald wizard with a quill behind his ear laughed out loud at that, and called after him that he was a scamp, and went on chuckling to himself after the light had gone by, so that when Arian came past him he was still wiping his eyes, and did not notice him at all.

The staircases had not gone to sleep. On the third floor one of them swung away from its landing the moment the boy set foot on it, with a long groan of stone, and bore him off across the dark well of the stairs, and left Arian on the landing below with nothing in front of him but the drop. The boy rode it standing quite straight with one hand on the rail, and looked back once across the gulf at Arian, and laughed under his breath.

Arian heard the laugh come down the well of the stairs to him, quiet and delighted, and stood still on the landing with his hand on the cold stone.

He had heard the boy laugh at supper, closed and pleasant and given out evenly, like coins at a church door. This one was low and helpless and his own, and it had got out of him because the castle had done something absurd and he had forgotten, for as long as it took a staircase to swing, to keep it back. Arian stood in the dark and listened to it go up into the stairwell and thought that he had found where the real one was kept.

He found another stair, and another, and went on climbing.

He kept a flight below the light, and then less than a flight. He did not try to hide in the dark. On the fourth floor a stout witch with her hair twisted up in curl papers for the night leaned out of her frame to whisper something to the boy, and when Arian passed beneath her, a long while after, she pursed her lips at him and drew her curtain.

On the seventh floor the boy stopped before a tapestry in which a wizard in a nightcap was teaching a row of trolls to dance, and watched the lesson to its end with his head tilted to one side, and applauded softly with the tips of his fingers, to which the largest troll lowered its club and curtsied. Arian, at the far end of the corridor, watched the troll curtsy, and then watched the boy laugh at it, and did not look at the troll again.

Then the boy found the tower.

It was the tallest of them, and the stair went up inside it in a tight spiral of worn stone, around and around, past arrow slits full of stars. At the top the stair came out under the open sky, and the boy went through the door onto the leads, and Arian stopped three steps below it, where the wind came down to him.

The boy had put out his wand. He stood at the parapet with the whole night around him, the mountains and the stars and the black lake with the ship lying in it, and the moon on his hair, and after a while he began to sing. When he lifted his chin to the stars the moonlight ran down the long line of his throat into his collar, and the wind had got under the curls at the nape of his neck, and Arian found that he wanted to put his mouth there. He had no name for what that was. His body did. It knew so plainly that his hand shut on the cold stone, and his breath came short in the dark, and he could not make either of them stop.

The boy sang under his breath, in French, something with a great many verses that went up and down like a song for rocking a cradle. It was the kind of thing one sang to oneself in an empty room, to be sure one still had all the words, and he went through it carefully, verse after verse, stopping once to go back and correct a line, and going on. The words meant nothing to Arian. His French went no further than what one was taught for dinners. He stood on the stair and listened, and it seemed to him that the song went into him somewhere under the ribs, where the coal from the Hall was, and settled there.

He stayed below the last three steps. If he went up, the boy would stop singing, and Arian would have stood on that stair until the sun came up over the mountains sooner than make him stop.

When the song was finished the boy stood a moment longer at the parapet, and then lit his wand again and turned from the stars. Arian went down ahead of him to the landing below, where the spiral opened into an arch, and stood in the mouth of the arch with his shoulder to the stone, and stayed there. He left the boy the half of it that he was not standing in, and not an inch more.

The light came down around the turn towards him. It came across the landing. It went over the toes of Arian’s boots, and up, over the fur and the red, to his face, and stopped there.

The boy had stopped too. He stood on the last step with his wand held low, and the light came up from it under his chin and lay in his eyes, and the two of them looked at each other across a yard of cold stone, and the boy saw how much of the arch he had been left, and did not step back. Then he came down off the step and turned his shoulder and came through it, so near that Arian could have put out his hand and touched the curls at his temple. He heard the boy’s breath catch as he passed.

“Bonne nuit, monsieur,” the boy whispered, as he continued on his way down.

Arian stood in the archway until the light had gone down the stairs and around the turn and out of the world, and for some time after.

Caleb went down the next flight at the same pace he had come up it, which took a great deal of effort.

He had known the whole way up that there was a von Conti two flights below him. He had known it at the Goblet and on the swinging stair, where he had looked back across the drop at the boy left standing on the landing and laughed, which had been unkind and which he did not regret in the least. He had known it on the tower, with his back to the door and the whole of the night in front of him. He had sung all the same. He had told himself as he began that he had forgotten the stair, and by the second verse he knew he had not, and by the fourth he had stopped telling himself anything.

And then the boy had been in the arch, with the light on his face, and had not moved, and had left Caleb half an arch to get by in and not an inch more, and Caleb had walked past him close enough to feel the heat that came off him across the cold of the landing, as one feels the sun on one’s face with one’s eyes shut, and his own breath had gone out of him in a way that he had not at all intended.

He went down the next flight, and the next. On the fourth floor the witch in curl papers was waiting for him at the front of her frame with her hands clasped under her chin.

“Still all in one piece, dear?” she whispered.

“Entirely, madame. You were kind to ask.”

“I asked you on the way up whether I ought to scream,” she reminded him. “You said not. I have been sitting here worrying myself into a fit.” She leaned out so far that her curl papers trembled. “He went up after you. I saw him. Tall, and all in red, and a face on him like a tombstone. I drew my curtain on him, I hope you don’t mind.”

“Not at all, madame. I am obliged to you.” His voice was perfectly steady, which pleased him. “He has been following me since breakfast yesterday.”

“And you let him?”

“I have not yet found the means to stop him, madame.”

He thanked her again, and bowed.

The marble staircase went down in front of him into the blue glow of the Entrance Hall. He looked straight before him, whether crossing the Hall, or slipping out through the great doors, or crossing the white lawn under the stars with the frost crunching under his boots and his breath going up in front of him in a long white plume. He knew without looking that the boy had come out of the castle behind him, and that he was standing at the top of the steps in the dark, and that he would stand there until the carriage door had shut. He found, which surprised him a good deal, that he was not frightened, or not only frightened. His heart was going fast and light, as it did in the forest at Sologne when the hounds had found. The cold air tasted sweet in his mouth, and he felt giddy with it, as if he had drunk champagne too fast, and was very nearly laughing.

Bien, he thought, at the carriage steps. He follows me up seven floors to hear me sing, and he will not say one word to me. We shall see how long that lasts.

Cecily was asleep when he came in, with her book on moonstone open on her face. He took it off her and marked her place and blew out her candle, and lay down in the bottom berth in the dark, and heard the carriage clock strike one, and then two.

The next morning, before breakfast, Caleb went up to the greenhouses to see what Hogwarts grew for its hospital wing.

It was Wednesday, wet and grey, with the mist lying on the lake so thick that the ship had disappeared into it altogether and only the tops of its masts stood up out of the white. The greenhouses were warm and smelled of earth and dung and something sweet that he could not put a name to. He was alone among the Mandrakes, reading the labels on the dittany and wondering whether the woman who kept them would let him have a cutting, when Rocco von Conti came up the path from the lake on his way to breakfast with the child in the fur cap at his heels.

Through the fogged glass Caleb could not hear them, and so he watched it as one watches a dumb show from the back of a theatre. Rocco talked easily, with one hand in his pocket, as a young man talks to a cousin he is fond of. The child carried two satchels, his own and Rocco’s, and on the frozen path he slipped, and one of them went into the mud.

Rocco took his wand out of his pocket and touched it to the back of the child’s bare hand, lightly, as one taps a man on the shoulder to tell him he has dropped a glove. The child’s whole arm jerked. Rocco said something, kindly, and touched the hand again in the same place, and then a third time, talking all the while, until the child had learned to stand quite still under it. Then Rocco laughed, warm and pleased. Rocco put his wand away and continued walking.

Caleb stood among the Mandrakes and found that he had crushed a leaf of dittany in his fist.

Then he went out to the child. The boy had not moved. He was standing on the path in the mist with the muddy satchel at his feet and his hand held out in front of him, a little away from his body, as if it belonged to somebody else, and he was not crying. That was the worst of it, Caleb thought afterwards. He had plainly been taught not to.

Caleb knelt on the wet path in front of him, which his governess would certainly have disapproved of, and took the child’s hand. It had come up in three red welts in a row, neat as the marks a ruler leaves, and they were already beginning to blister at the edges. Caleb knew it for the shallow end of something Grand-mère had once shown him in the stillroom with the door shut, done lightly, by a hand that knew how to do it hard. He touched his wand to them with four words of the old French, and the light came out of it pale and cool and sank into the skin, and the welts sank with it and were gone.

“Voilà,” Caleb murmured.

The child stared at him. He looked at his hand, and turned it over, and turned it back, and looked at Caleb again with his mouth open, and said something in a language Caleb did not know.

Caleb laid a finger to his lips.

The boy understood that, at least. He nodded fast, and picked up both satchels, the muddy one and his own, and ran up the path into the mist after the older Conti cousin without once looking back. Caleb stayed kneeling where he was until the fur cap had gone, and then he got up and went back in among the Mandrakes, and stood there for some time, with mud on the knees of his robes, and thought of a square of parchment in green ink.

He thought of Cecily walking across the gold line on Halloween morning with her chin up.

He thought of that laugh, warm and pleased as it burned a child’s hand.

The boy never came in to breakfast.

Arian had come up the lawn ahead of the others, as he had on Tuesday, and stood at the doors of the Great Hall in the cold of the Entrance Hall to see Beauxbatons come in, and Beauxbatons came in, two by two in their pale blue silk, and the sister was among them with her chin up and her arm through another girl’s. The boy was not.

Arian remained by the doors. He did not know where the boy had gone, and the not knowing was a thing he had not felt before and did not care for, as if a step he had counted on had not been there.

He had decided to seek out the boy instead of waiting when Mirek entered the Hall alone, carrying two satchels and with mud to his shins.

He held out his right hand to Arian and whispered in Russian, “Smotri. Rocco burned my hand this morning for dropping his satchel, but the Blanchemer prince saw and healed me.” He turned his hand over and back to show Arian.

Arian stared at Mirek’s hand.

He knew what it ought to have looked like. He had seen Rocco’s three marks on other hands, on the ship and at Chernoyar, and he knew how long they took to go and that they left a shine on the skin when they did. There was no shine. The hand was completely healed.

He thought of the boy’s hands breaking bread under the candles, long and quick and certain.

“Go and eat, malysh,” he told Mirek.

Mirek went. At the Durmstrang end of the Slytherin table Rocco had seen the exchange from across the Hall, at once, as Rocco saw everything, and he looked from it to the empty place under the windows, and to Arian at the doors, and smiled, and took out his writing case again.

Arian met his gaze evenly.

He stood at the doors of the Great Hall and looked down its length at his cousin until Rocco found something in the writing case that needed his attention. Then the doors opened at Arian’s back, and the boy came past him into the Hall close enough to touch. He did not glance at Arian as he passed.

Arian followed after him into the Hall.

*   *   *

He was at the corner of the cloister on the Wednesday morning, when Caleb crossed the courtyard after breakfast with Ronsard under his arm, and when Caleb stopped, so did the step behind him, and when he turned, Arian von Conti was standing at the corner of the cloister, watching him quite openly. He was on the library stair on Thursday morning, three flights down, leaning on the banister with his arms folded, looking up at Caleb.

This time the cousin was also there.

Caleb saw him from the top, looking down over the rail. Two flights below Arian, on a landing of his own, the cousin Rocco stood with his hands in his pockets and his face turned up the well of the stairs, smiling, watching the two of them as a man at a fair watches a pair of dancing bears. His eyes went to Arian, and up past Arian to Caleb, and his smile was warm and easy and pleased, and Caleb thought of a wand going down three times on the back of a child’s hand and felt the cold of the banister come up through his glove.

Then Arian turned his head.

He turned nothing but his head, and looked down the well of the stairs at his cousin, and Caleb could not see his face, only the back of the black head and the set of the shoulders under the fur. Whatever was in the face, Rocco saw it. He stopped smiling. He stood a moment longer, and then he took his hands out of his pockets and went away down the stairs, not quickly, and not looking back, and the landing was empty.

Arian turned his head back, and looked up at Caleb again, as if nothing whatever had happened.

Caleb went on up to the library and sat down at a table in the window and opened his book, and read the same line of Ronsard until it had stopped meaning anything at all.

On Thursday afternoon, once the ship had let its pupils go, it came on to rain, and Caleb went down to the boathouse for no better reason than that he had not yet seen it. Where the path narrowed under the dripping trees, the von Conti was standing in the middle of it, in the rain.

He kept his place in the middle of the path. People stepped aside for a de Blanchemer. They had done it on every path and staircase of Caleb’s life, without his ever having to ask. Caleb went past him by what was left of the path, which was not much, with the wet fur a hand’s breadth from his sleeve, and said nothing, because Andrew had said he was not to. An hour later, when Caleb came back, the von Conti had not moved. He stood just where Caleb had left him, as if he had been planted there, with the rain running off the fur of his cloak and his black hair plastered to his brow, and he watched Caleb come up the path as if the rain were falling on somebody else.

He thought about it all that evening in the bottom berth, with his hands behind his head and his sister reading above him. He was not a fool. He had known what that look meant since the first night in the Hall, and if he had not known it then he would have known it in the corridor on the fifth floor, when he had looked up over Ronsard and found it on his mouth. He knew what it was to be wanted. What he did not know was what one did with being wanted by a Conti, who did not send flowers, verses, or anything at all, and who did not speak.

He thought of telling Cecily. But she would tell Andrew, and Andrew would come up from Paris on the next Portkey and have the whole of Durmstrang sent home, and Caleb would be followed for the rest of the year by a Ministry clerk instead, which would be a great deal of bother and not nearly so interesting. He thought of going to the Headmistress, but one did not complain to one’s Headmistress about a Conti who was simply looking. His family had been looked at by the Contis for two hundred years and had never yet complained of it to anybody.

Besides, he had begun, against every sensible thing he had ever been told, to enjoy it.

On Thursday night he decided to see what the boy would do if he lost him.

Cecily had gone to bed at ten with a headache and the curtain drawn. Caleb gave her half an hour, and took his cloak and his wand and went out across the lawn. He had no need to look around. Halfway up the lawn he heard the frost break under boots that were not his own. He went up through the dark castle by his own ways, the portraits and the swinging stair and the corridor of armour, at his ordinary pace, with the step two flights behind him all the way. On the sixth floor there was a long gallery with a suit of armour at the far end, very large and very rusty, standing in a deep alcove with a tapestry beside it. Caleb had found it on the first night. He walked down the gallery with his wand lit, turned the corner past the armour, put out the light, and stepped back into the alcove behind it, between the cold iron and the wall, and waited.

It was very dark. It smelled of dust and old metal, and something in the armour creaked faintly as it breathed, if armour breathed. Caleb stood with his back against the stone and his wand in his hand and his heart going hard, and listened.

For a long time there was nothing at all.

Then, at the other end of the gallery, a step. One, and another, coming down the long floor in the dark, nearer and then faster, almost to the corner. Then it stopped.

Caleb held his breath.

The step came no closer and went no further off. It simply stopped, perhaps a dozen yards away, around the corner, out of sight, and did not stir. There was no sound in the gallery at all except for the armour creaking and Caleb’s own heart, which seemed to him to be making a great deal more noise than was reasonable. He waited. He counted fifty. He counted a hundred. The other stayed where it was.

He knows, Caleb thought. He knows I am here, and he is waiting for me to come out.

Then he thought of Rocco’s smile on the library stair that morning, and wondered for one long moment whether the step around the corner was the one he had supposed. The dark around him felt very cold.

Then he thought of the two of them, standing on either side of a corner in the middle of the night, each waiting with enormous patience for the other to give way first, like two cats on a garden wall, and of Andrew’s plan of the castle on his desk in Paris with all the dark parts shaded in, and the laugh came up in him before he could stop it. He put his hand over his mouth. It came out all the same, low and helpless, between his fingers, and carried down the gallery in the dark.

*   *   *

Arian heard it.

He had lost the light at the corner. One moment it had been there ahead of him, at the end of the long gallery, going around past the great suit of armour, and the next it had gone out, and the gallery was black from end to end, and there was nothing.

He had stopped as if he had been struck.

For a moment he could not think at all. He stood in the dark with his heart going like a drum and every part of him gone cold and sharp, and he thought of Rocco. He thought of Rocco’s wand, and the back of Mirek’s hand, and Rocco on the landing that morning, smiling up the stairs. He thought of the step he had heard on the swinging stair behind him tonight, twice, a long way back, which might have been the castle and might not, and of every turn of that castle where somebody might be waiting in the dark with a wand, and of the light that had been there and was not, and the fear came up through him so fast that the stones under his boots went white with frost.

Then the boy laughed.

It came around the corner to him through the dark, low and smothered and helpless, the laugh from the stairwell, the one he kept for himself. It came from very close. It came from behind the armour.

Arian stood where he was and felt the fear go out of him all at once, as water goes out of a basin, and something else come into its place that was hot, and foolish, and went through him from his face to his boots. The boy was there behind the armour, perfectly safe, and laughing at him. He had put out his light to see what Arian would do. Arian knew what he would do. He would go around that corner, and into that alcove, and put a hand on the wall on either side of the boy’s head, so that the boy could laugh at him from a great deal closer, and he did not know, in the dark, with the heat in him and that laugh in his ears, what would come after.

He had taken three strides towards the corner when he heard the other step behind him.

It was at the far end of the gallery, where he had come in. It was light, and quick, and it stopped when he stopped, a breath too late, like the step of a boy who has been told to follow and has not been told what to do if he is caught. Arian knew it. He had heard it behind him in the corridors of Chernoyar since he was five years old, coming after Rocco’s.

He turned around.

He walked back down the gallery towards it, without hurrying, and without lighting his wand, and he had no need of light to know that Vittorio had flattened himself against the panelling at the end of it as if the wall might take him in. Arian stopped in front of him. He did not touch him. None of them ever touched one another. He stood there in the dark, a foot away, and let the cold come off him, and heard Vittorio’s breath begin to shake.

“Go back to the ship,” Arian told him in Russian.

Vittorio went. Arian went after him, down six flights and across the Entrance Hall and out onto the lawn and all the way down to the gangway, a pace behind him the whole way, so that whatever Rocco had sent into the castle that night after the boy should come out of it again in front of Arian, and go nowhere else.

On the sixth floor, behind the armour, the boy was alone, and safe, and still laughing. Arian heard it, or thought he did, all the way down the lawn.

*   *   *

Caleb waited behind the armour for a long while after. Nothing came around the corner. When at last he lit his wand and stepped out into the gallery, it was empty, and there was nothing on the floor but dust and, a dozen yards along, a round patch of frost on the flagstones, the size of a cartwheel, glittering in the wandlight, which was melting as he watched.

He crouched and touched it. It was very cold.

Further along, at the far end of the gallery where he had come in, there were other marks in the dust, two sets of boots, one going away fast and the other after it, at its heels.

He went back to the carriage the long way, by the swinging stair and the portraits, at his ordinary pace. Nobody followed him. He listened for the step on every flight, and it did not come, and the castle was quiet.

He told himself that he was relieved, and was so put out with himself for not believing it that he did not say good night to the swinging stair at all.

*   *   *

On the Friday morning a letter came for Rocco by eagle owl, at breakfast in the Great Hall, before Beauxbatons had come up from the carriage, and Rocco read it aloud.

He did it as he always did, in Russian, slowly, with his elbows on the table and the letter held up before him in both hands, so that everybody at the Durmstrang end of the Slytherin table could hear every word of it in Dario’s hand, and nobody else in the Hall could understand any of it. The Headmaster, who took his breakfast with his own school and left the staff table to the English, went on eating his kasha. Enzo always ate breakfast early, and was not at the table. Vittorio did not lean on his fist and grin, as he generally did. He sat at the far end of the bench with his eyes on his bowl.

“‘The Prophet says the old fool has put you all in the Great Hall together, with the English between,’” Rocco read. “‘How very civilised. You will put your name in, of course, cousin. Your father expects it.’” Rocco read that part a second time, for the pleasure of it. “‘So the white house has sent its two youngest, and you write that my little brother cannot take his eyes from one of them, and follows it about the castle like a dog at heel. Tell me which of them is the softer. I should like to know which one my little brother has been looking at.’”

He lowered the letter, and smiled down the table at Arian.

“I wrote to him on Monday,” he confided. “I thought he would like to know. He always likes to know what you are fond of, cousin. Do you remember? He was so interested in the bird.”

Arian went on eating his bread.

“I have not told him which,” Rocco went on, in the same voice. “Not yet. I thought I should like to watch a little longer first. Vittorio tells me you walked him home last night from the sixth floor, all the way to the gangway, like a governess.” He folded the letter neatly in three. “I have mentioned to the Headmaster that you go ashore at night. He did not much care, but he dislikes being told a thing he would rather not know, and so you are to stand on deck on Sunday, from first light until the bell, and think about the reputation of the Institute.” He smiled. “Shall I tell Dario which one, bratets?”

Arian set down his bread.

“Tell him.”

It was the first thing he had said to Rocco since the Baltic. Vittorio looked up from his bowl. Down the table the seventh-years went on with their kasha and did not lift their eyes, and the Headmaster turned a page.

Rocco looked at him along the table for a long moment, and Arian looked back, and after a while Rocco smiled again, and it was Dario’s smile, the one Dario kept for mornings he had arranged something, which Rocco had been practising since he was nine. It did not sit on Rocco’s face as well as it sat on Dario’s. It never had.

Arian got up and left the table.

He knew what Rocco was, what Dario was, and what Vittorio was. There were three of them, and there was one of him, and it had been so since the nursery at Chernoyar, and it had never once been enough. Let Rocco write. Let Dario know which one. If any of them wanted the boy, they would have to come through Arian to get to him, and they knew what that would cost, every one of them, and so did he.

He went out to the doors of the Great Hall, and was standing there when Beauxbatons came in from the carriage.

*   *   *

The Saturday was clear and very cold, with a high pale sky and the lake lying flat as a plate under it, and Caleb had resolved over his chocolate that he would not think about Arian von Conti all day.

He had been followed all through the Friday, from breakfast to the bell, at the length of a ballroom, and at supper the Durmstrang end of the Slytherin table had looked at him through the whole of the treacle tart. Caleb had borne it beautifully. He had not once looked back.

“Eleven times,” Cecily had remarked, on the way down the steps after supper.

“Pardon?”

“You looked back at him eleven times between the soup and the tart. I counted. I have never known you look back at anybody more than once in your life, and that was the Comte, and only to be sure he had gone.”

Caleb had said that she was mistaken, and that it had been only nine, not eleven. He had then gone very quiet after realising.

After luncheon on Saturday Cecily went down to the dungeons to be shown the Potions master’s stores, which was to Cecily what a fitting at the finest robe-maker in Paris was to other girls, and Caleb, with a slice of treacle tart wrapped in a napkin in one pocket and a roll in the other, went down to the lake.

He told himself it was to see the squid. He had heard about the squid from four different Ravenclaws and did not believe any of them, and it seemed to him that a Frenchman who came all the way to Scotland and did not look into its lake for a monster could not hold up his head in Paris afterwards. He did not tell himself anything about the step he could hear on the lawn behind him, a ballroom’s length back, all the way down to the water.

Beyond the boathouse, where the lawn gave out and the trees came down to the water, there was a strip of grey shingle hidden from the castle by a shoulder of rock, with a beech leaning out over it that had kept its leaves, the colour of a fox. The lake lay flat and pewter to the mountains. The Durmstrang ship was out of sight around the point, though he could hear it now and then, creaking at its anchor like an old man settling his bones.

He sat down on a flat rock at the water’s edge and tore a corner off the roll and dropped it into the shallows, to see what the lake would make of it.

For a while it made nothing. The bread floated, and went soft, and turned slowly around on the still water as if it were looking for somewhere to go. Caleb dropped in another piece, to keep it company.

“You are being very discourteous,” he informed the lake. “I have come a long way to be introduced.”

A long pale arm came up out of the deeper water and felt about among the stones, delicately, like a lady feeling in her reticule for her fan, and took it.

Caleb sat very still.

The arm went down. After a moment it came up again, a little nearer, and lay along the shingle in the shallows with the tip of it curled, waiting, the suckers on its underside opening and closing in the cold water like a row of mouths. Caleb tore off another piece of the roll, a crust this time, and held it out over the water at arm’s length, and the tip of the arm rose out of the lake and took it from his fingers, as gently as Maman took a sugared almond from a dish, and went down again.

“Merci,” Caleb whispered, enchanted. “Merci, madame.”

By the time he was halfway through the roll he had established that the giant squid would take bread, and preferred the crust, and would never take it from the water if it could be had from his hand. He gave it the last of the crust and the whole of his attention, and for a quarter of an hour he quite forgot to listen for anything else at all.

Behind him the shingle shifted.

It was not the squid. Caleb wiped his fingers on his handkerchief and turned around on the rock.

Arian von Conti stood at the edge of the trees, a few yards off, in his red robes and his cloak of fur, with the beech leaves about his boots. He had stopped there, as if the shingle had given him away before he was ready, and though he came no nearer, he did not go.

Caleb’s heart gave one hard knock against his ribs, as it had behind the armour.

They were alone. The shore was empty, and the lawn above it, and the shoulder of rock hid them both from the castle. There was nobody here to see, and nobody to carry it to Andrew, and nobody to count. He thought of Rocco on the library landing, and of the greenhouse path. His wand was in his sleeve. He left it there. One did not draw on a guest, not even a Conti. Besides, if this boy had meant him harm, he had had six days and a castle full of dark corners in which to do it, and he had spent them standing in the rain.

Caleb rose, as one rises when somebody is shown in, even on a beach.

“Good afternoon, monsieur.”

“Good afternoon.”

His voice was low and level, with an accent from somewhere a long way north. Caleb had not heard it before. He had wondered all week what it would sound like. It was deeper than he had thought, and quieter, and it went into the ear and stayed there.

“You have followed me for six days, monsieur,” Caleb observed. “Up the library stair, and around the cloister, and down the whole of a corridor on the fifth floor while I was reading to two old ladies, which was very ill-bred of you, and along the boathouse path in the rain, where I believe you stood for an hour in the middle of the path and did not notice it was raining. To the top of the tallest tower in this castle on Tuesday night, past a lady in curl papers who asked me whether she ought to scream, and I told her it was not necessary, though I was not entirely certain of it myself.” He folded his hands before him. “And on Thursday night you followed me to the sixth floor and went away again, and left me behind a suit of armour. Nobody has ever left me behind a suit of armour, monsieur. I was mortified.”

Arian did not trouble to deny any of it. He just looked at him. His face gave nothing, but the look itself was not empty. It never was. It lay on Caleb steadily and with great attention, as if he were something that had to be learned by heart.

“What is it that you want of me, monsieur?” Caleb asked him, though he knew.

He had seen it across the Hall on the first night, and he had felt it in the dark behind the armour, where he could not see it at all, a look that had no business in so cold a face. When those eyes were on Caleb they were heated, and he could feel the weight of them regardless of the distance, and when they rested on him for long, they burned. They burned now. Arian let the question go by, and only kept looking, and Caleb felt the warmth go up his neck, and was annoyed to feel it, and understood that he had been answered after all.

He decided, since one of them had to, to proceed as if he had not understood.

“Caleb de Blanchemer,” said Caleb, since there was nobody on the shore to make the introduction for them. “And you, monsieur?”

The boy considered him.

“Arian.”

Caleb eyed the fur, and the stoic face above it. “Is your cloak purely decorative, monsieur?”

Arian looked at him a moment longer.

“Yes.”

“Then you will not need a fire. I do, and you are welcome to share it.”

It was not warm on the shore for Caleb, and so he drew his wand and traced a ring on the flat stone at his feet and filled it with a double handful of flame the blue of bluebells, which burned without wood or smoke and threw out its warmth a yard on every side. Arian looked at the fire. Then he came down over the shingle, past it, and sat on Caleb’s own rock, at his elbow, so near that the fur of his cloak lay against Caleb’s sleeve, with his hands in his lap and his face as still as a portrait whose subject has gone out for the evening. He had finished with the fire. He looked at Caleb.

Caleb was sitting as straight as a candle, and could not think what to do with his hands.

He had meant to sit down again on the other side of the fire. That was how it was done. One made the fire, and one’s guest took the far side of it, and there was a decent width of warm air between, across which one might say anything one liked. He had not thought for a moment that the guest would simply walk around it. Nobody had ever walked around a fire of his. He had braced himself for the cold of the boy, all down one side of him, like standing beside a window in winter. There was no cold at all. Arian von Conti was warm as a wall is warm at the end of a long day in August, when the sun has gone and the stone goes on giving it back, steady and close and much too near, through the fur and the silk and the flannel, and Caleb, who had been cold since Carlisle, had not the least idea what to do with it. He could smell pine, and snow, and something underneath them both that was only warm skin.

Above them the beech let go of a leaf. It came wafting down through the blue light and caught in Caleb’s hair, but before he could put up his hand to it, Arian had reached up and taken it out, with two fingers, unhurried. His fingers brushed Caleb’s temple as they went, and they were hot.

Startled, Caleb drew back before he could help it.

“One does not, monsieur,” he rebuked him, voice as cool as the lake.

Arian turned the leaf over. He made no apology, and seemed not to know that one was owed.

Nobody touched Caleb de Blanchemer. Everyone who had ever admired him, in Paris and at Beauxbatons and at every ball since he was six, had kept to their own side of the room, as one keeps to the far side of the rope in a gallery. His family touched him, and Cecily, who had taken a cobweb out of his hair on the first night, had the right of it by eleven minutes. This boy had none, and he had sat down at Caleb’s elbow and put out his hand and touched him all the same.

The place at his temple where those fingers had been went on feeling the heat of them.

“You need not sit so close, monsieur,” Caleb protested, with perfect courtesy. “There is a great deal of room for the both of us.”

Arian was on his feet at once. He went around the blue fire and sat down on the far side of it, on a stone of his own, with the beech leaf still in his hand, and went on looking at him.

Caleb breathed again.

The speed of it surprised him more than the leaf had. He had said it as he would have said it to a too-pressing partner at a ball, with a smile that closed the door, and the partner would have laughed, or sulked, or pretended not to hear and edged nearer. This one had got up and gone the moment he was asked, without a word or a look of reproach. Then he had sat down at the distance he was given and gone on looking, as if he meant to look until the lake froze over, and had not the least idea that there was anything strange in it.

It came to Caleb, with a curious drop of the heart, that he could have sent this boy anywhere in Scotland with one word, and that he would have gone, and that he had better take care which words he used.

Caleb did not know what to do with that either. He sat down again on his rock.

He had been brought up to carry a conversation for two at any table in France, and so, a little coolly at first, he carried this one. He spoke of the carriage and the moors, and the river like a dropped ribbon, and the twelve palominos, who disliked Scotland and had said so, and the bath at the back of the carriage that smelled of the sea, and the trolls on the seventh floor, which Arian had seen as well as he had.

“Do you know, I believe the large one is in love with the dancing master. She curtsies to him on every second turn, and he has not once noticed. It is a tragedy in wool. I mean to go back and see whether he comes to his senses.”

Arian said nothing. Whether he listened was another matter. Caleb had sat through a great many dinners beside people who looked at him and did not listen, and he had always known the difference. With this boy he could not tell. Arian looked at him as if every word were being written down somewhere inside him to be read again later. Or perhaps he was only watching Caleb’s mouth, and not a word of it went in.

So Caleb asked whether there were portholes in the Durmstrang cabins, and what one saw from them.

Arian kept the answer to himself. He looked only at Caleb, never at the lake or the fire, steadily, as nobody in France would have thought it civil to look, and at the question his hand closed on the beech leaf in his lap, and he drew breath as if to answer, and let it go again. Caleb thought that the boy could not often have been asked anything about himself.

He asked him the other thing then, since he had been wondering about it for a week and had never been good at leaving a question alone.

“My sister tells me that you are first in the Dark Arts, monsieur, and my brother has written that I am not to be in the same room as you.” He glanced up at the sky. “Is it dull? Charms is very dull.”

It was not at all what one said to a von Conti on a beach. Arian’s hand went quite still on the leaf. He did not answer that either. But he looked at Caleb for a long moment as if nobody had ever put it to him as a question, and Caleb was a good deal more curious about the answer than afraid of it.

So Caleb went on, and somewhere in the going on the coolness went out of his voice without asking his leave.

He told Arian about the squid. He told him about the swan at Chantilly, which had been very rude to him first, and about Andrew’s Monday letter and his plan of the castle with the dark parts shaded in, and how he had answered the letter to say that he had been in bed by ten every night and had fed nothing whatever, and had then come straight down to the lake and fed a squid. He told it all lightly and well, as he told things at home, and at the end of it, when he said and so I am a liar, monsieur, and a disobedient brother, and the squid prefers the crust, something happened at the corner of Arian’s mouth.

It was over too quickly to be called a smile, but it was there, and gone, like the light from a window when a curtain is lifted and let fall, and Caleb saw it, and was so pleased with himself that he forgot to be wary for almost a minute.

By the time the sun had gone down behind the mountains, Arian had come a third of the way back around the fire, stone and all, to the side the wind blew from, so that the wind off the lake reached Caleb by way of Arian or not at all. Caleb had not seen him do it. He looked at the stone, and at Arian, who looked back without the least shame.

“If you come any further around, monsieur,” Caleb told him, “you will be sitting in the lake, and I shall have to explain you to the squid.”

Arian left the stone where he had put it. Caleb decided, for the present, to let it be.

When the lake had turned from pewter to slate, he drew his knees up under his chin, and remembered the napkin in his pocket. He took it out and unwrapped the treacle tart, which had suffered a little on the journey, and put it into Arian’s hands without asking him, since that appeared to be how things were done on this shore. Arian looked at it, and then at Caleb, and broke off a corner.

“Alors?”

Arian swallowed.

“It is sweet.”

“That is its whole intention.” Caleb smiled at him, and it was a different smile from the one he gave the Hall. “The English take it with custard. You must make up your own mind about that, as I am making up mine about you.”

There was a crumb of it at the corner of Arian’s mouth, where the not-smile had been. Caleb looked at it, and found that his own hand had begun to lift from his knee, as it would have lifted to take a crumb from Cecily’s lip at breakfast, and he put it down again very fast. He was still looking at the mouth as well, and he wanted, quite plainly and to his own astonishment, to know whether it was as warm as the fingers had been. He looked at the lake instead, and was glad of the dusk.

Arian ate the rest of it slowly, as if somebody might come down the shingle and take it from him, and when it was gone he folded the napkin in four, with the leaf inside it, and held it.

Caleb watched him do it, and for once had nothing clever to say.

He had seen people eat as if somebody might take it from them. He had seen it in the village below Blanchemer, in the bad winter when he was ten, when Grand-mère had taken him down with her to the cottages with a basket. He had not thought to see it in a von Conti, in a fur cloak worth more than the village, at the table of the Great Hall with gold plates. But Arian had not eaten at that table. Caleb had watched him not eat, every night of the week.

He did not say anything. One did not.

“Will you put your name in the Goblet?” Caleb asked instead.

“Yes.”

“Would you advise me to put in mine?”

Arian regarded him for a long moment.

“It is dangerous,” he answered.

“My brother Andrew, who sits on the Tournament committee for our Ministry, writes that it is perfectly safe.” Caleb drew the letter out of his coat and showed him the last sheet across the fire. “I find that I believe you both, which is disobliging of you.”

He folded it away, and thought of the greenhouse path, and said nothing of it.

The blue fire was the only light on the shore now. From the trees a moth came fluttering out towards it. It was a soft brown thing, the colour of milky tea, the last of its kind that year, and it found the fire and flew into it, and out, and around, and into it again, and fell onto the stone beside Arian’s boot with one wing crumpled and smoking.

Arian’s face stayed as it was. Without looking down, he put out his hand and closed it over the moth, gently, and drew it up off the stone into his lap, where it was dark and the fire could not get at it, and kept it there.

Whether Caleb had seen, he never looked to find out. Caleb looked out at the lake, and kept it to himself.

Up at the castle a bell began to ring for supper, faint over the water. Arian got to his feet, but before he went along the shore he came around the fire and stood over Caleb’s rock, so near that Caleb had to tip his head back to look up at him, and looked down at him for so long that Caleb felt it at the back of his neck, and for a moment thought he might be going to say something more.

“Good night.”

“Bonne nuit, monsieur.”

He went away along the shore towards the point, where the ship was, with his right hand closed loosely at his side, carrying something into the dark.

Caleb sat on by the blue fire for a while after he had gone, with his arms around his knees.

He had no idea what had happened. He had come down to the lake to see a squid, and been followed there by a Conti, as he had been followed everywhere for six days, and sat beside one on a rock, and been touched by one, which nobody did, and told him a great deal of nonsense about swans, and given him his treacle tart. And the Conti had sat and looked at him through all of it, as if Caleb were the only thing on the shore worth looking at, and gone away again with a moth in his hand.

He thought he ought to be angry about the leaf. He tried, for a minute or two, and found he could not keep it up. It was too absurd. A boy who looked like a tombstone and walked like a funeral and had in all probability been taught to curse before he could read, sitting down on a rock without so much as a by-your-leave and taking a leaf out of a stranger’s hair as if he had a perfect right to it, and then getting up and going around the fire the moment he was told, and then creeping back around it, stone and all, to keep the wind off, when he thought Caleb was not looking.

Caleb had been courted before. He had been courted by the most practised admirers in Paris, with flowers and verses and sonnets in three languages. Nobody had ever courted him like that.

He stopped, very still, with his chin on his knees, and the word went on lying there in his head, where he had put it without meaning to.

Then he put out the fire and walked back up the lawn under the first stars, counting on his fingers. He made it thirteen words, one of which had been the boy’s own name, and he was quite certain that Arian had meant every one of them.

Cecily was in the dining saloon at the back of the carriage when he came in, warming her hands around a cup of chocolate, and she looked up as he passed.

“You smell of the lake.”

“I have been feeding the squid.”

She looked down. His hem was wet to the shin, and a beech leaf had stuck itself to it.

“Andrew says we are not to speak to any of them,” Cecily remarked, and went back to her chocolate.

Article Four. Each candidate shall write his name and the name of his school upon parchment, and shall place it in the Goblet of Fire with his own hand, before the feast of Halloween. The decision of the Goblet is final. A candidate once chosen is bound by it, and may not withdraw.

*   *   *

Cecily put her name in the Goblet at seven o’clock on Halloween morning, before the Beauxbatons seniors, who had come down to watch her do it.

She had dressed for it. She wore the pale blue silk with the silver buttons that Maman had had made for her in Paris in the summer, and her hair was up, and she had borrowed Caleb’s best gloves without asking, which he did not mind, because she looked in them like a duchess going to a coronation and he would not have had her look like anything less. The seniors stood behind her in their shawls watching, far too well mannered to yawn, and the Goblet burned before them in its gold ring as it had burned all week, blue-white and soundless and quite indifferent to the hour.

She walked across the Age Line, which lay on the flags of the Entrance Hall in a thin bright ring of gold, with her chin up, and dropped her parchment into the flames, and the flames went up white and came down.

The Beauxbatons students applauded courteously, in the French way, with the tips of their fingers, and murmured félicitations, mademoiselle to her across the flags. Caleb applauded loudest. He was sitting on the bottom step of the marble staircase with a piece of toast he had smuggled out of the carriage, because he could not be expected to wait for breakfast, and he waved it.

As she turned from the Goblet, the great front doors opened, and the Durmstrang party came in out of the cold with the frost still white on their furs.

Rocco came first. He stepped over the gold line as Cecily stepped out of it, and stood aside to let her by with a little bow.

“Mademoiselle de Blanchemer.” He smiled at her, warm and easy and pleased. “I do hope it chooses you.”

Caleb had last seen that face smiling up the well of the library stair, and before that through the glass of a greenhouse, and he had seen what the hand was doing while the face was pleased.

He did not move. The toast stayed where it was, halfway to his mouth. He watched Rocco’s hand, which was in his pocket again, where it had been on the greenhouse path before it came out with the wand, and he watched his sister walk past it, a foot away, with her chin still up, and he felt the cold of the flagstones come up through the step into him and go all the way up his back.

Cecily walked past Rocco as if he were a draught under a door. Behind her he put in his name and made a performance of it, while Vittorio, who was a year too young and knew it, stood at the edge of the Age Line and applauded him. Arian came in after his cousins, as he had come up the Hall, and stepped over the gold line without looking at it, and let his name fall into the fire.

The flames went up white, almost to the ceiling, and came down again.

Arian turned from the Goblet and stared straight at Caleb on the bottom step. He did not go in to breakfast by the shortest way. He went the long way around, by the foot of the marble staircase, with a cousin at either shoulder, and passed the bottom step so near that the hem of his fur brushed the toe of Caleb’s boot. He kept his eyes on Caleb the whole way, and did not take them off him until the doors of the Great Hall closed behind him.

It was the first time Caleb had seen him since Saturday.

He had not been at supper on Saturday, nor anywhere at all on Sunday. Ambrose Fennick, who had it from a Slytherin who had it from the Durmstrang boys, reported that the younger Conti had spent the whole of Sunday at a punishment drill on the deck of the ship, in the sleet. Caleb had told himself that a Sunday without being followed was very restful, and had stood at the top of the library stair for some while, looking down, before he went in.

Cecily called the way he looked at people cold. Caleb had sat beside him on a rock, and knew better.

He found that he was holding the toast as if it were a wand.

Cecily sat down beside her brother and took the toast out of his hand.

“Did you hear him? He hopes it chooses me. Quel toupet.” She bit into it. “So do I, since I should very much like to teach these Contis a lesson.”

Caleb watched the doors swing shut behind the red robes. One name would come out of the cup for each school, and only one. He had read the notice.

He thought of the greenhouse path, and the three red welts. He thought of the laugh he had heard that was warm and pleased and cruel. He thought of Cecily on the dragon’s ground or the lake’s edge or wherever it was the Tournament meant to send its champions, in her blue silk with her chin up, being seen to do it properly, and Rocco von Conti on the other side of the enclosure with his hand in his pocket, smiling, hoping.

He drew Andrew’s letter out of his coat and found the last sheet. He tore the foot off it, just below perfectly safe, borrowed a quill from a passing Ravenclaw, and wrote his name and his school on the back of it against the wall.

The Ravenclaw was Ambrose Fennick, on his way in to breakfast, and he stood holding out his hand for the quill with his mouth a little open.

“Merci, monsieur.” Caleb gave it back to him with a smile. “I shall buy you a better one in Hogsmeade.”

“Caleb.” Cecily was on her feet. “Caleb. No.”

“You told me that I do not decide things.” He folded the slip in two. “I am deciding now.”

He stepped over the Age Line. It let him pass, with a warm prickle at the ankles like walking through a sunbeam, and he held his name out over the Goblet and let it drop. The flames took it, and went up white, then came down.

Somebody behind him gasped dramatically. Two of the Beauxbatons girls had their hands over their mouths. Ambrose Fennick dropped the quill.

“You did not tell me you wanted to.” Cecily did not raise her voice, which in a de Blanchemer comes to the same thing as shouting. “In the carriage you said you had not so much as thought of it, and I believed you. You have told me everything since we were in the nursery.”

Caleb looked down at the torn edge of Andrew’s letter, and at the line now left at the bottom of it in their brother’s hurried hand. The Tournament has been made perfectly safe.

“I had not. Not until a minute ago.”

“Then you might have thought of it aloud.” She sat down again on the step beside him, which was worse than if she had walked away. “Had you told me you wanted to be champion, I should never have put in my name at all. You have never once tried at your lessons, or at anything at all except hunting, and you would win it all the same, because everything comes to you without trying. I know that as well as I know my own face in the glass, and I would have been proud of you and cheered you on.” She looked at the Goblet, burning in its gold ring. “I have been preparing myself for this since August, Caleb. I thought it was left to me to do it for the family since you were not interested.” Her mouth shook, and she firmed it. “If the Goblet chooses you, and it will, I shall not speak to you for a week.”

Caleb said nothing.

There were a great many things he might have said, and he looked at all of them, sitting there on the cold step with his sister beside him with her gloved hands folded in her lap. He might have said that it would most likely choose her, because she had worked for it and he had not. He might have said that he had not done it to take anything from her, and that he would rather have cut off his hand. He might have said Rocco. Every one of them was true, and every one of them would have told her that he thought she needed him, and so he said none of them.

He took her hand instead, gloves and all, and held it.

She let him, for the length of three breaths. Then she drew it away, and stood, and went in to breakfast with her back very straight and her blue silk whispering on the flags, and did not look around.

Caleb sat on alone on the bottom step, with the letter in his hand, and watched the Goblet burn.

Arian heard of it at breakfast.

Vittorio had been last in through the doors of the Great Hall, and had looked back, and seen. He came down the Durmstrang end of the Slytherin table with the news before he had sat down, bursting with it, and Rocco let him tell it.

“Bozhe moy. On the back of a letter,” Vittorio crowed, in Russian, keeping his voice low because the English were all around them, though not one of them could have understood a word. “Torn off. With somebody else’s quill. The little prince just got up off the stairs and tore a bit off a letter and walked over the line and put it in, in front of everybody, and the girl —” he laughed his tray-dropping laugh, “— the princess looked as if he had slapped her.”

Rocco smiled into his tea.

“Molodets,” he murmured. “How very gallant. To put himself on the table in her place.” He turned the cup slowly in its saucer. “I did hope it would be the sister, I confess. She has such a very proud way of walking. But it does not matter. One de Blanchemer will do as well as another.”

Arian went on looking at his bread.

He had seen Rocco bow to the girl in the Entrance Hall, and he had heard what Rocco said, and he had turned at the Goblet and seen the boy’s face on the bottom step. It had been quite pale. Arian had not known, then, what he was looking at. He had thought the boy was afraid of Rocco, and had been glad of it, because a boy who was afraid of Rocco would stay away from him.

He knew now. He sat at the table with the bread going to crumbs in his fingers, and understood that the boy had not been afraid for himself at all.

Will you put your name in the Goblet? the boy had asked him across the blue fire. Would you advise me to put in mine?

He had told him that it was dangerous. The boy had listened, and gone and done it anyway, for his sister, on the back of a letter.

Arian put down the bread.

Beside him Rocco was talking to Vittorio about the dragons, which he had heard of from somebody, and about what a dragon might do to a soft French boy who had been taught nothing but how to bow.

At luncheon Arian went down to the shingle under the beech.

The blue fire had left its mark on the flat stone where the boy had laid it, a ring the size of a dinner plate, burned clean and pale into the lichen. The beech was still shedding its leaves. They came down one at a time through the still air, fox-coloured, and lay on the shingle and on the stone, and one of them fell into the pale ring, and lay there.

Arian stood over it for a long time. Then he took a folded napkin from the breast of his robes, where it had been since Saturday, and opened it on the other leaf, the one he had taken from the boy’s hair. He had taken it because it let him touch. He had felt the boy’s temple under his two fingers all the way back to the ship, and for an hour after in his hammock, and the curl that had brushed his knuckle, and the breath the boy had drawn. On Sunday he had stood on deck from first light until the bell, with the sleet driving in off the lake, and all he had wanted was to come back to this beech and put his mouth where his fingers had been. He folded the napkin and put it away.

A party was coming up the drive from the gates. Arian watched them pass between the winged boars, a dozen figures in Ministry grey with two clerks and their boxes a pace behind, and at their head a tall man who walked as if the drive had been laid for him. Even at that distance he was beautiful, with the high brow and fine cheekbones of the de Blanchemers, which Arian had spent a week looking at across the Great Hall in another face. As he passed the lake he ran his eye over it, and over the ship, as a man reads down a page for the clause that will give trouble, and Arian, under the beech, felt himself found and filed away.

“The French Ministry,” said Enzo, behind him.

His uncle had come along the shingle without a sound, as he always did, with the collar of his black coat turned up.

“That is Andrew de Blanchemer,” he went on, in Russian. “The eldest son, and the next head of the family, if he chooses it, which they say he does not. He likes the Ministry better. He sits on the Committee for the French, he is one of the judges, and he wrote the greater part of the rules, which he will be reading aloud tonight to anyone who stands still.” He took his gloves from his pocket and drew them on. “There are two sisters in Paris. One of them is an Auror, and a good one. I knew her captain. She will come with the guard for the tasks, and in time, I should think, she will lead the family. And there are the parents. He has a great many relations, Arian. Every one of them would cross the Channel on foot to put a curse on any of us who came within a yard of him, and the way you are pursuing the boy, every one of them would be right to.”

Arian said nothing.

“He has put his name in the Goblet.”

“Yes.”

“So have you.”

Arian let that stand.

His uncle considered him for a while, there under the beech, with the leaves coming down between them.

“Ladno. Then we shall see what it says,” said Enzo, and turned back towards the ship.

Andrew came down to the carriage at five o’clock, alone, in his white travelling cloak, with the frost already settling on the lawn behind him, and knocked on the door as if it were a door at the Ministry and he had an appointment.

He was let in by Odile Marchand, who saw who it was and curtsied so low that she very nearly sat down. Andrew thanked her in the voice he used for ambassadors’ wives, and asked after her mother in Rouen by name, and left her pink to the hair in the doorway, and came through the dining saloon at the back of the carriage to where his brother and sister were, and stopped, and looked at them.

He had their father’s fair hair and blue eyes, and a face that three Ministries had learned could not be read, and in the dining saloon of the carriage, where nobody could see him but the two of them, he did not trouble to keep it. He embraced Cecily first, briefly and hard. Then he embraced Caleb, for rather longer, and held him off at arm’s length and looked him over from his curls to his boots, as if he were counting the limbs and wished to be sure of the total.

“You are both well.”

“Perfectly, Andrew,” said Caleb.

Cecily said nothing. She had a book open on the table in front of her, and she turned a page.

Andrew glanced at her, and then at the book, and appeared to decide that Cecily was reading, which she very often was. He took his hands back and surveyed the dining saloon instead, at the silk benches and the lamps and the little round window over the berths, with the face he wore for inspecting the accommodation of a delegation.

“The Forest is forty yards from this window,” he observed.

“Thirty-eight, mon frère. I paced it.”

“You paced it.”

“By daylight,” Caleb assured him. “With a great many Beauxbatons seniors in attendance. It was very dull.”

Andrew went to the round window and looked out at the Forest, which stood black at the edge of the lawn with the last light going out of it, and he regarded it for some time, as if he meant to have it moved further off by the Committee and was drafting the memorandum. Then he turned around.

“Show me.”

Caleb sighed, and stood up, and unfastened the top two buttons of his collar. Andrew came across the saloon and turned back the pale blue silk with one finger, as a physician turns back a dressing, and inspected what was underneath it, which was flannel, and let the silk go again.

“Good.”

“I have worn it every day, Andrew. I have worn it so faithfully that the Ravenclaws have begun to remark on how warm I look.”

“Good,” said Andrew again, and did the buttons up for him himself, both of them, as he had done them up when Caleb was four, before Caleb could stop him. Then he looked down. “Where are your gloves?”

Caleb glanced at his sister. His sister’s hands lay on either side of her book in his best grey kid gloves, a size too large for her, and she turned another page and did not look up.

“I have lent them,” said Caleb.

Andrew looked at Cecily’s hands, and at Caleb’s, and at Cecily’s again, and drew the conclusion any brother would have drawn, which was the wrong one, and let it go. He bent and kissed Cecily on the forehead.

“You have put your name in.”

“At seven o’clock this morning, Andrew,” Cecily said to her book. “Before the whole of Beauxbatons. As I wrote you I would.”

“Good,” said Andrew, for the third time, and his face did something that only his family would have seen, and that was pride. “It will be you,” he told her, as a man announces the result of a vote he has already counted. Then he turned to Caleb, and the pride went out of it, and something more anxious came in. “And you have kept away.”

Behind him Cecily turned a page so sharply that it tore a little at the edge.

“I have been the soul of discretion, mon frère,” Caleb told him, with perfect truth, since he had not discussed it with anybody. “You need not have worried for one moment.”

“I do not worry. I take precautions.” Andrew drew his gloves from his pocket and began to put them on, finger by finger, which he did when he had finished with a room. “You have had my letters?”

“Every word of them.” Caleb laid his hand flat over his heart, where the letters were, and they crackled, all but the foot of the last sheet, which was at that moment lying at the bottom of the Goblet of Fire with his name on the back of it. “I keep them here, Andrew. I could not bear to be parted from them.”

“And the younger Conti?”

Caleb had been waiting for it all week.

“The Headmistress writes that he has been seen at the end of a great many corridors,” Andrew went on. He did not raise his voice. He never did. “He is first in the Dark Arts in the whole of that Institute, Caleb. I have read the returns. I grew up under the portrait, as you did, and I know what that house does with what it is taught. A boy who can do what he can do does not stand about a castle for a week out of an interest in the architecture. He wants something. When a Conti has wanted something of a de Blanchemer, somebody has generally been carried home.”

“I have not once been in a room with him outside the Hall, as you wrote,” Caleb said, which was quite true, since nobody could call the shore of a lake a room.

At the table Cecily turned a page, and said nothing about beech leaves.

Andrew studied him for a moment, as if something about it did not altogether satisfy him and he could not find the clause. Then he inclined his head, as he would have inclined it to a Minister, and went to the door, and turned there.

“Supper is at seven. I sit with the judges. You will both sit where I can see you.”

“We always do, Andrew.”

“Yes,” said Andrew, “that is what concerns me,” and went out, and they heard him on the steps telling Odile Marchand that he hoped her mother’s chest was better, and then the frost under his boots going away up the lawn.

Cecily shut her book.

“You lied to him,” she said, to the round window.

“I did nothing of the kind, Cecy. I told him I had been discreet. I have been extremely discreet. I told nobody at all, not even you.” Caleb buttoned his coat over the letters. “You may tell him after supper. I give you leave. I should like to see his face.”

Cecily opened her mouth, and thought better of it, and shut it again, and went out of the saloon with her book and his gloves, and the door of their cabin closed behind her without a sound.

The Great Hall had been decorated for Halloween with a thousand live bats, which swept in black ribbons beneath the enchanted ceiling, and with pumpkins the size of carriage wheels, which grinned down from every corner with candles in their heads. The Goblet stood before the staff table, and the whole Hall came in to supper looking at it.

The Durmstrang party was in its place already when Caleb came in with Cecily, and Arian looked up when Caleb came in, and did not look down again.

Andrew sat among the judges at the staff table, changed for supper into grey robes so correct that they seemed to have been drafted rather than tailored. He saw them come in and lifted his hand an inch from the cloth, which from Andrew de Blanchemer in a public place was as good as a wave. Then he saw who else in the Hall was looking at Caleb, and laid his fork down beside his plate, square to the edge of it, as a man lays down his pen when a treaty has gone wrong.

Caleb lit up at the wave all the same.

He threw his brother his best smile, the one which shaped his eyes into crescents and showed his perfect teeth, up the whole length of the Hall to the staff table, and though Andrew did not smile back he was very plainly pleased. Heads turned all along the Ravenclaw table to see who had been given it.

Andrew de Blanchemer had stood beside the Minister at the treaty of Geneva, and his face had been in the Prophet a dozen times. Everybody knew the de Blanchemers, and now the Hall looked from the twins under the windows to the staff table and back again, at three of the five de Blanchemers in one room, the eldest and the two youngest.

Cecily, who had said one sentence to him since the morning, watched Andrew’s fork in the curve of the jug. “Andrew has seen who else is looking at you,” she told the jug. Caleb tried not to smile, as it would only make her crosser.

All through supper the Hall watched the Goblet, and Andrew watched the Slytherin table between mouthfuls, and Cecily watched Andrew, and Caleb, who had been told often enough that one does not look at people more than is polite, admired the bats. When the gold plates had been cleared, Professor Dumbledore rose and lifted his wand, and every candle in the Hall went out except the ones inside the pumpkins. In the orange dark the Goblet blazed up blue and white, and every face was lit by it from beneath and turned towards it, and it was so quiet that Caleb could hear the bats.

He thought of Cecily’s parchment in its green ink, lying in the bottom of that fire. He thought of his own, on the back of Andrew’s letter, with perfectly safe on the other side of it.

Choose me, he asked the Goblet silently, as politely as he would have asked a favour of a great-aunt. S’il vous plaît, madame. Choose me, and not her.

Then the flames turned red.

They turned the red of a hot coal, all at once, and sparks flew up out of them, and out of the sparks a slip of charred parchment came fluttering up and down again. Professor Dumbledore caught it in his long fingers and held it to the light.

“The champion for Beauxbatons,” he read, in a voice that carried up into the rafters among the bats, “will be Caleb de Blanchemer.”

For a moment the Beauxbatons end of the table did nothing at all.

Then it rose. It rose all together, as a concert hall rises when the last note of a great performance has gone up into the dome, with a scrape of benches and a rustle of pale blue silk, and it applauded him, bare hand against bare hand, with their napkins fallen on the flags and their goblets rocking. But they were astonished. They had come to Scotland expecting Cecily, who had been preparing since August, and the Goblet had chosen her brother, who had never in seven years at Beauxbatons tried at anything. Odile Marchand had her hands over her mouth. Two of the Snap boys were staring at him as if he had grown wings. At the head of the table the Headmistress had risen with the rest, slowly, and she was applauding too, but she was looking down the table at Caleb with her eyebrows raised up into her hair, as if Caleb had stood up in the middle of a lesson and begun to speak fluent Mermish.

Caleb barely heard it. He was watching Andrew.

He saw Andrew go still at the staff table. To anyone else in the Hall he would have looked as composed as his own portrait, with the face he wore for ministers. But his hands had come to lie flat on the cloth on either side of his plate, and a muscle moved once in his jaw, and Caleb, who had known that jaw all his life, could see what it cost him. He could see, too, that Andrew had just remembered a conversation in the carriage at five o’clock about the soul of discretion. And he thought of Matilda, who was in Paris and would hear the news before morning, because somebody at the Ministry always told Matilda everything.

Pardon, ma sœur, he told her silently, across the Channel, and could very nearly hear her sigh.

Beside him, Cecily set her goblet down with a click like a pistol being cocked.

“You.”

“Cecy —”

“You did not even write your name on a proper piece of parchment. Instead, you wrote it on the back of Andrew’s letter. Quelle horreur.” She sniffed disapprovingly at him. “You can be certain I will let him know of that.”

She turned on the bench to face the staff table with her hands flat on the cloth and her mouth set, suddenly looking remarkably like their older brother. Caleb had shared a nursery and a governess and eleven minutes less than everything else with her, and he knew she would not speak to him for a week, as she had promised, and that she would mind the silence a good deal more than he did. She would tell Andrew about the letter, too, and Andrew would be unbearable about it, and Caleb supposed that was fair.

He had only just remembered that he ought to be standing when the Goblet turned red for the second time.

*   *   *

The parchment came up out of the fire, and down, and the long fingers caught it.

Arian had not looked at the Goblet once. All through the first naming and the applause, he had watched the boy under the windows. He had seen the boy’s face when his name was read out. It had gone quiet and still, like the face of somebody who has put his hand into a fire to take something out of it, and drawn it out again, and found the burn worth it. He had seen the boy look up the Hall at his brother, and then at his sister beside him. The boy stayed seated among the standing Beauxbatons students, his hands quiet on the cloth.

It is dangerous, Arian had told him.

The boy had done it anyway and would go out to face whatever the Tournament had made ready for its champions, with nothing but his wand and his good manners.

“The champion for Durmstrang,” Professor Dumbledore announced, “will be Arian von Conti.”

For a moment Arian did not hear it.

Beside him Rocco had been halfway to his feet. He had been sure since September, since before the ship sailed, and Arian watched his face do nothing at all for a long moment, and then set, slowly, the way plaster sets in a mould. Rocco had wanted to stand across an enclosure from a de Blanchemer with the Tournament’s leave to do whatever he liked, and write to Dario about it afterwards.

But it was Arian.

Rocco turned to him. The plaster had set, and he smiled over it.

“Pozdravlyayu, bratets,” he murmured in Russian. “Congratulations. I shall write to Dario. He will be so pleased it was you.”

Under the table his hand had shut on his wand so hard that the knuckles stood up white.

Nobody at the Durmstrang end applauded except the first-year in the fur cap, who had leapt up on the bench and was clapping alone with his whole heart, until Vittorio pulled him down by the back of his robes. “Sit down, durak.”

Arian stood up slowly and deliberately from the Slytherin table, all the while looking only at the boy.

He stood with the whole of the Great Hall turned to look at him, three schools and a high table and a thousand bats, and Rocco at his left hand like a blade laid on the cloth, and he did not see any of it. He saw the boy under the windows lift his head, and the candlelight run along his jaw and down his bare throat to the blue of his collar, and his own mouth went dry.

The Goblet had done it. For a week he had followed the boy about the castle before three schools with no right to it at all, and let Rocco write down every yard. Now the Goblet had put the two of them side by side in front of the whole world, by its own law, which nobody could argue with, not Dario, not his father, not the boy’s brother at the high table with his fork laid square. Wherever the boy went now, to the Forest and the cage and whatever was in it, Arian had the right to go too.

He had been going already. Now nobody could call him off.

Across the Hall, the boy finally stood up and looked directly back at Arian von Conti.

The Hall had gone very quiet. Professor Dumbledore had lit the candles again with a wave of his hand, and they stood up tall and still above the tables in the warm air, every one of them straight as a lance, and the light lay evenly over everybody. Nothing moved. The two of them stood at their two tables with the whole width of the Hall between them, and two house tables, and every head in the Hall turned from one to the other and back, and nobody said a word.

The youngest de Blanchemer, and the youngest von Conti.

The boy stood quite straight, with his hands at his sides, as he must have been taught to stand when his name was spoken in a great house, and he looked across at Arian steadily and gravely, with his chin up, and there was a question in his face, and Arian did not know what it was.

“Through the door behind the staff table, if you would,” Professor Dumbledore called down the Hall. “Both of you.”

*   *   *

They went up the Hall by different ways, Caleb past the Ravenclaws and Arian past the Slytherins, and met at the Goblet. Caleb went with a smile for everybody and a word for nobody, and the Hufflepuffs across the way smiled back as if each of them had been given it alone. At the staff table Andrew sat very upright among the judges, and only his family would have known that he was unsettled. Arian walked a pace apart and looked at nothing. They went around the end of the staff table and through a door, and in the dark of the doorway a hand came to the small of Caleb’s back, hot through the silk, and stayed there for the whole of the step, unhurried, and took him through the door as if the door were Arian’s and Caleb his guest. Then it was gone, and Caleb was perfectly sure of it. The door shut behind them on a rising wave of noise.

It was a little panelled room with a fire in the grate, and its walls were hung so thick with portraits that the plaster hardly showed. The portraits had heard the roar through the door, and every one of them had crowded into the nearest frame for a better look. A witch in a ruff leaned out of a seascape with a lorgnette.

Caleb went to the fire and held out his hands to it, and watched them shake.

Behind him the room was very quiet, apart from the portraits, who were whispering, and the noise of the Hall coming muffled through the door like the sea through a wall.

He stood with his back to the room until his hands were still, and thought about what was behind him.

On the shore it had been a boy in a fur cloak, who said thirteen words and took leaves out of one’s hair and carried moths away in his fist. Here it was a von Conti. It was the champion of Durmstrang, of the house that had burned his great-grandfather’s face at Lyon and been on the other side of every war his family had fought in two hundred years, and first in his year, by Andrew’s account, in the arts it had burned him with, the cousin of a boy who hurt children on garden paths and laughed. The Goblet had put the two of them in a room together, and in the Hall beyond the door three schools were already talking about it, and at the high table sat his brother.

Caleb turned around.

Arian stood by the door, a little apart, with his back to the panelling, and the fire threw his shadow up the wall among the portraits. He had not moved since he came in. He stood with his hands at his sides and his cloak open, and he was watching Caleb.

Caleb bowed to him across the room.

“Mes félicitations, monsieur.”

Arian returned the bow.

“It seems that we are to be rivals.”

It came out cooler than he had meant. He let it. It was correct, and a good deal safer than anything else he could think of to say to a von Conti with the door shut. Arian heard it, and something in the way he stood changed, very little, as if a door had been closed somewhere and he had heard the latch.

And Caleb, who had been taught to be gracious to everyone and had never yet found a reason not to be, found that he could not leave it there.

“I confess I am relieved,” he went on, more quietly. “I had feared it would be your cousin. Your cousin and I should not have been rivals, monsieur. We should have been enemies, which would be troubling, as I have no experience in dealing with enemies.”

It was more than was wise. He waited to see what a Conti did with an insult to his blood.

Arian was silent for the space of a breath.

“Yes.”

Caleb had not expected that. A von Conti had just agreed, before a room full of portraits, that his own cousin was an enemy of the white house, and Caleb did not know what to do with it. The witch in the ruff lowered her lorgnette and looked from one of them to the other with her mouth pursed, and in the next frame an old wizard in a skullcap said well! to the room at large.

Neither of them mentioned that Arian had once told him it was dangerous.

It lay between them on the hearthrug all the same.

When he looked up from the hearthrug, Arian was no longer by the door. He had crossed the room without a sound while Caleb was looking at the rug, and he stood at Caleb’s shoulder with one hand laid on the mantelshelf behind him, so that Caleb had the fire on one side and the boy on the other and nowhere at all to go, near enough that the heat came off him like a second grate, and he was looking down into Caleb’s face. His breath stirred the curl at Caleb’s temple, the place where his fingers had been on Saturday, and Caleb felt it there as plainly as if he had been touched again. Something went down through him at that, warm and low and entirely unexpected, and he was glad of the fire, which could take the blame for his face.

Caleb did not step back. A de Blanchemer did not give ground to a Conti in a shut room. He folded his hands before him instead, as his governess had folded hers before she set him his lessons.

“One does not stand quite so near a gentleman one has met but once, monsieur,” he protested. “There is a great deal of room.”

Arian looked around at the room, which was not a large one, and very full of portraits, and then back at him. He took one step back. One. His hand came off the mantelshelf last, and the heat did not go with it.

Caleb looked down at the hearthrug between them, which had been a foot and was now a little more than a foot, and then up at the face above it, which had not changed in the least, and the corner of his own mouth went before he could stop it.

“One step, monsieur,” he observed. “At this rate we shall arrive at a respectable distance by Easter.”

Something moved at the corner of Arian’s mouth. He did not take another step.

Then the door burst open, and a girl fell into the room.

She was a tall freckled Hufflepuff with her hair escaping its ribbon and a smear of treacle on her chin, and she caught herself on the back of an armchair and stood there breathing hard, and saw who was in the room, and went crimson.

“Oh. Oh, sorry. Sorry, I didn’t mean to come in like a herd of cows, my nan always says I come into rooms like a herd of cows.” She let go of the armchair and smoothed down her robes and stood up straight, which made her nearly as tall as Caleb. “Hello. Good evening. I’m Rhiannon Hale. The Hogwarts champion, apparently.” She pushed her hair out of her eyes. “I only put my name in because my mum wrote that I wouldn’t, and I’m starting to think that was the whole idea.”

“Caleb de Blanchemer.” Caleb bowed over her hand. “Enchanté, mademoiselle. Your mother will be proud of you, I am sure, once she has stopped screaming.”

Rhiannon laughed, and then looked unsure whether she ought to have. She bobbed something that was very nearly a curtsey and was plainly the first she had ever attempted, and then looked at her own knees in horror, as if they had done it without asking her.

“Sorry. I don’t know why I did that. It’s just, you’re — I mean, everyone knows who you are. Your brother’s on the committee. It was in the Prophet.” She turned, with a visible effort of courage, to the boy by the fire. “And you’re Mr von Conti. Congratulations. Both of you. Is it congratulations? I think it’s congratulations. My nan’ll say it’s a judgement on me.”

Arian bowed to her, gravely, as he had bowed to Caleb.

Rhiannon stared at the bowed head, and then at Caleb, and then, a little wildly, at the witch with the lorgnette, as if for advice.

“He’s ever so polite,” she whispered to Caleb, as one whispers in church.

“He is,” Caleb agreed, and found, to his own surprise, that it was true.

The door opened again before he could think what he meant by it, and the room filled with Heads and judges, and in the middle of them all was Andrew.

“You are not participating,” Andrew announced as he entered.

“Bonsoir, Andrew.”

“You are a child.”

“I am seventeen, and the same age as Cecily, which was the whole of the requirement, and it was you who wrote it.” Caleb kissed him on both cheeks, which made his brother frown, because Andrew wanted to be angry with him and could not be, when Caleb greeted him so cheerfully. “And you told me yourself, mon frère, that it was perfectly safe.”

Andrew regarded him in contemplative silence, as he regarded a minister who had quoted his own memorandum back to him. Then he looked past Caleb’s shoulder, once, at the boy standing a step from his brother, where no von Conti had any business to be standing, as he looked at a clause he meant to have struck out of a treaty, and Arian looked back at him and did not move, and gave no more ground to the eldest de Blanchemer than he had to the youngest.

By the door, with his gloves in one hand, stood Enzo von Conti. He glanced at Andrew, and then at Arian, and then, for rather longer, at Caleb.

“The Goblet is a binding magical contract,” he finally remarked, in English as dry and exact as a clerk’s copperplate. “Monsieur de Blanchemer is aware of it. He drafted the notice.”

Andrew inclined his head to him, one official to another, and squared his parchments, and only Caleb saw that he squared them twice.

“The first task will take place on the twenty-fourth of November. Its purpose is to test your daring, and you will not be told of its nature. You will face it with your wands and nothing more, for courage before the unknown is a quality of the first importance in a wizard, and —”

He stopped, and looked at Caleb.

His hands, Caleb saw, were trembling on the parchment.

Caleb knew that tremble. All four of his siblings had it, and he was the cause of it. At fourteen months he had walked off the terrace at a Blanchemer estate to see where the peacocks went, and at five he had walked into the hippogriff paddock to wish the hippogriffs good morning, and while he had laughed joyfully both times, none of his four siblings had ever quite recovered.

“Andrew?” he prompted.

“It is perfectly safe.” Andrew addressed the parchment. “Every proper precaution has been taken. Every single one. I have seen to it personally.”

He turned away to the Headmaster of Durmstrang and began to speak of the timetable, in his Committee voice, as if nothing had happened, and the Heads and the judges gathered around him, and the room filled up with the business of it, dates and wands and the weighing of wands, and an argument about the press.

Rhiannon edged along the panelling to Caleb’s side.

“I didn’t expect that from Andrew de Blanchemer,” she breathed in wonder. “He always seems so perfect.”

“He is perfect, mademoiselle,” Caleb sniffed, offended at the insinuation that his brother was anything but.

“Oh, I meant it nicely.” Rhiannon smiled at him, delighted. “It’s lovely, that. Him minding so much. Last year my nan sent me a jumper for the Welsh Green. When we did Welsh Greens in Care of Magical Creatures.” She considered the back of Andrew’s head. “It had a dragon on it. Knitted. So I’d know which one it was and know to be careful.”

Caleb laughed. He could not help it, and he put his hand over his mouth too late, and saw that Andrew’s shoulders went very square in the middle of the Headmaster’s sentence.

Then he looked across the room, and stopped laughing.

Arian had not moved from where he stood by the fire. Everyone else in the room had turned to the business at the table, the judges and the Heads and Enzo von Conti with his gloves, and the portraits with them, and Rhiannon had turned to Caleb. Arian alone was looking at Andrew’s hands where they lay on the parchments. He had seen them shake. Nobody else had, not the judges or the Heads or the witch with the lorgnette. Nobody but Caleb, who had been born to it, and a von Conti, who had stood a yard off and watched.

Then Arian lifted his eyes from Andrew’s hands to Caleb, and Caleb understood that he knew what it meant.

A Conti had seen the one thing about the de Blanchemers that nobody outside the family was ever allowed to see, and he held it as quietly as he had held the moth, without looking around to see whether anyone had noticed. Caleb had the strangest feeling that if he crossed the hearthrug and asked Arian to forget it, it would be forgotten at once.

He did not cross the hearthrug. He stood beside Rhiannon with his hand still raised to his mouth and looked back, and for a moment the noise of the judges went a long way off.

When the judges let them go at last, Enzo von Conti stood aside from the door to let the champions pass, and as Arian went by him he spoke without turning his head.

“You will walk back to the ship with me.”

“Yes, Dyadya.”

At the turn of the passage Arian looked back, once, over his shoulder. Then he was gone into the dark of the castle beside his uncle.

When he went below that night his hammock lay on the boards in a heap, with both its lashings cut clean through, and across the berth deck Vittorio lay in his own with his face to the hull, too still to be asleep. Arian did not ask. He took his cloak up on deck and lay down on the planks in it, with the frost coming down on the fur, and slept until the bell.

*   *   *

Cecily kept her silence all the way back across the grounds and up the little ladder into the top berth of their cabin, where she pulled the blue curtain shut with a rattle of brass rings. Caleb lay in the bottom berth and listened to her not speaking to him, and smiled at the ceiling.

“If you contrive to get yourself killed,” said the curtain at last, “I shall never speak to you again.”

“That seems to me a poor inducement, Cecy,” Caleb murmured, “since I should hardly be in a position to notice the difference.”

“I am serious.”

“I know it.”

The curtain stirred. “I put my name in the same cup, before half the school. Andrew has known I would do it since September. He wrote me two lines to say that he was sure I should do the family credit, and a third to ask whether you were wearing your flannel.”

“He trusts you.”

“But not you.” The brass rings shivered. “You are the youngest, and his only brother, and you walk up to things that other people run away from, and nobody has ever been able to say what you will do next, you least of all. Andrew knows perfectly well that you set Papa’s collarbone on the terrace at twelve before the Healer had found his hat, and that the Healers in Paris have written twice to ask for you. And still he looks at you and sees his baby brother at the top of a staircase. And this evening he did up your buttons for you.”

“Three times, ma chère,” Caleb corrected her. “The Healers have written three times. The third came on Tuesday.”

“You see? Nobody tells me anything, either. It is the reward for being sensible.”

The curtain was quiet for a long time.

“Why did you do it?”

Caleb did not answer.

“You have never kept anything from me before.” The brass rings shivered once more, and her voice came out petulant and upset. “Not one thing, in seventeen years. Bien. Keep it, then. I shall find it out without you. And the Durmstrang champion is the youngest Conti.”

“Yes.”

“He looked only at you.” Her voice came down to him through the curtain, low and puzzled and not at all angry, which was worse. “When the Goblet called his name, Caleb, the whole Hall turned around to look at him. I turned around. Andrew turned around. He did not look at any of us. He was looking only at you, and he kept on looking at you all the way up the Hall, as if there were nobody else in it, and Andrew saw, and his cousins saw, and I should think the bats saw.”

“I know,” he whispered. “I saw.”

He lay very still in the dark.

He had seen. He had stood up under the windows with the whole Hall looking at him and found that he could look at nothing but the boy at the far end of the Slytherin table, who was looking at nothing but him. He had looked away from that face a hundred times in a week, at breakfast and on stairs and over the top of a page. Tonight, with three schools watching, he had not been able to.

He did not tell her about the leaf, or the tower stair, or the one word in the panelled room that a Conti had given him about his own cousin.

“What does he want with you?” asked the curtain.

“I do not know.”

“Nobody seems to,” Cecily said, after a while. “I used to think that was what I liked least about him. Now it is the way he looks at you. He knows very well what he wants. He is first in everything that house has ever done to ours, Caleb, and I do not like a boy like that looking at my brother as if he were hungry.”

After that her breathing slowed and lengthened, and he knew she was asleep.

He could not sleep, and at last he got up and wrapped himself in his cloak and knelt on the end of the berth at the little round window, and wiped the mist off the glass with his sleeve.

The grounds lay silver under the moon. On the black lake the Durmstrang ship rode at anchor with its masts against the stars and one porthole lit, low down near the stern. The Forest came so close that its branches almost brushed the carriage, and Caleb looked in among the black trunks without any reason, as one looks into a fire.

Deep in the trees, something lit them from below.

It was a glow at first, low down and orange, so that every branch between him and it stood out black like the leading in a church window. A long tongue of flame went up between the trees, roaring, higher than the tallest of them, and for as long as it lasted Caleb saw everything it lit. He saw a clearing churned to mud, and a ring of torches, and men in heavy leather running. He saw iron bars as thick as a man’s arm going up into the dark, the bars of a cage that would have held the Beauxbatons carriage and the palominos with it. And inside the cage he saw something vast and scaled turn its head, slowly, and look out through the bars with one eye like a lantern of molten brass.

Then the flame sank, and the Forest was dark, and in their paddock the palominos threw up their great heads all together and stamped in the cold.

Caleb sat on the end of the berth with his cloak about him and his breath misting the glass.

Out on the lake, in the Durmstrang ship, the one lit porthole near the stern went dark.

*   *   *

Cecily had promised not to speak to her brother for a week, and on the first morning of November she began, and she did it beautifully.

She did not speak to him on the carriage steps, where the frost had come down in the night as thick as sugar on a brioche, and where he offered her his arm and she took Odile Marchand’s instead. Nor did she on the long walk up the lawn, though he pointed out to her a heron standing on one leg in the shallows of the lake, grey as a lawyer and quite as patient, and she would have liked it, and told Odile so. At breakfast she sat at his elbow, because she had sat at his elbow at every breakfast of their lives and would not give the Ravenclaws the satisfaction of seeing her move, and when she wanted the salt, which stood at Caleb’s hand, she asked Ambrose Fennick for it.

Ambrose had not been told what was happening. He could feel it all the same, and he reached across Caleb for the salt with the face of a man putting his arm into a cage at a menagerie.

“Merci bien, monsieur,” Cecily told him warmly.

“Cecy, I am sitting beside the salt. I have been sitting beside it since the porridge. I should have been delighted to —”

“Mademoiselle Marchand.” Cecily leaned a little way down the table. “Would you be so kind as to tell my brother that I am not speaking to him?”

Odile looked at Caleb, and at Cecily, and went pink, and told him so in a whisper.

“Please thank Mademoiselle Marchand for me,” Caleb returned, “and tell her that I had begun to suspect it, and that she has broken it to me very gently, and that I shall bear it as well as a man can who has lost his sister and the honour of passing her the salt in the same morning.”

Odile, who had sung Cherubino before the Headmistress and made her cry, opened her mouth to pass it all on, found that she could remember barely half of it, and ate her egg instead.

It went on like that all through breakfast. Silence from Cecily was like a fire in a grate, which had to be kept up, and fed, and seen to at all hours, and she would tire of the trouble long before the week was out. What Caleb minded was her face. Once, when she thought he was looking at the ceiling, he saw her look down at his bare hands, where his gloves ought to have been and were not, because they were still in her pocket. Then she looked away very fast. He could not give her the reason, which was the one thing she wanted, and so he gave her the salt by way of Ambrose, and smiled at Odile, and kept his eyes off the far end of the Hall, since the rest of the Hall was looking there for him.

For a week Arian von Conti had stared at him across this room at every meal he came to, and Hogwarts had begun to grow used to it, as a household grows used to the draught under a door it has given up mending. This morning it could not. The Goblet had stood the two of them up before three schools the night before, and now Arian sat at the Durmstrang end of the Slytherin table with a cup of black coffee he had not touched and watched the champion of Beauxbatons down the length of the Hall as if he had been given leave. Half the Hall watched him do it. The other half watched Caleb, to see what he would do about it.

Caleb buttered his toast once, and only once, and was very proud of himself.

At the staff table Andrew got to his feet. He had his white travelling cloak over his grey robes and his parchments under his arm, and he came down the Hall to the Ravenclaw table as a man crosses a room at the Ministry to have a word with a colleague, nodding to the Headmistress and to Professor Dumbledore as he passed. He stopped behind his brother and sister and laid a hand on each of their shoulders, in front of three schools, which was not a thing Andrew did in front of anybody.

“I go to Paris by the ten o’clock Portkey,” he informed them. “The Committee sits until the eleventh.”

“Shall you be gone long, Andrew?” Cecily asked him sweetly, past Caleb’s ear.

“Until your birthday.” He looked down at Caleb. “I mean to read the rules again.”

“You wrote them, mon frère,” Cecily pointed out.

“That,” Andrew replied, “is why.”

He kissed Cecily’s forehead, and then, after a moment, Caleb’s too, and his hand stayed on Caleb’s shoulder a breath longer than it had stayed on hers. “À bientôt, both of you.” Then he straightened and looked once down the length of the Hall to the Durmstrang end of the Slytherin table, and Caleb, who had paced the thirty-eight yards to the Forest for him, watched him measure that distance too and find it a great deal too short. He went out with his clerks behind him, and the Hall turning on its benches to watch him go.

Caleb finished his toast. Then he stood and went out into the cold of the Entrance Hall without looking back.

The Goblet had been removed, since it had served its purpose and revealed the three champions for the Triwizard Tournament. Its stool stood empty on the flagstones inside the faint gold ring of the Age Line, which nobody had yet troubled to sweep away, and Caleb stepped over the line as he passed, out of habit, and felt nothing at all.

Behind him, in the Great Hall, a bench scraped.

He did not turn around. He had been followed out of that Hall every morning for a week, at the width of a ballroom, by a boy who waited until Caleb was halfway to the doors and seldom came nearer. Today the bench had gone back in the middle of the porridge, before three schools, and the step came out through the great doors close behind him, steady and unhurried, and he heard the noise of the Hall go down behind it as if somebody had put a lid on a pot.

The Headmistress had excused her champion from lessons so that he might prepare, and Caleb meant to prepare by seeing everything. He went up the marble staircase, and the step came up after him, a flight behind, and then nearer. When he turned along the first-floor corridor it turned too. It neither hurried nor hung back. Caleb walked past the suits of armour and the long windows full of white November light with his back as straight as Cecily’s, and every step he took he heard the other take, nearer than it had ever come by daylight.

At the cloister he stopped and turned around.

It was a square of old grey arches around a patch of grass gone white and stiff with frost. The low sun came in under the vaulting on one side only, and laid the shadow of every pillar across the flags like the bars of a gate. Somewhere overhead a bell was ringing Hogwarts in to its lessons, and in another minute there would be nobody here but the two of them.

He had crossed it on the Wednesday before with a book under his arm, when Arian von Conti had stood at the corner of it and watched him go by. Now there was no corner. Arian stood under the arches five or six feet away, watching him.

Five or six feet. On the shore he had walked around the fire to sit at Caleb’s elbow, but that had been on an empty shore with nobody to see it, and about the castle, in October, it had been a ballroom’s. Caleb looked at the distance, and then at the expressionless face above it, and then at the eyes, which were not expressionless at all.

“Good morning, monsieur.”

“Good morning.”

“You are following me.”

“Yes.”

He had not expected it to be admitted so readily. On the shore it had been answered with a look. This was offered up as a man offers up the day of the week.

Around the corner feet were going away, fast, and a girl’s laugh was cut off. By supper it would be in a letter to Dario von Conti. The boy in front of him did not look as if he had given Dario von Conti a thought since he got up, and even at five feet, in the draught off the lake, Caleb could feel the warmth coming off him, and found it very trying, because he had no idea where to put it.

He made up his mind to be gracious. It was what he had been taught, and it had never yet failed him. It had sent a Comte home from a conservatory with his camellia still in his hand, and a great many young men back to their own side of a ballroom, and every one of them had gone away pleased.

“Monsieur,” he began, with great kindness, “the cloister is a good deal wider than that. If you would be so good as to walk in another part of it, I think we might both enjoy the morning. You will see me perfectly well from the far side. I am told I am very easy to see.”

Arian bowed, briefly, and turned, and walked away across the cloister garth over the frosted grass to the arches on the far side, and stood there.

Caleb carried on his way.

He had gone two arches when he heard the step again. He did not look around. He went three arches more, and through the door at the end, and down a flight of steps to a broad stone passage hung with paintings of food, and at the painting of a bowl of fruit he turned, because he could not help it, and Arian von Conti was five or six feet behind him, as if the cloister had never happened.

He did not look sorry. He had been asked to walk in another part of the cloister, and he had, and the person who had asked him had since left the cloister. Caleb, who had been obeying Andrew to the letter for a week, knew the method when he saw it. He knew something else as well. The smile had held against every admirer in Paris, and against this one it had held for the length of a cloister.

Caleb tickled the pear, as a Hufflepuff had told him one did, and the painting swung open on the kitchens of Hogwarts, where a great many house-elves dropped what they were holding and bowed to the flagstones. He sat down at the end of a long scrubbed table under the copper pans with a cup of chocolate he had not asked for, and stirred three sugars into it without noticing.

“There is a young gentleman in the passage, monsieur,” the oldest of the elves whispered, when he had been to look. “Is he yours?”

“I believe so.”

“Is he going to stay outside?”

Caleb stirred a fourth sugar into his chocolate and found that it was no help.

“I have not the least idea, mon vieux,” he admitted. “I suspect that nobody has, and he least of all.”

*   *   *

Arian waited outside.

He stood with his back to the wall opposite the fruit bowl for two hours, with the cold of the stone coming through his robes and the smell of new bread coming out around the frame of the painting. Hogwarts students passed him in twos and threes on the way to their lessons, and walked faster when they saw him. A fat friar of a ghost came out of the wall at his shoulder, begged his pardon and went back the way he had come. On the other side of the painting the boy was asking the house-elves how one made brioche, first in French and then in English, and making them sound clever for knowing, and Arian stood against the wall and listened.

All week he had kept a ballroom’s length between them, in the open, where anybody could see it. This morning on the marble stair he had thought of Rocco in the doorway of every room the boy went into, and given the distance up a yard at a time, and by the cloister there had been five feet left.

Then the boy had turned around and asked him, kindly, to walk somewhere else.

He had gone at once, as he had gone around the fire on the shore, wanting to go before the voice had finished asking.

At eleven the Beauxbatons seniors came down the passage two by two, sent by the Headmistress to find her champion and set him to practising something, and every one of them dropped their eyes from Arian at once and then stole a second glance. The sister came among them, and she did not look away at all. She stopped in front of him and looked him over from his boots to his face, as an old woman in the fish market at Strelna looks over a pike before she will say what it is worth. Then she tickled the pear and went in without a word.

The boy came out a little after.

He saw Arian at once, and Arian could see that he had known all along that he would be there. He stopped on the threshold and drew a breath.

“Have you been standing there all morning, monsieur?”

“Since nine.”

“There are chairs in Scotland. I have seen several. The Ravenclaws assure me they are a great improvement on a wall.” He looked at Arian a moment longer, from under his lashes. “Though I grant you the wall has never looked so well.”

Arian had nothing to say to that. The boy sighed through his nose, as Enzo sighed when Mirek had spilled something, and went off down the passage with the seniors around him like a flock of anxious doves and his colour high.

Arian went after him.

He followed him to luncheon and stood at the doors of the Hall until the boy came out. Then he went back to the ship, because Durmstrang excused nobody, champion or not, and sat the afternoon’s lessons in the great cabin and was first in all of them without appearing to try, as he had been since he was eleven, which Rocco had never forgiven. The last of them was the Dark Arts, which the Institute set twice a week out of a book, as other schools set Charms. He was first in that too. It was the one first he would have given Rocco gladly. At three he was down the gangway again, and Rocco watched him go from the rail with his writing case under his arm.

Let him. Arian had stood where Rocco could see him since the morning after the ship came up out of the lake, and he was not going to begin hiding now. Let Rocco write it all down in his neat Petersburg hand and send it north by eagle owl, the wall and the cloister and the doors of the Hall. Dario would go for the boy whatever was written, and when he did, Arian meant to be standing beside him, and not a ballroom away.

That night he went ashore.

He went at half past eleven, down the gangway with his wand lit, past the round black eye of Vittorio’s porthole, and he kept his eyes off it. Twice in the week before the Goblet he had come up the lawn after the light and followed it through the dark castle, near enough for anybody to see. Tonight he would not follow it. He walked up the white lawn with his wand held up and stood at the foot of the great steps in the frost, and waited for it to come to him.

*   *   *

Cecily went to her bath at a quarter to midnight without a word to him. Caleb waited until he heard the taps run, then took his cloak and his wand and let himself out, and had got as far as the foot of the carriage steps before he saw the light.

It was at the top of the lawn, at the foot of the castle steps, a single wandlight held up at the height of a man’s shoulder and quite still, like a lamp set in a window to show a traveller the road. It stayed where it was when he came out, and all the while he crossed the lawn towards it, with the frost crackling under his boots and his own light swinging, and when he was near enough to see the face behind it, it was the face he had known it would be.

“Bonsoir, monsieur.”

“Good evening.”

Caleb stopped at the foot of the steps and looked at him. Twice last week he had heard that step behind him in the dark, a flight down, a corridor back. Now the boy stood in front of him on the frozen gravel at the foot of the steps of Hogwarts, at midnight, with his wand lit, where anybody at any window in the castle might look down and see him, and he looked as if he had been there some while and would have stayed until morning if nobody came.

“You are in front of me tonight, monsieur. Not behind.”

“Tonight, yes.”

“May I ask why?”

Arian gave him no answer. He only stood there with the light held up, and it came to Caleb, slowly, that he was waiting to be told which way.

“My brother has forbidden me every part of this castle that is not lit,” Caleb reminded him. “I suppose you mean to be the lamp.”

Arian looked down at the wand in his hand, and then at Caleb, and lifted it a little, gravely, so that the light went up the steps ahead of them both.

Caleb laughed before he could stop it.

He had come across the lawn meaning to be distant, as his grandmother would have been, or Andrew. But the boy had lifted his wand to light the stair as gravely as a footman lifting a candelabrum in a great house, with nothing whatever in his face, and Caleb could not help it.

“Bien,” he said, when he could. “Then you may light the way to the seventh floor, monsieur, since you have seen the trolls already and may as well see how the story ends. You will walk behind me. At a respectable distance.”

The castle at midnight was all cold stone and held breath. Their two lights went up the marble staircase ahead of them and woke the gilt on the picture frames one at a time, and the portraits muttered and turned over in their sleep. There was a caretaker in this castle with a lantern and a cat, and a rule against students on the stairs after hours, and Caleb, who was a guest and a champion and a de Blanchemer, was breaking it with a von Conti at his back.

Arian walked behind him at a respectable distance for the whole of one flight.

On the second he was beside him. Caleb did not see him come. He was simply there at the turn of the stair, at Caleb’s left shoulder, between Caleb and the long dark drop of the stairwell, with his wand held up, and the heat came off him in the cold stair as it comes off a hunter that has been ridden hard and stands steaming in a frozen yard.

“Monsieur.”

Arian dropped back a step.

On the third flight he was beside him again, and by the fourth he was so near that the fur of his cloak brushed Caleb’s sleeve at every step, and Caleb could hear him breathe. Caleb, who had been brought up to keep a correct distance from everybody in the world who did not bear his name, and had kept it gracefully since he could walk, stopped dead on the fourth-floor landing under the witch in curl papers and turned around, and found that turning around had put him very nearly against the boy’s chest.

He did not step back. He tipped his head up instead, because he had to, and looked into the face above him in the wandlight.

“There is room on this staircase, monsieur, for a carriage and four, and two outriders, and very possibly an orchestra,” he said, “and you have chosen to walk in my pocket.”

It was a remark that would have sent any young man in Paris back three steps, laughing. Arian did not laugh, and he did not step back. He stood over him, so close that Caleb felt the warmth of his breath on his mouth, and his eyes went once, quite deliberately, to Caleb’s mouth and stayed there, and Caleb felt it as if a thumb had been laid on his lower lip.

The witch in curl papers put her hand to her own mouth.

Arian held his ground, with his wand held up beside them both and the light of it on Caleb’s face, as if he would stand there until the witch in curl papers had grown old in her frame. It was Caleb who moved. He felt a smile get away from him before he could catch it, and turned around sharply and went on up, with his face hot and his heart going as if he had run every flight.

“I asked you whether I ought to scream, dear,” the witch in curl papers whispered after him. “A week ago tonight. You said not.”

“I still say not, madame,” Caleb called back. “It would only encourage him.”

He reached the seventh floor with Arian a flight behind him, and then half a flight, and then at his shoulder again, because there seemed to be nothing in the world that could keep the boy from his side for longer than a staircase. The corridor was long and cold, and halfway down it hung the tapestry. Caleb held up his wand to it and Arian held up his, and between the two lights the wizard in the nightcap came up out of the dark with his long legs and his little fiddle, teaching the trolls to dance.

They were at the waltz. The largest troll stood at the end of the line with her club laid on the grass beside her and a garland of moth-eaten wool flowers around her neck, and she was doing her best, one heavy foot and then the other, and lowering her great head to the dancing master on the turn. The dancing master was occupied with a troll at the other end of the line who had trodden on her own foot.

“There,” Caleb breathed. “You see? He has still not noticed. She will have to tell him, I think. Men in nightcaps never see anything unless it is put into their hands.” He watched her go around. “I cannot think why she wants him. He has very thin legs.”

He turned his head, meaning to share the joke, and found that Arian was not looking at the tapestry.

He stood with his wand held up to light a tapestry he was not watching, and the light lay on half his face and left the other half in the dark, and in the lit half his eyes were on Caleb, on his mouth and the line of his jaw and the open collar of his robes, as if Caleb were the tapestry, and the lesson, and the whole of the waltz.

Caleb looked back at the troll. The troll curtsied. The dancing master tuned his fiddle.

“She is very patient,” he told the wizard in the nightcap, a little unsteadily. “It is a quality I admire in a troll.”

Arian made no answer. But he stood beside him all the while the trolls went around, with the heat coming off him and his light held up, and he did not spare the tapestry a glance, and Caleb did not look at him once either, which was a great deal harder than it ought to have been.

At the carriage, a long while after, the frost had got onto the little gilt steps, and on the second of them Caleb’s heel went out from under him.

He did not fall. There was a hand at his waist before he could, spread wide and hot across his side through his cloak and his robes, and another at his elbow, and he was set upright on the step as easily as a cup is set back in its saucer. The hands stayed where they were. The second step had brought Caleb’s eyes nearly level with the boy’s. He could feel the heat of the palm at his waist, and then the fingers tightened there, once.

“You may let go of me, monsieur,” he said, rather faintly. “I have stopped falling.”

Arian let go. He took the hand away slowly, and stood back on the frozen grass.

Caleb found his voice where he had left it, somewhere about the fourth floor.

“Tomorrow night, monsieur, you will stay on your ship.”

Arian held his gaze.

“I am perfectly able to find my own way about a castle,” Caleb told him. “Bonne nuit, monsieur.”

“Spokoynoy nochi.”

Arian inclined his head to him as Caleb had once inclined his across the Great Hall, no deeper and no less deep, and went away down the lawn to the lake with his wand lit, and never once looked back. Caleb climbed up the steps very carefully, and found that his hands were cold and his face was not, and that his side, where the hand had been, was warmer than all the rest of him put together.

On Wednesday night, at a quarter to twelve, he let himself out of the carriage, and the light was there again at the foot of the castle steps, as still as before.

Caleb stood on the frozen grass and looked at it for some time. Then he went up the lawn to it.

“I told you to stay on your ship, monsieur, and yet here you are.”

“You told me.”

“Why?”

Arian did not answer. He stared at him instead, as he had on the shore when Caleb asked what he wanted of him, and it was the same answer, and Caleb felt it go up his neck and into his face in the frost, and was furious with it, and could do nothing about it at all.

He had given an order, plainly, in the voice that footmen and Ministers obeyed, and it had been heard and set aside. Nobody had ever done that to him.

He looked at the light, and at the boy behind it, and gave up.

“If you are going to follow me, monsieur, you may as well carry the lamp.”

Arian lifted the wand an inch, as he had on Tuesday, and waited to be told where.

“The kitchens,” Caleb decided. “I promised the elves I would come back for the brioche, and I keep my promises.”

At midnight the kitchens of Hogwarts were wide awake. The ranges stood open and roaring, and the long scrubbed tables were white with flour, and a great many house-elves were making the morning’s bread with their sleeves rolled up past the elbow. They dropped everything when the pear swung open and bowed to the flagstones, and then the oldest of them saw who was standing behind Caleb with a lit wand, and put both hands over his mouth.

“It is the young gentleman from the passage,” he whispered. “The one who stood.”

“It is,” Caleb agreed. “He has come in out of the cold at last, mon vieux. I told you he would.”

They were on Arian before he had got his wand down. They tried to take his cloak and were refused, and took his elbow instead, and sat him at the end of the long table under the copper pans, and before Caleb had unwound his scarf there was a plate in front of him with six jam tarts on it, too hot to touch, and a jug of milk, and an elf at either side of him, waiting.

Arian considered the tarts. Then he turned his eyes across the table to Caleb, as a man turns to a friend who has walked him into an ambush.

“They wish you to eat them,” Caleb explained.

“Why?”

“Because you stood two hours against their wall and nobody fed you, and they have thought of nothing else since. It has wounded them.” Caleb accepted the inevitable cup of chocolate. “It is no use telling them that you are not hungry. I tried it yesterday. They brought me a second cup.”

Arian ate a tart. The elves watched every bite of it go, and when it was gone the oldest of them put another on the plate in its place, and Arian eyed it as if it had laid an egg.

Caleb had been given a tart of his own and had eaten it in three bites while he talked, and without thinking he licked the jam and the sugar off his fingers one after another, as he had done at the kitchen table since he was four. Across the table the light jerked. Arian’s wand had very nearly gone out of his hand, and the wandlight swung up the copper pans and down again, and when Caleb looked up Arian was staring at his mouth, and at his finger.

Caleb took his finger away from his mouth. He had gone scarlet to the ears in front of forty house-elves. Then, because he was a de Blanchemer, and would not be put out of countenance in a kitchen by anybody, he licked the last of the sugar off his thumb as well, rather more slowly, with his eyes on the boy across the table, and watched the wandlight jerk a second time.

He had not known he could do that. He put it away to think about later.

“They will go on,” he managed at last, “until you stop them.”

“How does one stop them?”

“One does not. One thanks them, and takes the last one away in a napkin, and comes back on Sunday.” He was laughing into his chocolate by then, and could not help it, because the champion of Durmstrang was sitting at a kitchen table with flour on his sleeve and a house-elf at either elbow, holding a jam tart in both hands like something he had been told not to drop, and he looked as if he would sooner have faced the dragon in the Forest. “You might begin with merci. They like merci. It sounds expensive.”

Arian turned to the elf at his elbow, who came up to the edge of the table and was holding the milk jug up to him with both arms.

“Merci,” he told it.

The elf burst into tears of joy and had to be led away by two others and given a dishcloth to cry into, and Caleb laughed so hard that he had to put his cup down. When they left, the last tart went with them in a napkin, and Arian put it away inside his robes as carefully as he had put away a leaf on the shore.

“I am going up to the top of the Astronomy Tower,” Caleb announced in the passage, with dignity, “to look at the stars. You may light the stairs.”

Arian carried the lamp up the Astronomy Tower, and he carried it well. Caleb noticed it by the second turn and could not stop noticing it. The light was always a step ahead of him, on the stair he was about to take and never the one he was on. Where the old stone had worn into a dip, it lay in the dip. Where a step had gone altogether on the fourth turn, it lay on the gap until Caleb was over it. Caleb put his hand on Arian’s arm to cross, without thinking, and felt the arm go hard as iron under the fur, and the light shake once and steady. He took his hand away on the far side. He could feel the heat of that arm in his palm for three more turns of the stair, and he did not once have to look for his feet.

“You have done this before,” he said.

“For my uncle.”

“Does your uncle go up a great many towers at night?”

“He reads late. The stairs in his house have no lamps.”

Caleb talked, since Arian plainly would not, about the tower at Blanchemer, which had a hundred and nine steps and a peacock at the top that had gone up in the spring of 1971 and declined ever to come down, and about Andrew, who had once been locked in it for an afternoon by his little brother and had come out and written a memorandum. Arian said nothing to any of it.

The stair let them out under the open sky. The top of the Astronomy Tower was a circle of bare flagstones inside a waist-high parapet, with the brass telescopes standing about it on their tripods and nothing over it but the stars. It was the highest place in the castle. The wind came across it straight off the mountains, and the lake lay a long way down, black and still.

Caleb went to the parapet, and asked him which of the stars was which, and this time he answered.

“That one.” He lifted the wand towards the north, where a single star stood by itself over the Forest. “At home they call it the Stake. The sky is tied to it, and goes around, like a horse on a rope.”

“And the horse never gets loose?”

“Not yet.”

Caleb laughed, and looked up at the Stake for some time, and at the whole sky going patiently around it, and when he looked down again Arian’s eyes were on him.

He stood at the parapet with the wand held low, so that it should not spoil the stars for anybody, and he gazed at Caleb as if Caleb had hung every one of them.

“You are not looking at the stars, monsieur.”

“I have seen them.”

The wind came over the parapet and blew Caleb’s collar open, and Arian’s hand was there before his own. He drew the two edges together at Caleb’s throat and held them shut in his fist, with his knuckles against the bare skin under Caleb’s jaw. They were very warm. Caleb’s pulse beat against them, hard and quick, giving him away, and he knew that Arian could feel it, because he heard him stop breathing.

“I was going to tell you something about the horse, monsieur,” Caleb managed.

Arian waited, his hand still at Caleb’s throat.

“I have quite forgotten what it was.”

He had been brought up to carry a conversation for two at any table in France, and he could not find one word more.

The fist at his collar opened. The hand went up along his jaw and stayed there, with the palm under his ear and the fingers in his curls, and tipped his face up.

Caleb knew what was coming, and he knew that he could stop it. He had learned on the shore that a word from him would send this boy to the far side of any fire in Scotland, and he had the words ready. One does not, monsieur.

He did not say it.

Arian bent his head and kissed him. His mouth was hot in the cold wind, and not in the least practised, and it did not hesitate. Caleb’s back met the parapet. The stone was cold through his cloak and Arian was warm against the whole front of him, and for a moment he stood between the two with his eyes wide open.

Nobody had ever kissed him. He had been kissed on both cheeks all his life by everybody who had the right, and on the hand by a great many who had not, and nobody had come nearer than that, because he had never let them.

His hand came up and closed around Arian’s wrist. He meant to take the hand from his face. He held it there instead, with the pulse going under his fingers as hard as his own, and shut his eyes, and kissed Arian von Conti back, and somewhere over their heads the horse got loose.

He had no idea how it was done, and it did not seem to matter. His lips parted, and the hand at his jaw tightened, and Arian made a low sound against his mouth that went through Caleb from his throat to his knees. He heard himself answer it, and was scandalised, and did not stop.

When Arian lifted his head it was only by an inch. He kept his hand at Caleb’s jaw. His breath came short against Caleb’s mouth, and his eyes were very black, and his face was as still as it ever was, and under Caleb’s fingers his wrist was shaking.

Beside them the brass of the old telescope had gone white with frost from one end to the other.

Caleb let go of the wrist. He had to tell his fingers twice.

“There is a time,” he said, “for such things.”

Arian’s eyes did not leave his face.

“And a place.” Once he had begun, Caleb could not stop. It came out of him in a tumble, with his back against the parapet and his face burning and that hand still at his jaw. “This is neither, monsieur. It is not in the least the place. It is the top of the Astronomy Tower, at one o’clock in the morning, in a wind. Pas devant les étoiles. One does not simply — not without a word, and certainly not when a person has only come up to look at the sky and has been told the name of one star. There is an order to these things. One waits for the proper time, and the proper place, and then one —”

“Yes,” Arian agreed, and his eyes dropped to Caleb’s mouth.

Caleb stopped.

He had been on the point of saying what the proper time was, and the proper place, and found he had not the least idea. He had been taught the order of precedence at a dinner of forty, and how to address a cardinal, a goblin and a dowager duchess, and at what hour one might decently call, and nobody had ever told him where one was meant to be kissed. Mon Dieu. The only proper place he could think of was a ballroom, which was how it had been done in every story Maman had ever told about Papa.

“Not here,” he concluded, with what dignity he could find. “I am quite sure of that. On a terrace, perhaps. At a ball, after the third waltz, with the band still playing.” He drew his cloak about him, since the hand at his jaw did not seem to be going anywhere and the cloak at least would do as it was told. “I do not suppose Durmstrang troubles itself with balls, monsieur.”

Arian did not answer. His thumb moved once along Caleb’s jaw, from the ear towards the chin, and then the hand came away, slowly, and the wind came into the place where it had been.

Caleb, who had not expected an answer, took the silence for a Durmstrang yes, and went to the stair with his face burning, and was grateful for the dark.

Arian lit the stair down as he had lit it up, a step ahead, in the dip and on the gap. On the fourth turn, where the step had gone, Caleb had no need to put his hand on Arian’s arm. Arian’s hand came back for his without a word and took it, bare in bare, and drew him over the gap and down the next three turns after it, and held on until the door at the foot.

All the way down the lawn Caleb could not find one thing to say in any of his languages.

At the carriage he stopped on the second step, as he had stopped on Tuesday, which brought his eyes nearly level with the boy’s, and turned around. He had meant to say tomorrow night, monsieur, you will stay on your ship. He had meant it all the way down the tower. It would not come.

“Bonne nuit, monsieur.”

Arian searched his face a moment longer, waiting for the rest of it. When it did not come either, he put his hand over Caleb’s on the gilt handle of the door behind him, where Caleb had reached for it, so that it could not be turned, and leaned in, and kissed him again.

It was short, and rough, and the handle went into Caleb’s back under both their hands. Cecily was asleep on the other side of that door, and the Headmistress not far beyond her, and Caleb did not make a sound. Caleb’s other hand went up of its own accord and took hold of the clasp of Arian’s cloak. Then Arian straightened, and let go of the handle, and gazed down at Caleb’s fingers on his clasp until Caleb remembered whose they were and let go.

“Do zavtra.”

He went away down the lawn to the lake with his wand lit. Caleb went in and shut the door and stood against it in the dark of the carriage, with his mouth burning and the cold of the floor coming up through his boots, and listened to his sister breathing behind her blue curtain, and went to bed without lighting the lamp, so that nobody should see his face.

*   *   *

On Thursday morning there was a letter from Dario.

It came at breakfast in the Great Hall, by the same eagle owl as before, a great tawny bird with eyes like two coins at the bottom of a well, and Rocco took it from its leg and broke the seal and read it aloud, as he always did.

“‘Rocco writes that the Goblet has chosen my little brother. Father is pleased, which I am sure will be a great comfort to him.’” Rocco paused to let Vittorio laugh, and Vittorio laughed. “‘Rocco writes also that our champion walks about the English castle behind the French boy in broad daylight, like a footman carrying his parcels. I find I do not mind it. I find I am curious. But remind our champion, cousin, whose name he carries, and see that he is kept well occupied.’”

He lowered the page and smiled down the table, and turned to the head of it.

“Gospodin direktor. Sir. My cousin Dario has written to ask a kindness of you. He thinks a champion must be hardened, or he grows soft and shames us before the English and the French. He asks that our champion stand the middle watch, every night.”

There had been no such request in the letter. Arian had heard every word of it, and so had Vittorio, who sniggered into his kasha and turned it into a cough when the Headmaster looked up.

The Headmaster listened. He was a lean old man with a silver beard clipped close to the jaw, and he had been at school with Arian’s father and had come out of it with the same opinions. When Rocco had finished, he nodded slowly over his kasha.

“Khorosho. Well put. The champion will stand the middle watch. From tonight.”

Nobody looked at Arian. When one von Conti moved against another, Durmstrang took care not to be seen watching, and the seventh-years down the table looked at their bowls, and only Mirek, at Arian’s right hand, looked at him, because he had not yet learned not to.

Enzo’s place was empty. He had gone to Petersburg at first light on Wednesday on the Institute’s business, and was not expected back for ten days. That was why Rocco had chosen this week, and why nobody at the table would say a word.

“Yes, sir,” said Arian, and ate his bread.

It was Rocco’s, all of it, with Dario’s name put on it so that it would weigh more. The middle watch ran from midnight until four, the coldest and the longest and, on a ship at anchor in a Scottish lake, the most useless. But at a quarter to midnight the carriage door opened, and the light came down the little steps and went up the lawn, and two nights running Rocco had watched Arian go ashore to meet it. Now he had found the one hour of the day when Arian could not be beside it.

Nobody asked whether a champion who stood four hours on a frozen deck every night would be fit for the first task at the end of the month, the Headmaster least of all. A Conti had asked him. At the end of the table old Master Halvorsen of Arithmancy looked at his plate. In Dario’s fourth year he had marked Dario down in front of the class, and the next morning his raven, which had sat on his shoulder for thirty years, was found on his desk with its neck wrung, laid neatly across Dario’s essay, and the mark corrected in red ink to full marks. Nothing had ever been proved. Halvorsen had not marked a Conti down since. The Contis had outlasted every headmaster Durmstrang had ever had, and whether Durmstrang won its Tournament was nothing to them. Dario would like it best if Arian lost, and lost to a Blanchemer, and went into the family book as the Conti who was beaten by a Blanchemer at a schoolboys’ tournament.

None of it was anything Arian had not expected. Dario had hated him since the hunt, and the bear, when Arian was five, and he had been used to it ever since, as one is used to weather. He did not want Chernoyar, or the name, or anything else that was Dario’s. He wanted only to be let alone.

He thought of the light going up through the dark castle with nobody beside it.

He had kissed the boy on the top of the Astronomy Tower seven hours before, and again against the door of the carriage, and he could still feel the boy’s fingers shut around his wrist, holding the hand where it was. Rocco did not know it, and had asked for the night all the same.

Arian drank his tea. Rocco watched him drink it, as a man watches a fuse he has lit, and got nothing for his trouble.

“You will have so much time to think, cousin,” he murmured. “Four hours, every night, with nothing to look at but the lake. I envy you.”

*   *   *

Andrew’s first letter from Paris came that same morning, which meant that he had written it on Tuesday night, within hours of arriving, and it ran to two sheets.

It began beautifully. Andrew’s hand at the head of a letter was the hand of a man who had been taught by a writing master at Blanchemer to make every capital as if it were going to be framed, and for a paragraph it was all very well. He had arrived. The Committee had sat. The Committee had been informed that the champion for Beauxbatons was his brother, and had been very understanding about it, and had declined in three separate motions to do anything about it. Here his handwriting began to lean.

“‘I have consulted the rules,’” Caleb read under his breath to his sister, since she was not speaking to him and had to be read to by somebody. “‘I have consulted them a great many times. I asked the Committee this afternoon whether a champion might be withdrawn on the grounds of being my brother, and they say not. I have asked them to minute that I asked. You are participating.’” He turned the sheet. The hand here had gone downhill like a cart with no brake. “‘You will keep up your Flame-Freezing Charm. I do not say why. I am not permitted to say why, and you will not ask me. You will keep it up in general, and in November in particular, and then you will burn this letter.’”

Cecily put down her spoon.

“Is he allowed to tell you that?” she asked the jug.

“He has not told me anything, madame,” Caleb assured the jug. “He has told me to keep up a charm, which any brother might tell another at any season of the year.” He folded the letter in three and put it away with the others. “It is only that he has underlined November three times, and very nearly gone through the parchment.”

An hour behind his letter, Andrew came down the marble staircase himself.

Caleb was crossing the Entrance Hall with the letter over his heart when a hand took his.

It took it without asking, as it had taken it on the tower stair, bare and hot, and Caleb, who had come out of breakfast meaning to say something extremely composed about the night before, found himself drawn out of the grey morning light of the Hall and in under the marble staircase, into the deep shadow behind it, where the stone of the stair went up overhead in a long cold slope and somebody kept the brooms. Arian von Conti had left the Slytherin table before the porridge was cleared, and had walked five feet behind Caleb all the way across the Hall.

“Monsieur,” Caleb began, “there are two Hufflepuffs —”

Arian kissed him.

He did it against the underside of the marble staircase, where the stair came down lowest, with Caleb’s shoulders to the slope of the stone, so that Caleb was shut in between the stair and the heat of him and could see nothing of the Entrance Hall at all.

This kiss was different. It was harder, and it went deeper, as if Arian meant to make a meal of him, which was unreasonable in a person who had come straight from breakfast. He had both hands at Caleb’s neck, with his thumbs under Caleb’s jaw, so that Caleb could not turn his head or draw it back, and he could not get a whole breath. It had never happened to him in seventeen years. He wondered whether this was what drowning was like. Then he got hold of the front of the red robes in his fist, and kissed him back, and forgot the Hufflepuffs.

Over their heads somebody came down the marble staircase.

Caleb recognised the steps immediately. Nobody else in the world came down a flight of stairs as if it were the first item on an agenda. The step came down directly over his head, step by step along the stone at his back, and Caleb went rigid from his heels to his hair, and Arian lifted his head and fixed his eyes on the underside of the stair as if he could see through it, and did not take his hands from Caleb’s neck.

“That is my brother,” Caleb breathed. “That is not possible. My brother is in Paris.”

Arian’s eyes came back to him. He stayed where he was, and Caleb had to duck out from between his hands and go out into the light of the Hall by himself, with his mouth burning and the letter crackling over his heart, and come around the newel post as Andrew reached the foot of the stair.

Andrew had soot on the collar of his white travelling cloak and a smut along one cheekbone, and a Committee paper in his hand. He had plainly come through the Headmaster’s fireplace from Paris and not stopped to be brushed.

“You have had my letter.”

“At breakfast, Andrew.” Caleb looked at the soot, because it was the only part of his brother he could bear to look at. “You wrote it from Paris.”

“I am in Paris. The Committee has adjourned for an hour.” Andrew held out his hand. “Have you burned it?”

“I have it by heart.”

“That is not what I asked.”

Caleb gave it up. Andrew touched the tip of his wand to one corner and held the letter while it burned, there on the flagstones of the Entrance Hall, before two Hufflepuffs and a portrait of a monk, as composedly as if he were sealing a treaty. Then he let the last of it fall, and looked past Caleb’s shoulder, and stopped.

Arian von Conti had come out from under the marble staircase behind Caleb, unhurried, and stood at Caleb’s shoulder, where he had no business whatever to be, and did not look as if he had ever in his life considered standing anywhere else.

Andrew looked at him. It was the look he kept for a delegation that had arrived a day early and expected a luncheon. It had sent ambassadors home before luncheon.

“Monsieur von —”

“Monsieur.” A clerk had come down the marble staircase behind him two steps at a time, sooty to the knees, with a second Committee paper and the face of a young man who had been promised that the hour would be twenty minutes. “Monsieur, the Bulgarians have withdrawn the amendment. The Minister asks —”

Andrew did not take his eyes off the boy at his brother’s shoulder.

“Keep up the charm,” he told Caleb. “In general. And you will wear your —”

But the clerk already had him by the elbow, and he was borne up the staircase mid-word, and the end of his sentence vanished with him around the turn at the top.

Caleb looked at the empty stair, and then down at the grey curl of ash on the flags that had been his letter.

“Flannel,” he finished for his brother.

When he turned around, the corner of Arian’s mouth had moved, a very little.

“That was my brother, monsieur. He is in Paris,” Caleb added, since it seemed to bear repeating.

Arian’s eyes went to the empty stair where Andrew had been, and then back to Caleb, and the corner of his mouth moved again.

Caleb did not smile back. He could still feel two hands at his neck, and his heart had not yet come down off the stair.

“You did not let go.” It came out lower than he meant, and not in the least charming. “My brother was over our heads, monsieur, and you did not let go. Had he come around that post a moment sooner, he would have had you sent home in your own ship and me to Paris behind you.”

Arian considered it.

“He did not come sooner.”

“That is not an answer.”

“Let him see,” said Arian. “I would not have let go.”

He said it as he had said Yes in the cloister, as a plain fact, with nothing in it to be sorry for. Caleb had a reply ready for everything, and he had none for that. He turned on his heel and went up the marble staircase with his colour high, and for once he could not tell whether the step that came after him was a thing he wanted or a thing he ought to be afraid of.

Caleb had already seen what was in the Forest, from the end of his berth with his breath on the glass, and he knew perfectly well why one kept up a Flame-Freezing Charm in November. That afternoon he went down to the edge of the lake and built a bonfire of beech leaves, and put his hand into the heart of it. The charm held, as charms generally did for Caleb, and he felt nothing but a warm wind, and after that it was a game. He drew the burning leaves out one at a time and sent them spinning away over the water like little lanterns, and then he did it with his eyes shut. Cecily came along the path above with Odile, and looked down at the bonfire, and at her brother with his arm in it to the elbow, and missed a step, and went on.

At the end of the paddock rail Arian von Conti stood with his hands at his sides and watched him do it.

At a quarter to twelve Caleb let himself out, and the foot of the steps was dark.

He stood on the carriage steps and looked up the lawn for some time, at the frost and the castle going up black into the stars. A guest who had been asked to stay on his ship, and had stayed on it at last, had done no more than was proper and was to be commended. A person who had been kissed on the top of a tower, and against his own door, and under a staircase with his brother coming down it, all inside twelve hours, might reasonably be grateful for a quiet night. Caleb told himself both of these things, standing on the step, and was grateful for neither. He thought of what he had said under the staircase, and how it had sounded, and wondered whether a boy who went around a fire the moment he was asked had this time been asked too sharply. Then he went up the lawn alone, and up through the castle by his own ways, and it was very large, and very quiet, and he enjoyed it not half as much as he had in October.

On the fifth floor he stopped at the long window over the lake.

The ship lay in the middle of the water, black on black, with the stars around its masts. There was a lantern at its rail near the bow. It was too far to see who stood beside it. He could see only the light, and the shadow of somebody at the rail, quite still, looking out across the water towards the castle, and he stood at the window and looked back at it for much longer than he had meant to.

*   *   *

From the rail Arian had watched the carriage door open at a quarter to twelve, and the light come down the little steps and stand a moment on the grass, turned up the lawn towards the foot of the castle steps, where there was nobody, for a long time. Then it had gone up the lawn alone. On the fifth floor, in the long window over the lake, it stopped, and stood there so long that he began to think it had been set down on the sill and forgotten. At ten past one it came down again, slower, and the carriage door shut on it.

At four a sixth-year came up yawning to relieve him, and wished him spokoynoy nochi, a good night, with four hours of it already gone. Arian went below and found his hammock soaked through, blankets and all, with lake water that had not yet had time to freeze, and across the berth deck Vittorio asleep in his own with an empty bucket beneath it. He did not wake him. He slept on deck in his cloak until the grey came, as he had on Halloween, and the next night it was the same, and the night after.

On the fourth morning Mirek found the bucket. He stood over Vittorio’s hammock with it in both hands, scarlet and shaking, and called him a word no first-year ought to have known, and Arian took the bucket from him. “Davai. Breakfast.” He had the child off the berth deck before Vittorio was awake enough to have heard any of it.

*   *   *

The foot of the steps was dark on the Friday night, and on the Saturday.

By day it was another matter. Caleb spent his days as he had spent them in every great house he had ever been a guest in, finding out where everything was kept, and except for the two hours after luncheon when Durmstrang had its lessons, he could not cross a courtyard without Arian von Conti at the end of it. He was in the passage outside the kitchens, and outside the steamed glass of the greenhouses, and at the foot of the Owlery stair, and in the stands of the Quidditch pitch at seven in the morning, where Caleb had gone to applaud every turnip Rhiannon Hale stopped. He was at the end of the boathouse jetty, and under the trees at the edge of the Forest, and at the end of the paddock rail, and at the end of the library stacks, and he was never more than five feet off, and often a good deal less.

On the Friday morning Caleb taught the suits of armour along the first-floor corridor to bow to his sister. It took him a quarter of an hour and a charm nobody had ever set him, and when Cecily came along the corridor from breakfast every helmet dipped to her in turn, with a creak like a row of old gentlemen rising for a lady. She walked the whole length of them with her chin up and her colour high, but said not a word. At the far end Arian von Conti, who had watched Caleb do it, studied the armour, and then Caleb, as a man might study a card trick done by a bishop.

An hour later a Gryffindor sixth-year stopped Caleb in the cloister, pink to the ears, with a sonnet he had written and a hope that he might read it aloud. Arian came across the garth and stood at Caleb’s shoulder, and laid his hand flat at the small of Caleb’s back, in the middle of the cloister, in the middle of the morning, and stared at the Gryffindor. He only stared, but the Gryffindor took a step back, and then another, and suddenly remembered that he was wanted somewhere else.

“That was very likely a dreadful sonnet,” Caleb informed the shoulder beside him, “and I was looking forward to it enormously. You might at least pretend to be sorry, monsieur.”

“I am not sorry.”

“No. I had gathered as much.” Caleb turned to face him, which brought the hand around to his side, where it stayed. “He wished to read to me. It was mine to hear or to refuse, and I have been refusing young men with sonnets since I was fourteen, very kindly, without anybody’s help. One does not send away another person’s callers.”

A line came between Arian’s brows. He looked less angry than puzzled, like a boy who has been set a sum in a language he does not have.

“He wanted you.”

“A great many people do, monsieur. It has never yet been any affair of yours.”

“It is now.”

Caleb had once turned a Comte out of a conservatory with nothing but a raised eyebrow. He raised it. Arian looked at the eyebrow, and at him, and did not take his hand away, and Caleb understood that there was nothing in the whole of his armoury that this boy would know for a door being shut.

He stepped out from under the hand himself, since nobody was going to take it away for him, and walked off down the cloister with his chin up. He had gone the whole length of it before he found that he could not tell whether he was offended or pleased, and that not knowing made him crosser than either.

In the afternoon, when Durmstrang had gone back aboard for its lessons and come off again, Caleb went down to the edge of the Forest with two rolls in his pocket, because the squid had sulked since Wednesday and he thought the Forest might be more grateful.

It was. He had gone a few yards in under the first black pines, where the frost had not reached and the needles lay soft and brown, when something took the roll out of his hand.

He did not see it go. One moment the roll was in his fingers and the next it was not, and there was a breath on his wrist, warm and damp and enormous, and a soft snort such as a horse gives when it has found an apple in a pocket and means to see whether there are more.

Caleb stood still. Then he took the second roll out of his other pocket and broke it in half and held it out on his flat palm, as one holds out sugar to a mare. It went. A mouth lipped it off his hand, leathery and cool and very delicate, and the breath came again over his fingers, and he laughed out loud in the empty wood.

“Merci, madame. Or monsieur. I cannot see which, and it would be rude to guess.”

“Madame.”

Arian stood a few yards off among the pines, looking, for once, past Caleb at the empty air beside his shoulder, where the breath was.

“You can see her,” Caleb said.

Arian nodded once.

“What is she?”

“A thestral. There are a dozen of them. They come to the edge of the trees in the afternoons.”

Caleb broke off another piece of bread. It left his fingers as the first had, and the breath went over his knuckles and stayed there, patient, waiting for the next.

“What do they look like?”

“Like nothing you would want to see.”

He said it low and flat, as if he were reading it off a stone, and Caleb looked at the bread in his hand, and at the boy among the pines, and at the place in the air where something enormous and invisible was breathing on his fingers.

“Then I shall not ask,” he said, and gave her the rest of the roll.

He fed her the crusts, which she preferred, as the squid did, and told her that she had beautiful manners, and when the bread was gone she blew once more into his palm to be sure of it and went away into the trees. He heard her go, a long soft sound like a sheet being shaken out.

Arian had not moved. When Caleb came out from under the pines he fell in beside him, a step to the side where the path was roughest, and they went up the frozen lawn together without a word. Halfway to the castle Caleb looked down and found that he was still holding his hand a little away from his side, palm up, and that Arian’s eyes were on it too. He closed it. Arian looked at him then, once.

Caleb did not ask why the champion of Durmstrang should see what he could not.

At supper Jean-Baptiste Roux, who sang the baritone in all Odile’s duets and had appointed himself the champion’s conscience, leaned across Odile to say what the seniors had been saying all week.

“Rhiannon Hale has been out on the Quidditch pitch every morning with half of Hufflepuff throwing things at her, so that she may practise her shield charms. The Headmistress excused you from lessons so that you might do the same. And you, Caleb, have spent the week with the owls and the house-elves.”

“And the squid,” Caleb reminded him. “You must not forget the squid.”

“We set up twelve targets for you on the lawn, Caleb.”

“And they danced charmingly. You must allow that they danced charmingly.”

They had. On the Wednesday Caleb had strolled past the targets the seniors had painted for him and touched his wand once to the nearest, and all twelve had bowed to one another, paired off and waltzed away down the lawn in perfect three-four time, and the last of them had not been fished out of the lake until supper. The Headmistress had watched it from the carriage window with one hand at her throat. From the deck of the ship Arian von Conti had watched it too, and the first-year in the fur cap beside him had laughed out loud for both of them.

“The Headmistress asked me this afternoon what, precisely, you were preparing.”

“The house, mon cher. I am learning it.” Caleb reached for the bread. “Grand-mère says that the first duty of a guest is to know where everything is kept, and the second is to compliment the cook. I have done both.”

“And Odile tells me,” Jean-Baptiste went on, lower, “that when von Conti stands at the end of the stacks, you cannot finish a sentence. I have known you for seven years, Caleb. I once watched you talk a duchess out of a duel. I have never seen you lose your place.”

“The library is very quiet,” Caleb told him. “One loses one’s place in quiet rooms. It is well known.”

Jean-Baptiste looked at Odile. Odile looked at her plate. At Caleb’s other elbow Cecily, who had heard every word, buttered her roll in silence, with the air of somebody who had a great deal to say about the squid.

*   *   *

Andrew’s second letter came on the Saturday morning, and the hand had gone downhill from the first line. Caleb could make out the date, and my dear Caleb, and the word Matilda twice, very large, and the word Conti once, underlined, and then nothing at all until the postscript, which was perfectly legible, and said flannel.

Matilda’s parcel came at luncheon, by a French Ministry owl that had plainly been told the Ravenclaw table and nowhere else.

The Tournament had a long history of dead champions, and this year it had a de Blanchemer and a von Conti among its champions for the first time since the war, and so the Department of International Magical Co-operation had required a detail of Aurors from the three Ministries to attend the tasks. That the French share of it should include Matilda was a coincidence that nobody who knew Andrew believed for a moment. The detail would come up for each task and go home to its own Ministries between them, since there was nothing in a school for an Auror to guard but the porridge, and so Matilda was in Paris. From Paris there were parcels.

Cecily’s held a ribbon of dark blue velvet for her hair and a note that said Eat. You may be cross with him on a full stomach. M. Cecily read it, and went faintly pink, and put the ribbon in her pocket and ate her potatoes.

Caleb’s held four calissons from the shop in Aix that Maman liked, and a note in Matilda’s neat hand.

Caleb. Wear your flannel. Do nothing interesting before the twelfth. I shall know. M.

He read it twice. He thought of a tower, and a carriage door, and the underside of the marble staircase, and wondered how much she knew, and how. Then he looked along the bench at his sister. Cecily was eating her potatoes with perfect innocence, like a girl who had never written a letter in her life, and Caleb would have wagered all four calissons that she had written to Matilda every night that week. Then, because he could not help it, he looked down the Hall to the Durmstrang end of the Slytherin table, where Arian von Conti sat with his black coffee, staring back at him without the least shame, as he had every day for a fortnight.

He ate one calisson, and put the other three in his pocket for later.

That afternoon the palominos were out in the last of the sun with their wings half spread to it, and at the paddock rail he asked Arian, rather less courteously than he had asked anything all week, whether he had ever slept.

Arian made no answer. There were dark circles around his eyes, and for all that his face was as expressionless as ever, he looked tired to the bone, and Caleb found that his hand had gone up to that face before he could keep it on the rail. He laid the backs of his fingers under one of the shadows, as Grand-mère laid hers on a forehead to feel for a fever, and the boy went perfectly still under them. Then he turned his face into Caleb’s hand and shut his eyes, and stood there at the paddock rail in the open, with half the lawn to see it, and leaned his cheek into Caleb’s palm with the whole of its weight, and Caleb left his hand where it was.

“Oi! De Blanchemer!” Rhiannon Hale was coming up from the pitch with a sack of turnips over her shoulder. Caleb took his hand back. Arian opened his eyes on him, and did not look in the least as if he had been caught at anything.

It was Rhiannon who told him, scandalised, ten minutes later, with a turnip in each hand. “He’s been standing a watch on that ship from midnight to four every night since Thursday. That little one in the fur cap told half the Hufflepuff first-years. Dumbledore would have me tucked up with cocoa. What’s their Headmaster playing at?”

Caleb looked down the lawn at the ship, lying black on the grey water, and at the lantern hung unlit at its rail near the bow, where the light had gone.

He had spent one night supposing himself obeyed, and the next supposing himself forgotten. All the while Arian had been standing at that rail in the frost, four hours at a time, with his face turned to the castle.

*   *   *

On Sunday evening, at a quarter to nine, there was a knock at the carriage door, and it was Andrew.

He stood on the little gilt step with his despatch box under his arm and his hair wet through. He had been in Paris that morning and in Brussels at luncheon, and he had come on from Brussels by a Portkey that set him down in the rain outside the castle gates, and he had walked up the drive without stopping for an umbrella, which nobody had ever seen him do. Behind him on the grass stood the clerk from the marble staircase, holding a lamp. He had not, by the look of him, sat down since Thursday.

“I have ten minutes,” Andrew informed them, and came in.

Caleb took one of Matilda’s calissons out of his pocket and laid it on the table by the lamp. “Ten,” he told his sister.

Cecily did not look at him. She set down her book, and laid a Sickle on the far side of the table, and with the tip of one finger drew a figure in the spilt sugar beside it. It was an eight.

Andrew did not see. He was looking at Caleb’s collar.

He spent the first four of his ten minutes on the flannel. He did not ask. He only held out his hand, and Caleb sighed and undid the top two buttons for him, since it was quicker, and Andrew turned back the silk with one finger and looked, as he had looked on Halloween, and let it go. Caleb, who had had another hand at that collar on Wednesday night, kept his face perfectly straight and his eyes on the lamp. This time he did the buttons up again himself, before Andrew could.

“Matilda will come on Saturday,” Andrew said. He sat on the edge of the bench, very upright, with his despatch box open on the table in front of him and his hair dripping onto the lid. “And Eleanor. And a cake. I have written to the Portkey Office.”

“It is not our birthday yet, Andrew,” Cecily pointed out.

“No,” said Andrew. “This is a precaution.” He took a sheet out of the box, read it, and put it back. “I came to tell you that I shall not be able to come before Saturday.”

Caleb looked at him, and at the water running off his hair, and out of the window at the lamp on the grass with the clerk under it.

“You are here, Andrew.”

“That is why I could not write it.”

At eight minutes by the carriage clock an owl came out of the dark and knocked at the little round window with its beak, and went on knocking, as only Ministry owls do. Andrew opened the window and took the letter and read it standing, with his back to the table and his despatch box open on it behind him, while the rain came in around him onto the berths.

“The Minister,” he said, and was already doing up his cloak. “You will keep to the carriage after supper. You will keep up the charm. You will eat what is put in front of you, Cecy, whatever you are not saying to whom, and you, Caleb, will —”

“Until our birthday, Andrew?” Caleb asked him.

“Until your birthday,” Andrew agreed, for the second time that week, and kissed them both on the forehead, and shut his despatch box, and went out into the rain to his clerk.

Cecily picked the calisson up off the table and ate it.

She went to bed early that night, which she rarely did, and Caleb heard her behind her curtain until long after midnight, turning pages.

*   *   *

Cecily kept her silence for six days, which was one fewer than she had promised. On the morning of the seventh, at half past six, when the carriage elves sent up the early chocolate, she came into the little dining saloon at the back of the carriage and laid a sheet of Ministry parchment down on top of his brioche.

“Dragons,” she announced. “I am still cross with you. But there are dragons, and I would not have you hear it from Andrew.”

Caleb regarded the parchment, and the brioche beneath it, and at last his sister.

“Bonjour, Cecy. I have missed you cruelly.” He laid down his knife. “And yes. I know.”

“You know?”

“I saw one, on the night the Goblet chose us. There is a cage in the Forest the size of a chapel, and something in it turned its head and looked out at me through the bars, and I have seen its eye every night since whenever I shut mine.” He eased the parchment out from under the apricot confiture, which had come off worst. “I ought to have told you. But you were not speaking to me, and it seemed unkind to make you break your silence for a dragon, when you were keeping it up so beautifully.”

Cecily sat down across from him and poured herself a cup of chocolate, slowly, and Caleb, who had shared a cradle with her, could see that in her mind she was pouring it down the back of his collar.

“That is two things you have not told me.”

He looked at the memorandum instead of answering. It was headed NOT TO BE SHOWN TO THE CHAMPIONS, which told him that it had been through Andrew’s hands, since nobody else at the Ministry would have thought to underline it. Near the foot of it, in Andrew’s own hand, one line had been struck through with a single stroke of the pen, and Caleb, who knew his brother, could see that it had been struck through under protest and not one moment before the Committee made him.

The Beauxbatons champion is to draw the Welsh Green.

“Andrew wanted me to have the Welsh Green.”

“Andrew wanted you to have a Pygmy Puff. The Committee sat with him until midnight and would give him nothing less than a dragon, and then struck out the line altogether. They are to be drawn from a bag on the day, and nobody is to know which until then, not even Andrew.” Cecily drank her chocolate. “Had the Goblet chosen me, he would have handed me the Horntail and a copy of the rules, and slept soundly. There is the Green, and a Chinese Fireball, and a Hungarian Horntail.”

“How did you come by this?”

“I asked Andrew’s despatch box for it. Andrew was reading an owl at the time.” She set her cup in its saucer without a sound. “Then I went in search of a book on dragons, to see what might be done. Every one in that library has been gutted. Somebody went along the shelf with a knife and took every page worth having.”

“Not every book. I took out From Egg to Inferno on the first of November. It has been under my pillow ever since.”

“Under your pillow.”

“It is the one place in the carriage you do not search.”

“Then you are the only champion in this castle with a book.” She buttered a tartine in short hard strokes. “Vittorio von Conti has a knife with a mother-of-pearl handle, with which he cleans his nails at the back of the Great Hall. On Thursday I watched him go into the library with an empty satchel and come out an hour later with a full one, and I do not imagine he had been borrowing.”

Caleb put the memorandum down.

“Then it was not done to me.”

“No.” Cecily bit into her tartine. “It was done to him.”

He thought of the end of the stacks, and a boy standing there hours at a time with eyes for nothing but Caleb, with every book on dragons in the library cut to ribbons on the shelves at his back by his own cousin’s penknife. He thought that very likely Arian knew, and had kept it to himself, as he kept everything, and would walk out to whatever was in that cage with nothing in his head but what Durmstrang had put there and four hours’ sleep.

He was on his feet before he had finished the thought.

“Caleb. It is no business of yours —”

“It is the library’s book, Cecy,” Caleb called back from the door of their cabin, with From Egg to Inferno already under his arm, “and I have kept it a week, which is selfish even for the youngest.”

Behind him Cecily said something to her chocolate in French that he was glad not to catch. It sounded, as he went out into the cold with the book, like somebody who had forgotten, for the length of a tartine, that she was not speaking to him.

*   *   *

The library at Hogwarts was long and dim, with shelves that went up into the gloom further than any candle could follow them, and at seven in the morning, with nobody in it but the librarian, it had the hush of a church in which a dreadful deed has been done and the parish not yet told. The librarian watched Caleb carry From Egg to Inferno down the length of it as if he had dug it up out of her own garden. He chose a table in the end window, where the light came in white off the lake, and sat down with his back to the shelves and opened the book at the Chinese Fireball.

He had got as far as its fringe of golden spikes, and its habit of firing in a great boiling mushroom, which the author admired, when the chair across the table was drawn back and Arian von Conti sat down in it.

He had come without a sound. He sat very straight, and the window light lay white along one side of his face and showed Caleb everything the last four nights had done to it. He had taken the chair across the table, with the whole width of it between them, which was about the width of a fire on a beach, and Caleb, who had learned on the beach what that was worth, gave it a quarter of an hour.

“Good morning,” Caleb greeted him.

“Good morning.”

“I believe this is the last book in Scotland with all its pages.” Caleb turned it around on the table and slid it across. “Shall we share?”

Arian glanced at the book. He did not touch it.

“I did not come for the book.”

Caleb found it necessary to study the Fireball for a moment.

“It is the library’s in any case,” he said, when he had his voice in order. “I have only been keeping it warm.” He leaned his chin on his hand. “Besides, I have an unfair advantage over you. I know what the task is.”

“So do I.”

Caleb sat back.

“How?”

“Rocco has heard it from somebody. He talks of nothing else at table.” Arian paused. “And I saw the fire.”

“In the Forest,” Caleb realised. “On the night of the Goblet.” He had watched it himself from the round window at the back of the carriage, the flame going up over the trees and the palominos stamping in their paddock, and out on the black lake a single porthole lit, low down at the stern, that had gone out just after the fire did. “The porthole at the stern. Was that yours?”

“No. I was on the deck.”

“Then we were both awake, and looking at the same dragon.” Caleb considered it, and found that he liked it a great deal more than he ought.

Arian did not take his eyes off him. Then he reached out and drew the book towards himself, and stopped it halfway, so that it lay between them and they could both read.

They read. Or rather Caleb read aloud, because he read aloud beautifully and saw no reason to hide it, and Arian listened. Caleb read about the Fireball’s mushroom of flame, and the Welsh Green’s fondness for mutton, and the Swedish Short-Snout, which was not coming and which he read about anyway because its flame was a lovely blue and the author had drawn it. He read as he read to Maman when she had a headache, making the dull parts lively and the lively parts a little dull, so that she should not be excited. Somewhere in the Fireball the chair across the table came around the end of it, with Arian in it, until Arian was reading over Caleb’s shoulder.

Caleb never saw it come, only felt it. The heat came down one whole side of him, from the shoulder to the knee, as if somebody had slid a warming pan down a cold bed beside him, and he looked around, and there was Arian, so near that Caleb could see the frost-burn across his knuckles, and his eyes were not on the book.

“You are a little too close, monsieur.”

The chair went back a foot.

Caleb read on. He turned the page, and found that the author had come to the Fireball’s courtship, and had a great deal to say about it. He considered skipping it. Then, because he was a de Blanchemer and would not be seen to blush at a book, he read it aloud, all of it, in his best and steadiest voice. He read how the male came down out of the hills in the first frost and would not be driven off, how he followed the female from crag to crag for a month at a time and stood at the mouth of her cave in all weathers and did not trouble to hide, and how the keepers in Shanghai had learned to leave a pair alone in that season, since nothing on earth would part them once she had let him near.

Somewhere about the mouth of the cave the heat came back down his side. He looked around, and the chair was where it had been before he sent it back, and Arian was watching Caleb’s mouth make the words.

Caleb found that he had stopped in the middle of a sentence about the female’s ruff. His face was burning. He could feel the breath of the boy beside him on his cheek, slow and not quite even, and he thought of saying it again, you are a little too close, and looked at the face beside him, which had turned back to the page as if it had been reading all along, and at the hand on the table, which had gone very still, waiting to be sent away. He did not send it. It was a cold morning, and the window was draughty.

“The female,” he read on, not quite steadily, “is said to be very hard to please.”

The hand on the table closed slowly, once, and opened again.

At the end of the stacks, half hidden behind a case of goblin treaties, Cecily stood with a book open in her hands that she was not reading, and watched.

Caleb smiled at her. She did not smile back. But she stayed, all the same, and left the page unturned, and when Caleb looked up again a minute later she was still there, and her eyes had gone from her brother to the boy beside him.

When the bell rang for breakfast there was nobody behind the goblin treaties any longer, and Caleb closed the book, and found that Arian was still in his chair.

“Tomorrow,” Arian said.

“Pardon?”

“Here. Tomorrow.”

Caleb felt himself begin to smile, and saw no reason to stop.

“Tomorrow, then,” he agreed. “À demain. I shall bring the book, and you shall bring nothing at all, which I suspect is how you prefer to arrive at most things.”

He had gone half the length of the library with the book under his arm, and come to the last bay before the door, where the shelves of law went up into the gloom on either side of a passage no wider than a man, when a hand closed around his wrist and turned him into it.

Caleb had left Arian in the end window with his hands on the table, and now he was here, between Caleb and the light, and Caleb’s shoulders were against the spines of a whole shelf of the Decisions of the Wizengamot, and the book had gone out from under his arm somewhere, and the boy had bent his head.

On the tower there had been the whole sky and the wind. Here there was nothing but the smell of old calf and dust, and the dark between the shelves, and the heat, which filled the passage from one shelf to the other. Arian kissed him with both hands at his waist, spread wide across his sides through the silk, as one of them had been on the carriage step, and this time they held on. Caleb’s head went back against the books. Something behind his shoulders slid back along the shelf with a long dry whisper, and a volume came off the end of the row and hit the floor beside them as loud as a shot, and neither of them looked at it.

From the far end of the library the librarian’s pen stopped scratching.

Arian lifted his head. He did not step back. He stood in the narrow passage with his hands still at Caleb’s waist and his breath coming hard, and his eyes went to Caleb’s mouth and stayed there, and Caleb understood that he was not going to step back until he was given a reason, and that the librarian’s pen was not one.

“This is a library, monsieur,” Caleb informed him, when he could.

“Yes.”

“People come here to read.”

Arian looked at the volume on the floor, and then back at Caleb.

“Yes,” he agreed, and did not move.

Caleb took the hands off his waist, one and then the other, and slid out sideways between the boy and the shelf. He picked up From Egg to Inferno, which had gone under the lowest shelf, and the fallen volume, which he put back in the wrong place, and walked the rest of the library to the door with his chin up and his colour to the roots of his hair. He nodded to the librarian, who did not nod back, and he had his hand on the door before it came to him that he had made an appointment with a von Conti that morning, before a witness, in his own voice, and been kissed against the whole of the case law of the Wizengamot for it, and that he did not in the least regret either.

He stood for a moment with his hand on the cold brass. Then he went out and down the stairs, and at the foot of the next staircase but one Arian von Conti was waiting for him, as Caleb had known he would be.

*   *   *

The boy came to the window on Tuesday with the book under his arm and a look about him that Arian had seen before, on Enzo, on the mornings Enzo had decided to teach him something.

He did not open the book. He laid it on the table, square to the edge, and sat down across from Arian and folded his hands on top of it, as Arian supposed a governess folded hers.

“I have been thinking, monsieur.”

Arian waited.

“It seems to me,” the boy went on, “that if I am to be followed about this castle by a von Conti for the rest of the year, and waylaid in it, which appears to be settled whatever I say about it, then the least I can do in charity is to see that it is done properly. You have been very badly brought up. I do not say it to wound you. I say it because nobody else will, and somebody ought, and I have the advantage of having been brought up extremely well, at great expense, by a governess who once made a duke cry into his soup.”

He delivered all of it pleasantly, as if he were offering to show Arian around a garden. His hands were folded. His chin was raised. There was colour along his cheekbones that had not been there when he came in, and Arian, who had been studying those cheekbones for a fortnight and knew them as he knew the bones of his own hand, saw it and kept still.

“Alors. We shall begin with how one greets.”

He began with how one greeted, and he did it thoroughly. One bowed, it appeared, to a lady from the waist and to a gentleman from the shoulders, and to an old lady in a bath chair from the waist and a little more, in case she was awake, and to the Headmistress of Beauxbatons as low as one could go without falling over, because she noticed. One inclined one’s head to anybody who passed one’s table. One said monsieur and mademoiselle until one was invited to say something else, and one waited to be invited, and if one was never invited one went on saying monsieur. One kissed one’s family on both cheeks, and nobody else on any part of them at all.

The boy heard himself say that last, and stopped, and went on a little faster. Arian, who had kissed him against a shelf of law books the morning before, and under a staircase on Thursday, and on the top of a tower before that, watched the colour come up to his hair and said nothing, since he had not been asked anything.

“To a lady one may bow over the hand. One takes the fingers so, and bends, and one’s mouth comes down to them and stops an inch short. An inch, monsieur. Never less.” He looked across the table, and seemed to decide something. “It is easier shown. Give me your hand.”

Arian gave it to him.

The boy took it in both of his as if it were something he had been handed at a shop counter and meant to examine. His fingers were cool from the walk up the lawn, and very light. He turned it palm up and palm down, and settled the fingers along his own, and bent his head over it, and the curls at his brow came down, and his mouth came down to Arian’s knuckles and stopped. Arian felt his breath there, warm on the skin, an inch off. He felt it go in at his knuckles and up the inside of his wrist and his arm and down the whole length of him at once, as if the boy had put his mouth to every part of him, and he did not move a muscle anywhere, because he did not trust a single one.

“Voilà,” the boy said into the inch of air, and the word was another breath. Then he straightened and gave the hand back, and Arian saw that his colour had gone up from the cheekbones to the hair, and that he had known perfectly well what he was doing for perhaps the last half of it, and had done it anyway.

“You gave me your name on a beach without anybody to make the introduction,” the boy pointed out, rather quickly. “That is not done. I confess I did the same, which only shows what a bad influence you have been on me already.”

Arian stared at him.

He was not listening. He knew that he ought to be. He had been taught at Durmstrang to listen to every lesson as if his life would depend on it afterwards, and very often it had, and he had never yet failed to give a lesson the whole of his attention. But the boy was sitting across the table from him in the white light off the lake, of his own choosing, for no other reason than to talk to Arian, and his mouth was moving, and Arian was watching it.

He watched it make the French words, monsieur and mademoiselle, which went around in it like sweets. He watched it purse when it disapproved of something, which it did often, and prettily. He watched the lower lip with a crease down the middle like the split in a cherry, which the boy bit, sometimes, at the end of a sentence, when he had made a remark he thought was clever and was trying not to smile at it. He watched the colour come and go along the cheekbones above it, and the curls come loose over the brow, and he thought that if he were allowed to sit at this table every morning for the rest of his life and watch that mouth tell him how to bow, he would never ask for anything else.

It was not true, and he knew it even as he thought it. He would ask for a great deal else. The want had come into him at the doors of the Hall on the first night and burned under the surface ever since, like a fire in a peat bog, and now it came up through the whole of him at once, hot and heavy and much too big, and he had to put his hands flat on his knees under the table and hold himself still. He wanted that mouth. He had had it four times, and each time had only made him want it more. He wanted the inch taken away. He knew now what the crease in that lower lip tasted of, and what sound the boy made at the back of his throat when it was found, and he sat in the white light with his hands pressed flat and did not move, because if he moved at all he would lean across the table and take it.

He had not once been told to stop. He had listened for it each time with the whole of himself, as he had listened on the shore, and if it had come he would have stopped where he stood, on the tower or under the stair or among the law books, and gone around whatever fire he was sent around. It had not come. The boy had held his wrist, and taken hold of his clasp, and taken his robes in a fist, and told him it was a library, and kissed him back each time where he stood. But the boy had come to this table this morning to teach him something, of his own choosing, and Arian found that he wanted to be taught it.

“— and then one sits,” the boy was saying, “and one sits at a proper distance, which is a little more than an arm’s length, so that neither party need smell the other’s soap. There. Like this.” He got up and moved his own chair back, and then, after a moment’s thought, moved Arian’s too, with Arian in it, by a foot and a half, with a flick of his wand, and sat down again. “One does not sit at a person’s elbow. One does not read over his shoulder. One does not come around the end of a table in a library a yard at a time, like a tide, in the hope that he will not notice. He notices, monsieur. I promise you that he notices. And one never — monsieur, are you listening?”

“No.”

The boy stopped.

He sat quite still with his hands on the book and looked across the foot and a half of air he had made between them, and Arian could see him decide whether to be offended, and decide not to.

“What are you doing?”

Arian’s eyes went to his mouth.

He looked because the boy had asked, and that was where his eyes had been all along, and he let the boy see him do it, slowly, as he let Rocco see him look, because there was nothing in it to be ashamed of.

“Watching.”

The colour went up the boy’s face from his collar to his hair, all at once, as a flame goes up a curtain.

The boy knew what he had been watching. Arian saw him know it.

He did not look away. He had looked away across the Great Hall often enough, at his toast and his sister and the ceiling, but Arian saw him want to now, and saw him not do it, and the two of them sat there across the foot and a half of air, with the librarian two stacks off scratching at her ledger, and neither of them moved, and Arian could hear his own heart in his ears like a drum on a parade ground.

Then the boy drew a breath.

“Bien,” he said, not quite steadily. “Then you may watch this, monsieur, and I hope you will remember it.” He laid one hand flat on the book, as a man lays his hand on a Bible to swear. “When one wishes to call upon a person, one sends first to ask whether one may. One writes a note. One sends a card. One waits for the answer, and if the answer is no, one does not come, and if the answer is yes, one comes at the hour one has been given and not a minute before. One asks, monsieur. One does not simply appear.”

Arian heard it.

He heard it as he had heard the song on the tower, all the way down, and laid it away somewhere inside him, carefully, beside the leaf and the thirteen words and the taste of the treacle tart.

One asks.

He had not known it was permitted. At Chernoyar nobody asked for things, because everything was there before one thought to ask, and if one was ever seen to care for something, Dario took it away. He had not asked for the boy’s mouth on the tower. It had not crossed his mind that there was a way to. And here was the boy telling him, with his hand on a book and his chin raised, as if nothing could be simpler, to ask.

He knew how one asked, in the north.

Every boy in the old houses knew it, as he knew how to bow, though few of them had done it in living memory, and the ones who had done it had mostly been laughed at. His grandfather’s brother had done it in the winter of the great frost, for a girl of the Orlov house who would not look at him, and had laid a bear at her father’s door, and been let in. On the ship the boys told it in their hammocks, for a joke.

“On Saturday,” the boy went on, more steadily, “Cecily and I shall be eighteen, and Andrew is coming up from Paris to do something unforgivable with a cake, and I shall not be in the library, monsieur, so you need not come. I tell you so that you will not wait. That is also manners.” He opened the book at last. “The Hungarian Horntail. Attend.”

Arian attended to the Horntail. He attended to it with the whole of himself and remembered every word of it, and when the bell rang he got to his feet and inclined his head to the boy, and left, and the back of his hand burned all the way down the stairs where nothing had touched it.

*   *   *

The middle watch that night was very long.

He stood it at the rail with his hands in his cloak and the frost coming down. At a quarter to twelve the light came down the carriage steps and went up the lawn alone, as it had every night since Thursday, and a little after one it came down again and went in, and at four the sixth-year came up to relieve him.

Arian did not go below.

He went down the gangway into the dark, in the open, past Vittorio’s porthole, and across the shingle and the foot of the lawn, and into the Forest.

It was forbidden. He knew that. Professor Dumbledore had announced it at the first feast, and the boy’s brother had forbidden it in a letter, which the boy had told him about on the shore, laughing. Arian went in under the trees all the same, because one asked, and in the north this was how one did it.

In the north the proper thing to bring was a wolf, or a bear. There was no wolf in that Forest he would take, and the only bear he had ever stood in front of had been Enzo’s to kill, and he could not see either of them on the boy’s step. Arian thought instead of him coming down off those carriage steps on the first night under the lamps, and of that face, and of a winter pelt held up against it, white, against all that chestnut, a collar first, and then a scarf, and in time a coat to the heels. He would look very fine in it. He would look beautiful, and Arian saw no reason why he should not think so, since it was true. The boy would be warm in it too, which was something, since he had come out of that carriage cold and been cold ever since. Arian thought of white fur against those curls, and knew where he would begin.

The Forest at that hour was black and still and smelled of frost and leaf mould and something older underneath, and the trees went up on every side of him like the pillars of a church with the roof fallen in. He did not light his wand. He had been taught to hunt in the dark at Chernoyar, in the birch woods behind the house, by the old huntsman his father kept, who was blind in one eye and could see better in the dark with the other than most men could with two. Arian had been a better pupil than either of his cousins, and had been punished for it, and had gone on being better. He went into the Forest as he had been taught, slowly, without a sound, and stood among the trees with his breath held until the wood forgot that he was there.

Far off, something vast shifted in its chains, and was still.

He found the hare at the edge of a clearing where the bracken had died back and gone the colour of rust. It was a mountain hare, a big buck already gone over to its winter coat, white from its ears to its feet but for the black at their tips, crouched in its form with its ears laid flat along its back, and it did not know he was there until he had made up his mind, and then it did not know anything. He took it with one charm, cleanly, so that it should not suffer, and so that nobody who knew his business could say afterwards that it had been badly done. Then he knelt in the frozen bracken and gathered an armful of it, the driest and the reddest, and carried both out of the Forest in the dark.

The carriage was asleep. Every window in it was dark, and frost had furred the gilt along its eaves, and the winged horses in their paddock shifted and blew in their sleep as he went by. He laid the bracken on the step, and the hare on the bracken, and put its long hind legs and its forepaws together, tidily, as the old huntsman had taught him to lay out game on the flags of the hall for the house to see.

Then he stood back and looked at the door.

It was a pale blue door with a gilt handle, and behind it the boy was asleep. Arian did not knock. In the north one did not knock, and one did not wait on the step to be thanked. One laid down what one had brought and went away, and came back with more, and went on coming until the door was opened. The boy had told him that one must ask. This was the only way of asking that Arian knew.

He went back to the ship with his hands empty and the grey just coming into the sky behind the mountains, warm all the way down the lawn.

*   *   *

On the Wednesday morning Caleb opened the carriage door at seven, meaning to go up to the library early and be there first for once, and found a hare on the step.

It lay on a bed of bracken with its paws laid tidily together, as the keepers at Sologne laid out the bag after a good day, and it was white all over, a mountain hare in its winter coat, so that the frost that had come down on it in the night hardly showed. Caleb stood in the doorway in his cloak with From Egg to Inferno under his arm and looked down at it for some time.

Then he crouched and turned it over with one finger, and saw that it had been taken cleanly, with one charm, by somebody who knew his business. He had gone out with the hunt in Sologne every autumn since he was ten, and he knew a clean kill when he saw one, and he knew what it cost to make one in the dark. There was not a mark on it. Somebody had stood very still in a very cold place for a long time, to be sure of it.

“It is a hare,” Cecily remarked from the door behind him.

She had come out in her dressing gown with her hair down her back, because she had heard the door, and she was looking at the step over his shoulder with the face of a woman who has found a frog in her slipper.

“I can see that it is a hare.”

“Who has sent you a hare?”

It was out before she could stop it, and Caleb saw her hear it, and remember that she was still cross with him, and press her lips together. She looked at the hare a moment longer. Then she went back in to be cross in her dressing gown, and he heard her cabin door shut behind her, not loudly, but with great firmness.

Caleb contemplated the hare.

He knew who had sent it. Nobody had seen it laid, and it might have been anybody’s doing, a joke of the Hufflepuffs, a kindness of the house-elves, the Forest’s own idea. He told himself all three and believed none of them. He thought of the library window the day before, and a voice he had never heard raised saying No, quite simply, when it was asked whether it was listening, and of black eyes on his mouth, and of a hand lying in both of his with his breath on its knuckles, and he felt his face go hot in the frost on the carriage step, at seven in the morning, over a hare.

One asks, monsieur. One does not simply appear.

He had not simply appeared. Caleb would grant him that. He had left a hare instead.

He carried it around to the back of the carriage and gave it to the house-elf who kept the larder, who looked at it, and at him, with eyes like saucers, and asked in a whisper whether Monsieur would like it jugged. Caleb replied that Monsieur had no opinion whatever about the hare, and that she might do with it whatever seemed best to her, and went up the lawn to the library with the book under his arm at a pace he had not used since he was nine.

Arian was there before him. He sat in the end window at a little more than an arm’s length from the chair Caleb would take, which Caleb saw he had measured, and there was bracken caught in the fur at the hem of his cloak.

Caleb sat down and laid the book between them.

“Good morning, monsieur.”

“Good morning.”

Neither of them said one word about the hare. Caleb opened the book at the Horntail and read aloud for an hour in a voice that was very nearly steady, and did not look up from the page, and the whole hour he could feel the boy’s eyes on him, at his mouth when he read, and at his throat when he swallowed, and at his hands when he turned the page, as plainly as if each place had been touched in turn.

At the end of the hour he looked up, because he could not help it, and the boy glanced at the bracken on his own hem, and then at Caleb, and his face was very nearly pleased with itself.

Caleb shut the book rather hard and went to breakfast.

*   *   *

That night he had the hare at supper.

It came to him only at the second helping. The house-elves of the Beauxbatons carriage had jugged it, in red wine with juniper and a little bitter chocolate, as the cooks did at Blanchemer in the autumn, and sent it up to the dining saloon for the twins’ supper, with the skin cured and brushed and folded beside it in a square of linen, as white as a napkin, because Monsieur had told them they might do as they thought best, and they had thought it best that Monsieur should eat it. Caleb had eaten a good deal of it, with redcurrant jelly, and was reaching for more, before it came to him what it was.

He put down the spoon.

Across the table Cecily looked at the spoon, and at him. Then she got up and went to her cabin and came back with a book.

It was an old book, bound in calf gone as soft as a glove, and he knew it at once, because he had watched her pack it in August at Blanchemer, between her cauldron and her examination notes, and had asked her why, and been told that one studied one’s enemies. Usages et coutumes des vieilles maisons du Nord. There were pencil marks all down its margins in her neat examination hand. She opened it at a page she had marked with the paper from a sugared almond, and laid it on the cloth in front of him beside the jugged hare, and put her finger on a paragraph, and did not sit.

“It is how they court in the north, voyons,” she informed him, to a point somewhere above his head. “I looked it up. One brings meat to the door until one is let in.”

Caleb read the paragraph. He looked at the jugged hare. Then he read the last line of the paragraph again, which Cecily had underlined twice, and which told him that a family who ate what was brought and kept its door shut was held in those parts to have behaved very ill.

“Cecy.”

But she had gone, and her cabin door had shut behind her again, and he sat alone in the dining saloon with the book and the hare and the lamp, while the carriage creaked around him in the frost like a ship at its moorings.

He read the paragraph again. Then he read the rest of the chapter, which was about the Orlovs, and a winter of great frost, and a bear. Then he closed the book and sat with his hand on it for a long time.

He thought of the library, and a voice saying Watching, as plainly as a man gives the hour, and of a pair of hands under a table, held flat, as men hold a horse that wants to bolt. Here was a boy who had kissed him four times without asking once, and who had then been told, with great kindness and at some length, that one asked, and who had gone straight out into a forbidden forest in the dark and asked, in the only way anybody had ever taught him. He had laid bracken on the step like a carpet before a door, and Caleb had sent the answer to the kitchens and eaten it with redcurrant jelly.

He had expected to laugh. It was the most absurd thing that had ever happened to him, and a great many absurd things had happened to him, most of them on purpose, but he sat in the lamplight with his hand on Cecily’s book and could not find the joke in it anywhere.

Before midnight he took the white pelt out of its linen and held it for some time. It was very soft. Then he folded it again and put it at the bottom of his trunk, under the flannels, because he could not think what else to do with it.

At midnight he did not go to bed. He went to the carriage door in his nightshirt, in the dark, and opened it, and stood on the threshold with the cold coming in around his bare feet, and looked out.

The step was empty. The bracken had been swept away. Down the long white lawn the lake lay black under the stars, and the ship lay black upon it, and at its rail near the bow the lantern burned again, as it had burned on Thursday, with somebody beside it. At that distance there was no face to see, but Caleb knew it all the same.

He stood there until his feet had gone numb. Then he went in and shut the door, and after a moment he left it on the latch.

On the morning of his eighteenth birthday Caleb opened the carriage door at seven and looked at the step before he could stop himself.

He stood there in his dressing gown. The step was bare. It had been swept, and the frost lay on it unmarked, and beyond it the lawn ran down white to the lake, where the ship lay in the mist with only its masts showing, like the trees of a drowned orchard. There was no bracken. There was no hare. Tant pis.

He was surprised to find that he had looked.

“You are letting in the cold,” Cecily informed the door of their cabin, behind him.

It was the first thing she had said in his direction since the jugged hare, and it was not said to him. He shut the door and went to dress.

At nine the carriage was found to have been surrounded.

Andrew came up the lawn from the castle in his grey Ministry robes with two clerks behind him, as he came to everything, having arrived from Paris by the Headmaster’s Floo at six and spent the three hours since in conference with the Headmaster, the Headmistress, the Headmaster of Durmstrang, the house-elves of the castle kitchens and, by owl, the agent who let Ashcairn, the house above the village. Matilda, who had come through the Headmaster’s fire behind him, walked at his side. Between them, in a hat of her own design, walked Eleanor, who had come by Portkey before dawn and looked as if she had slept twelve hours in a feather bed. She was the middle sister, fair-haired and blue-eyed like Andrew, but with their mother’s soft features. She designed robes for one of the great houses of wizarding Paris, and had left it in the middle of the winter collections to come. All three of them wore their travelling cloaks in the Blanchemer white, and Matilda’s was clasped at the throat with a silver shell, which meant that she was out of her scarlet, off duty and, for a whole weekend, nobody’s but theirs.

Behind them the sleigh came up the lawn at a walk, with the white horses blowing in the cold, and it was Eleanor, who was looking ahead for the twins and not at her feet, who saw him first.

There was a boy on the lawn at the foot of the carriage steps. The carriage stood shut and pale blue in the frost, with its curtains still drawn and the twins somewhere inside it, and he stood a little apart from its steps with frost on his shoulders, as if he had been there a good while and meant to be there a good while longer. He was very tall and very dark, and his face had not slept. Eleanor had never seen him before. She knew him all the same. Every de Blanchemer knew the Conti face, as one knows the look of weather coming over a hill. She knew which Conti, too. Andrew had described this one to her over three dinners in Paris, and had not once got as far as his face. He had got as far as the Dark Arts each time, and stopped, and laid his fork square to his plate.

Andrew had stopped. His clerks stopped behind him, a pace too late.

“Monsieur von Conti,” Andrew said, in a voice so flat that a clerk could have ruled lines with it, and inclined his head as far as was correct, and no further. It was perfectly polite. Eleanor, who had known him all her life, heard how little he liked it.

Matilda inclined her head as a lady of an old house inclines it to the son of another, no more and no less. “Monsieur.”

Eleanor, for her part, had started like a girl at her first ball, which she had not done since she was sixteen, and her eyebrows had gone up nearly to the brim of her hat. She brought them down again, and found her manners where she had left them. “Bonjour, monsieur.”

The boy returned each of them a single inclination of the head, correct to the last degree, and said nothing.

Andrew took off his gloves and put them on again.

“Is there something you require from Beauxbatons?”

It was the voice in which he asked foreign delegations what they required of France, and by Beauxbatons he meant Caleb. Andrew was not blind, and he had certainly noticed the attention the second von Conti son paid to the youngest de Blanchemer.

“Caleb,” said Arian von Conti.

He said it low. Eleanor had dressed a great many brides in Paris, and she had heard a name said like that before, from the far side of a fitting room curtain.

Above them the carriage door opened.

“Oui?” said Caleb from the top step, as he had answered to his name all his life.

Then he saw who had said it. The colour came up into his face from his collar to the roots of his curls. It was the colour he went when he was caught being pleased.

Andrew took his right glove off again, finger by finger, and this time he kept it off.

Eleanor put a hand to her mouth. A Conti had called her brother by his name, on a lawn, before his whole family. Caleb had been answering archdukes on staircases since he was six. He stood on the top step with his lips parted and had no answer at all.

Arian von Conti looked at Caleb a moment longer. Then he looked at Andrew, for quite as long and quite as openly. He bowed to the three of them, and turned, and went away across the lawn and in under the trees at the edge of the Forest.

Eleanor was laughing now. She came up the carriage steps two at a time, which no de Blanchemer did in public, and caught Caleb in one arm on the top step, and Cecily, who had come to the door behind him with her hair half pinned and had seen nothing, in the other, and kissed them both.

“Happy birthday, my darlings,” she announced. “Fetch your cloaks. We are taking you away.”

“The Headmaster has given his leave,” Andrew informed the Headmistress, who had come to the carriage door in her morning wrapper to see what the clerks were for. “The Committee has been made aware, and the Headmaster of Durmstrang has been made aware, and they will be returned to you by Sunday supper in the condition in which they were taken. I have seen to it personally.”

Caleb was down the steps before he had finished speaking. He kissed all three of them on both cheeks, Andrew first, who bore it before his clerks with every appearance of not enjoying it, and kept hold of Caleb’s shoulders for some moments afterwards, then Matilda, and last of all Eleanor. Matilda had not seen him since he left for Scotland, and she did not let him off with both cheeks. She caught him around the neck with one arm and held him hard against her white cloak, as she had held him on her hip when he was two, until he laughed into her shoulder and told her that the clerks were looking, and she told him that the clerks might look at something else.

The Headmistress, who had known Andrew since he was a first-year with ink on his nose, looked down at the four of them from her great height for a long moment, and her mouth unbent.

“Joyeux anniversaire, mes enfants,” she told the twins, and went back in.

Cecily came down after him in her cloak and kissed all three of them too, and held on to Matilda longest, and did not look at her brother.

“She is still cross,” Matilda remarked in French, to nobody, without moving her lips.

“She is entitled,” Eleanor returned. “It is her birthday too.”

At the foot of the carriage steps, where Andrew had left it, stood the Blanchemer sleigh. It was white from its runners to its hood, with the crest in silver on its door and white rugs lined with silver-grey heaped on its seats, and a pair of white winged horses stood in the traces with their wings folded and their breath going up in the cold.

Eleanor and Matilda handed Cecily up between them in a flurry of white cloaks, and Andrew put Caleb in after her with his own hands, rather firmly, as if he expected him to climb out again. Caleb let him. He was looking at the trees at the edge of the Forest, where there was nobody now.

I shall not be in the library, monsieur, so you need not come. He had said it on Tuesday, very clearly. He had not said a word about the lawn.

Arian von Conti had kissed him on the top of a tower, and against the carriage door, and under the marble staircase with Andrew coming down it, and against a shelf of law books in the library with the librarian listening, and until this morning he had never once said Caleb’s name aloud. Caleb had been called by his name all his life, by duchesses and governesses and the Minister for Magic, and it had never sounded like that, as if it had been said a great many times already, somewhere Caleb had not been, and worn smooth with it. He could still feel it. It had gone into him on the top step and down, low and warm, as the breath at his temple had gone into him in the panelled room, and it had not come out again.

Cecily sat facing her brother and looked at the lake.

“Il a perdu sa langue,” Matilda observed to Eleanor across the rugs. “Note the date.”

Eleanor had noted it already. Caleb heard them both, and pulled the rug up to his chin, and looked very hard at the horses. Then the horses opened their wings, and the sleigh went away down the lawn and rose over the pines with its runners hissing on the air, and came down on the hill road below the castle as lightly as a gull comes down on water, and glided on down it towards the village.

Hogsmeade lay under the first real snow of the winter. It had come down in the night out of the mountains, and every roof in the village was iced like a cake and every chimney smoking, and the High Street went up the hill between the shops like a white cloth laid the length of a table. Above the village, on the road that climbed from it towards the castle, a house of grey stone stood behind a wall and a pair of iron gates in its own snowy garden. It was called Ashcairn, a little manor and a very fine one, with a drive that ran up to it between clipped yews, a terrace along its front, a glasshouse at one end with its panes gone white, and a fanlight over its door. Andrew had taken it for the weekend.

It had the Blanchemer linen and the Blanchemer silver and glass in it by nine, and a house-elf of the family in its kitchen, who had come by Portkey with the cake and had already formed opinions about the range.

The dining room had a fire in it the size of a stable door, and a bow window of thick old greenish glass that looked out over the garden to the road and the gates, and bent the snowy garden into ripples, like a garden seen in a pond. The table was laid with linen that Eleanor had brought from the Blanchemer estate, because she would not eat a birthday dinner off anybody else’s, and white roses from the hothouse at Sologne stood in a bowl that had been their great-grandmother’s, and on the sideboard, under a silver dome, stood a cake from Paris.

It had come by Ministry Portkey at seven that morning, in its own box, with a house-elf of the family to hold it level.

“He sent the Portkey Office a memorandum,” Matilda told the twins from the best armchair. “Four pages. I have seen it.”

“The cake travelled perfectly,” Andrew pointed out.

“The cake travelled under seal, Andrew. With an escort.”

“The Lalande christening —”

“Was eleven years ago, and it was a trifle, and it was not upside down for long.” Matilda set down her cup. “There was a whole page about the candles. Eleanor, tell them about the page about the candles.”

“Hélas, there was a page about the candles,” Eleanor confirmed, taking off her hat.

Matilda had left her scarlet in Paris. She sat by the fire in a plain wool gown the colour of a wren, with a cup of chocolate at her elbow and her back as straight as it ever was at a door on duty, and she looked like what she was, a lady at her ease among her own people, who sees everything and remarks on a tenth of it. She had brought nothing with her but a reticule and her wand, and the wand she had laid on the mantelpiece, out of her own reach, where she could see it.

“I am not here,” she warned them all. “If the Department asks, I am in Paris, drawing up the guard for the first task.”

There were presents before luncheon, because Eleanor could never wait.

Eleanor gave Cecily a pair of silver cauldron tongs from the old shop in the rue de la Harpe, chased with oak leaves, and she gave Caleb a pocket watch that had been their grandfather’s. It was gold and as thin as a coin. It had stopped at a quarter past four on the afternoon their grandfather died, and Eleanor had set it going again herself on the morning she came of age. Caleb held it in his palm and listened to it tick for a long minute. Eleanor wound the ribbon around her fingers and waited.

Then she brought out the parcels from home. Maman had sent two pots of quince jelly from the stillroom, labelled pour mes petits in her round hand, and four pages to each of them that smelled of her lavender and said a great many times over that she loved them. Papa had sent a pair of signet rings in a case from the place Vendôme, gold, with the Blanchemer crest cut in white sardonyx, and a card in his own hand that said Dix-huit ans and nothing else. Cecily put hers on at once. Caleb read the card three times, because Papa had written it himself, and for Papa that was a speech.

Grand-mère had sent two tall beeswax candles from the stillroom at Blanchemer, white and smelling of honey, wrapped in a page torn from her receipt book, and on the back of the page, in her old slanting hand, Allumez-les quand vous saurez pourquoi.

“Light them when you know why,” Caleb read aloud.

“She would not tell me why,” Eleanor said. “I asked her twice.”

Matilda gave Caleb a hunting bow of yew and horn from the bowyer at Romorantin, short enough for the Sologne thickets and strung to his draw, which she had known since she first put a bow into his hands when he was eight. She had tried it herself at the butts behind the Ministry, and had the grip bound in white kid. She gave Cecily a case of silver knives for cutting ingredients, each ground fine enough to split a hair down its length, because she had once watched Cecily slice valerian root with a borrowed penknife and had not forgotten it.

Andrew gave Cecily a first edition of Libatius Borage, which she had wanted for three years and had told nobody but him, and she turned the pages with the tips of her fingers and went pink and whispered merci, mon frère and kissed him. “You will write the next edition,” Andrew told her, quite seriously, and she went pinker still, because Andrew did not say such things to be kind. Then he gave Caleb a parcel tied with grey Ministry tape, very square at the corners, and inside it were twelve flannels of the finest Sologne wool, each with his initials worked on it in silver thread.

Eleanor laughed until she had to put her head down on the table among the Blanchemer silver.

“Andrew,” Caleb protested, holding one up to the firelight, where it hung like a flag of truce. “Mais enfin, I am eighteen.”

“Then you will need larger ones.”

“He has measured you,” Cecily remarked to Eleanor, since she was not speaking to Caleb. “He has had a clerk measure you in your sleep.”

“I have done nothing of the kind,” Andrew retorted, with great dignity. “I wrote to your tailor.”

“He wrote to the tailor,” Eleanor managed from the tablecloth, “in June.”

“He wrote to the tailor when Caleb had the measles,” Matilda contributed, peeling a clementine, “to ask whether flannel could be had in a size that would go on over the spots. And he slept across Caleb’s door for a fortnight on a camp bed, with his Ministry papers, and read them aloud to him.”

“He liked it.”

“He was four, Andrew. He liked the hippogriffs. You read him the minutes of the Committee on Cauldron Thickness.”

“He went to sleep at once,” Andrew reminded her. “It was a great success.”

Caleb laughed. He laughed with his head thrown back and the flannel in his hands, helplessly, until the tears stood in his eyes, as nobody at Hogwarts had yet heard him laugh, and Eleanor laughed with him, and even Andrew’s mouth went at one corner before he could catch it.

Under the flannels, when he could see again, there was an envelope with the Blanchemer seal, and in it the papers for the grey Arab mare Caleb had admired at Chantilly in June, mentioned once at breakfast, and completely forgotten. Andrew had bought her the following morning and said nothing about it for six months.

“I have never had so much as a pony out of him,” Eleanor remarked, without the least rancour.

Caleb gave Cecily her present then, because it seemed the moment. It was a pair of gloves in the pale grey kid she liked, from the glover in the rue Saint-Honoré, and he had bought them in August, before Hogwarts and the Goblet and everything else, on a day when she had complained to him that she had nothing fit to wear to a coronation.

He did not give them to her. He gave them to Eleanor, and Eleanor gave them to Cecily with her eyebrows up.

“He says,” Eleanor informed her, without being told, “that you are to keep his as well. He says you looked like a duchess in them, and that he would not have you look like anything less.”

Cecily studied the gloves for some time. Then she looked down the table at Eleanor, and not at Caleb, and her mouth was not quite steady.

“Tell him thank you,” she told Eleanor.

She had a parcel for him too. She did not give it to him either. She slid it down the table to Eleanor, wrapped in green paper and tied with green ribbon in her neatest bow, and Eleanor passed it on, and inside it was a comb of tortoiseshell with a silver back, of the kind that is made for a lady’s dressing table and never used.

“Tell him,” Cecily instructed Eleanor, to the window, “that it is for the cobwebs.”

Caleb held the comb in his two hands and could not say anything at all, and so he got up and went around the table and kissed his sister on both cheeks. She let him. She did not kiss him back, and when he sat down again she had gone very pink and was looking very hard at Libatius Borage.

All through luncheon, and the long white afternoon after it, while the snow came down outside the bent glass and the fire roared up the chimney, Caleb sat on the arm of Eleanor’s chair, and then on the hearthrug at Matilda’s feet, and talked. He told them about the giant squid in the lake, gesturing with both hands, and how it took bread from his fingers as delicately as a dowager takes a bonbon, and preferred the crust. He told them about the dancing trolls on the seventh floor and the largest troll’s unrequited love for the dancing master, and acted out the curtsy on the hearthrug, and Eleanor wept. Then he set Andrew’s twelve flannels dancing the trolls’ ballet two by two down the tablecloth, with one idle flick of his wand, and at the end of it they bowed to Andrew, all twelve, and Andrew pretended not to see. He told them about the lady in curl papers who had offered to scream for him. He did not tell them who had been on the stairs behind him when she offered, and Matilda, by the fire, kept her eyes on her clementine, and peeled the next segment very slowly indeed.

Andrew sat at the head of the table with his cup and watched him. He did not smile, because Andrew did not, very often, in front of anybody. But his face had gone quiet, as a face goes quiet before a fire at the end of a long journey, and every so often his eyes went to Caleb’s cheek, and to his hands, and found nothing there, and came away satisfied.

At home among his family for the first time since September, with the snow at the window and the Blanchemer silver on the table and nobody in the room who did not bear his name, Caleb was what he was at home and nowhere else, and he shone with it, as a house shines on a winter night when every shutter has been opened and every lamp in it lit.

At four the light went blue in the snow. Andrew went down to the village to see the agent about the horses, and Eleanor went up to dress, and Cecily, who had nobody left at the fire to speak through but her eldest sister, looked up from Libatius Borage.

“Matilda, would you be so good as to tell my brother that I saw him put his arm into a bonfire on the third, to the elbow, in front of Odile.”

Matilda glanced from Cecily to Caleb on the hearthrug, and folded her hands in her lap.

“Mon Dieu. Still?”

“I promised a week,” Cecily informed the fire.

“You promised it on Halloween, Cecy. It is the twelfth.”

“I broke it once, for a dragon. Dragons do not count.”

Matilda inclined her head. “Your sister saw you put your arm into a bonfire,” she told Caleb, without taking her eyes from Cecily.

“Tell her that it was Andrew’s idea.”

“It was Andrew’s idea, Cecy.”

“Bien sûr,” said Cecily to her book.

“There was a boy at the paddock rail while you did it. Tall. Red. A cloak he does not trouble to fasten. When the sleigh lifted over the trees, Arian went after it. He went down the hill road through the snow as fast as a man can walk without running, with the sleigh going away ahead of him over the tops of the pines, and he lost it beyond the shoulder of the hill. When he came over the rise he saw the two white winged horses being led into a walled paddock above the village, and the white sleigh standing in the drive of a grey house beyond them. He kept away from the gates. He turned away down the hill into the village, and was on the bridge when the Durmstrang party came down at noon.

Durmstrang had been given the afternoon in Hogsmeade with the rest of the school, and most of the seventh-years had gone straight to the inn by the bridge, where there was a back room and a fire and nobody who asked questions. Rocco had tried to keep him from it. He had gone to the Headmaster after supper the night before and said, very reasonably, that a champion who stood the middle watch ought to sleep through his afternoon ashore and not go wandering about a village, and the Headmaster had nodded over his kasha, and then found Arian’s name already signed at the foot of the list in Enzo’s narrow hand, as Enzo had signed every leave of the term before he went away, and had not cared to strike out his deputy’s signature while his deputy was not there to see it. Rocco and Vittorio had gone up the High Street to look in the shop windows, because Vittorio wished to find something to send home to his mother, which was the only thing about Vittorio that Arian had ever liked. Mirek had wanted to stay with him. Arian had told him to go with the others, and given him a handful of Sickles to spend, and watched him go off down the hill in his fur cap with his face shining, holding the coins in both fists.

He had been told not to come to the library, and he had not come. He had stood the middle watch, and gone below at four, and lain in his hammock with his eyes open until it was light enough to go and stand on the lawn. The boy had said nothing of the hare in three days, not a word. In the north a silence at the door meant only that the door was not yet open, and one did not stand in the snow and wonder at a shut door. One brought more. One asked, the boy had said, and there was only one way of asking that Arian knew, and he had asked once. He would ask again.

So he left the others to the inn by the bridge and went up alone into the woods above the village, where the snow lay fresh and unbroken under the pines. He went in among the trees without a sound and found the neat paired tracks of a stoat at the edge of a clearing, and followed them through the holly to an old wall, and took it there with one charm, so cleanly that the white of it was not marked. It was in its full winter ermine, white from its nose to the black tip of its tail, the softest thing in the wood, and he saw it, before he could stop himself, at the boy’s throat, white against those dark curls. He saw the bare skin above the fur, where the pulse went, and his own mouth there, as it had been on the boy’s mouth among the law books, and the want went through him so hard in the snow that he put his hand to the wall.

At dusk he came down out of the woods with the ermine inside his cloak, and met his cousins at the foot of the hill road, coming down from the shops.

“Smotri, Vittorio,” murmured a pleasant voice at his shoulder, in Russian. “Look. A hound at the door.”

Rocco had Vittorio at his elbow and a striped paper bag from the sweetshop in his gloved hand, and he glanced up the hill road at the grey house, and then at Arian, with great kindness.

“They are having a party,” he went on. “Poshli? Shall we go up and knock? I am sure they would be delighted to see us.”

Vittorio laughed. Arian spared neither of them a glance. He walked on between them, and the snow skinned over with ice where his boots had been, and neither of them came after him. Behind him Vittorio’s laugh went on a little way by itself, and Rocco’s did not join it. Arian heard the place where it should have been. He had walked through Rocco’s laughter all his life, through the cut lashings and the soaked hammock and the watch, and never left ice behind him for any of it. He had left it now, on a hill road, under the windows of a grey house, and Rocco had seen where it lay.

The light went out of the sky behind the mountains, and the lamps came on down the street one by one. Mirek came to him in the back room of the inn by the bridge with a sugar mouse in his fist, and stopped in front of Arian, and held out the mouse. “Vot.” Arian looked at it. Then he took it, and Mirek went off to the fire, pleased past speaking, and Arian sat with the sugar mouse in his hand with no idea what to do with it, and at last put it in his pocket, on the other side from the ermine. Across the room Rocco sat apart from the fire with the sweetshop bag unopened at his elbow, writing, with his head bent.

At eight he left the others to their fire and walked back alone through the village in the snow, and out onto the road that climbed to the castle, because it was his way home. It went past the gates of the grey house. Beyond them the drive ran up through the snowy garden between its yews, and across the garden the bow window was lit. The glass was old and thick, and the room behind it swam, gold and white and firelit, and through it, across the garden and through the falling snow, there came to him a laugh.

It was the boy’s laugh. He had heard it come down the well of the swinging stair to him in the dark, and from behind the armour on the sixth floor, and at the foot of the castle steps when he lifted his wand to light the way, low and helpless and smothered behind a hand. Now it was not smothered, and it was not alone. It went up into that warm room with the others around it, open and glad and as loud as it liked, as a bird sings in a garden where nobody has ever thrown a stone at it, and the others laughed back.

He had thought, on the stair, that he had found where it was kept. He had not understood until now for whom.

For the length of a few steps, past those gates, his heart went out of him. It went up through the falling snow and the thick old glass towards the boy, and it hurt, and he wanted it to go on.

He thought of the long dining room at Chernoyar, with the stags’ heads on the walls and the fire at the far end too far off to warm anybody, and his father at the head of the table with a face like a crag that has stood a great many hard winters. Nobody had ever laughed at that table. He could not remember having heard his father laugh at all, except once, on the evening Dario told him about the bird.

He did not stop. One did not stand at a family’s gate, not in the north and not anywhere, and he had been told already that one did not simply appear. He walked on up the hill in the snow towards the castle, and never looked back.

They dined at seven, by candlelight, with the curtains open on the snow.

The house-elf sent up a soup of chestnuts and cream, which Eleanor pronounced better than anything to be had in the rue Montorgueil, and then a pâté en croûte that had come from the traiteur at Romorantin in the same box as the cake, and then a saddle of mutton, and between each course Andrew got up and walked to the window and looked out at the garden and the gates, and came back and sat down again.

Cecily was still cross with Caleb, and so she sat at the far end of the table between Eleanor and the fire, and addressed every remark she meant for Caleb to Eleanor, which at a birthday they shared, under one cake with one set of candles, took a good deal of engineering.

“Eleanor, s’il te plaît, would you ask whoever is sitting at the other end of the table whether he means to eat every roast potato in Scotland himself.”

“Eleanor,” Caleb returned, “would you tell whoever has asked that she is welcome to every potato in Scotland, and that I shall carry them down the table to her myself on a silver dish, if she will only look at me while I do it.”

“I am not the poste restante, my darlings,” Eleanor complained, passing the potatoes. “I am your sister, and I have come all the way from Paris.”

Matilda ate her mutton and said very little, and between mouthfuls she watched the window, as she would have watched a door, and said nothing at all about what she saw there.

Then the house-elf cleared the plates, and Andrew stood and lifted the silver dome from the cake as a man lifts the lid of a despatch box, and there it was. It was three tiers high and as white as the garden outside, and it had been iced with sugar lace in the pattern of the old Blanchemer linen, and sugared violets lay scattered over the top of it as thick as a spring wood, and around the edge of the topmost tier stood eighteen white candles, one for each year, in a ring. Andrew had brought it four hundred miles under seal, and a house-elf had held it level all the way, and not one of the violets had moved.

Eleanor lit the candles with her wand, one after another around the ring.

For a moment they burned straight upwards, eighteen little flames as still as if they had been painted, with the firelight behind them and the snow beyond the glass.

Then, very gently, all eighteen of them leaned the same way.

Towards Caleb.

They all stared at the candles. Caleb saw it, and knew that they had all seen it. Nobody had opened a window. The fire was drawing hard up the chimney at the far end of the room, the other way, and the candles in the silver sticks on the table stood as straight as soldiers on parade, every one. Only the eighteen on the cake had bent, all together, as grass bends when the wind goes over a hill, and they stayed bent, and every one of them was pointing at him.

The room went quiet. Eleanor had stopped with her wand still lifted. Andrew had stopped with the cake knife held out to the twins.

Caleb looked at the candles. He thought, at once and firmly, of draughts. Old houses had a great many of them. This one was older than the Ministry, and its windows did not fit, and its doors had been hung by a carpenter who had died before anybody at the table was born, and somewhere a door was open that ought not to be, and that was all.

Nobody had opened a door.

“Grand-mère told us about this,” Cecily said to Eleanor, quite low, without taking her eyes from the flames. “In the stillroom. When we were eight. We were making beeswax candles for Candlemas, and Caleb had burnt his thumb, and she told us so that he would stop crying.” She drew a breath. “‘When somebody gives his heart away, the candles lean towards the one who has it.’”

Nobody answered her. Eleanor lowered her wand.

Caleb found that his mouth had gone dry, as it went dry before a hunt.

“Grand-mère says a great many things,” he managed.

Cecily did not look at him. She looked at Eleanor, and her face was quite white, and quite steady.

“Tell him,” she told Eleanor, “that she has never once been wrong.”

Caleb felt himself go scarlet, and reached for his glass and hid behind it. Across the table Andrew saw it, and frowned.

“You are red,” Andrew observed.

“It is the fire,” Caleb told him, from behind his glass. The fire was at the other end of the room. It was the poorest answer he had given anybody since he was four, and Andrew’s frown deepened, because Andrew knew it too.

Then, as gently as they had bent, all eighteen flames straightened together and burned for nobody in particular.

“Blow them out, my darlings,” Eleanor urged softly, before anybody could say anything else, “before they set fire to my linen.”

They had blown out their candles together every year since they were one, sitting side by side in their high chairs at Blanchemer with Maman holding the plate, and Cecily saw no reason to stop now. She leaned in across the cake at the same moment as he did, without looking at him, as if her body had remembered it before the rest of her could forbid it, and they blew together, and the eighteen candles went out all at once, and eighteen threads of smoke went up straight into the warm air and were gone.

Eleanor and Matilda clapped. Andrew put the knife into the twins’ hands, and they cut the cake together, as they had cut every birthday cake of their lives, without looking at each other.

Cecily looked at her brother for the first time all day, and then, thoughtfully, at the window.

Caleb had no need to follow it. His heart went once, very hard, against his ribs, and he looked at the cake.

It was Andrew who went to see.

He got up and crossed the room to the window and drew back the edge of the curtain, which was open already and did not need it, and looked out across the garden to the road. He stood there with the curtain in his hand and said, in the voice of a man who had heard that boy call his brother “Caleb” on a lawn at nine o’clock that morning and had not cared for it then either, “There is nobody on the road.”

“There was,” Matilda said, without looking up from her coffee. “He walked past at eight, and he has gone up the hill.”

Caleb went to the window beside his brother. Beyond the garden the road lay empty under the lamps on the gateposts, white and new, with the snow still coming down on it, and in the fresh snow there was one line of footprints. They came up the road from the village, and at the gates they slowed, so close together that they had almost stopped, and then they went on up the hill towards the castle.

“He brought Caleb a hare,” Matilda added, stirring her coffee.

Andrew turned around. “Comment? And you did not think to inform me?”

“I thought Caleb would like to inform you himself,” she murmured, with a glance at her younger brother.

Andrew turned to his brother. Caleb had become very interested in the cake. He went back to the table and cut himself a second slice of it with great care, and chose three sugared violets for the top, one at a time, and kept his eyes on every one of them.

“A hare. When?”

“On Wednesday. On the carriage step.”

“How do you know all this?”

“I am an Auror, Andrew. I am paid to know.” Matilda helped herself to a sugared violet. “I am not paid to tell you. Nobody could afford it.”

Andrew regarded his sister with deep disapproval. “You do not seem worried, Matilda.”

“I am worried.” She looked up from her coffee at last, with the patience of a woman who has had this conversation every quarter since she was twelve. “But you worry enough about him for all of us, Andrew. He is eighteen today.”

“He is the baby,” said Andrew, as if that settled it, and to Andrew it always had.

“That,” Eleanor told the tablecloth, “has been true since the day he was born. Andrew held him at the christening as if he were a soufflé.”

“He had him taught to swim at three,” Matilda agreed, “in the lake at Sologne, by a Ministry instructor, with a rope.”

“The lake is deep.” Andrew let the curtain fall. “Draw the curtain.”

“He did not drown in the lake, Andrew,” Eleanor pointed out. “He will not drown in an admirer.”

“Eleanor and I have seen to three of them since he was fifteen,” Matilda said, to her coffee.

“Three of what?”

“The Spanish boy,” Matilda supplied. “Two summers ago, under the terrace at Blanchemer, every night for a week. Eleanor sent his guitar back to Seville with a note.”

“The heir from Lyon,” Eleanor went on, counting on her fingers. “His father had a call from Matilda in her scarlet, and the boy has not left Lyon since. And a girl in the year above them at Beauxbatons, who wrote him eleven sonnets.”

“Nine,” Caleb told his cake. “Two of them were not at all bad.”

Andrew had gone white about the mouth. “And nobody told me.”

“Because you would have challenged him, Andrew,” Matilda reminded him. “You challenged the guitar.”

“The guitar,” Andrew stated, with the whole of his dignity, “was out of tune.”

“And the Comte,” Eleanor added.

“The Comte,” Andrew said, with great composure, “gave a child a camellia and told him he would wait.”

“He lost half of Burgundy, Andrew.” Matilda chose another violet.

“He lost some vineyards.” Andrew turned his glass on the cloth until it stood square to the edge of his plate, and took his hand away from it. “It was a very wet year.”

“He wanted to geld him,” Matilda informed the table. “He said so, at the time, in front of Papa.”

“I proposed it,” Andrew corrected her. “With precedents.”

“Papa asked which charm he meant to use,” Eleanor told the twins, “and Andrew told him, and Papa said it was the wrong one, and they argued it for an hour in the little salon. I have never seen Papa so tickled. He laughed, Caleb. Aloud.”

“Twice,” said Matilda.

“Then Maman came in,” Eleanor went on, “and told them both that it was a terrible idea, and that nobody asks a family to dinner once it has begun gelding Comtes.”

Andrew moved his glass. “It would have been done discreetly.”

“That is what Papa said,” Matilda agreed. “Maman said there was no such thing as a discreet gelding.”

“So I settled,” Andrew said, and set his glass square again. “The vineyards were too light a punishment, but Maman insisted.”

“You need not look at us like that, Andrew,” Eleanor told him. “You have had them too. The demoiselle de Hautecombe had a reply from you on Ministry parchment so correct that she framed it, and a month later her family discovered urgent business in Montevideo.”

“Montevideo was very fortunate to have her.”

“And her brother, who called to complain of it, went to Reykjavík,” Matilda added.

“Matilda has only to look at hers,” Eleanor went on. “By the second dance she knows their debts and their mothers’ maiden names, and they withdraw of their own accord.”

“It saves a great deal of dancing,” Matilda agreed. “Eleanor dresses hers. Beautifully. Then she introduces them to somebody far better suited, and they thank her for it at the wedding.”

“Cecy corrects their Latin.” Eleanor nodded down the table. “She sent the Montfort boy’s ode back to him in green ink, with a mark at the foot of it.”

“Three out of ten,” Cecily informed Eleanor, who had not asked. “He was persistent, so I gave him a draught. It was perfectly harmless. He sneezed every time he said my name, until Easter. Voilà.”

Andrew looked down the table at his brother. “And Caleb refuses them so charmingly that they go home happy and never notice they have been refused.”

“Only the ones who give up, mon cher,” Matilda told him. “The others came to us.”

“And not one of them,” Eleanor added fondly, “ever got so far as his hand.”

Caleb swallowed a sugared violet whole. Andrew, without looking around, passed him the water.

Eleanor laughed. Matilda did not. She set down her cup and turned to her younger brother across the candles.

“Say the word, mon petit,” she said, “and I shall see to this one before the first task.”

Caleb laid down his knife.

He had only to say it. If he said the word she would have the champion of Durmstrang kept to his ship by nightfall, and his cousins with him, and nobody would ever again stand at the end of a paddock rail in the cold, or light a stair that Caleb could perfectly well light for himself, or drive a Gryffindor and his sonnet out of a cloister. It was how it had always been done. Every admirer who would not take a charming answer had been seen to by Eleanor or Matilda before Caleb had to say a cross word. He had never once refused the help.

“No, ma sœur,” he said. “I have it in hand.”

It was the first time he had refused them. He had nothing in hand at all, and he knew it, and so did everybody at the table.

“He is first in the Dark Arts, Caleb.” Andrew addressed it to his glass. “In his year, and in the Institute. The returns come to the Committee, and I have read them twice.”

“He has never yet cursed me at breakfast, Andrew.”

“That is not —”

“I have asked,” Matilda said. “Our people in Petersburg know that family’s marks as I know my own hand. There are none of his anywhere. If he has ever used one of them outside a schoolroom, nobody has found where.” She took up her cup again. “I do not say that it comforts me. I say that I asked.”

Eleanor looked at Matilda over the cake, and Matilda looked back at her, and neither of them said anything further on the subject that night. The curtain was still open.

Caleb did not draw it. He sat down beside his brother, and stole the sugared violets off the top of Andrew’s own slice of cake when Andrew was not looking, and again when he was, and Andrew let him both times.

At the far end of the table Cecily had not moved. She sat with her hands folded in her lap beside the cake, which had a slice gone out of it now and eighteen burnt wicks on its top, and she was not looking at the window any longer. She was looking at her brother’s face.

Arian was on deck by midnight. The sixth-year he relieved looked at the snow on his shoulders, which had been gathering there since he came aboard, and at his face, and went below without a word.

The lake had not frozen. It lay black and still around the hull under the falling snow, so still that the snow seemed to go down into it without a sound and without a ripple, as if the water were swallowing it whole. The carriage on the lawn was dark, every window of it. The castle was dark. Hogsmeade was out of sight beyond the shoulder of the hill, and the grey house above it was asleep behind its gates.

Arian stood at the rail with his hands inside his cloak.

At two his uncle came up the gangway out of the snow, ten days after he had gone, in his travelling cloak, and went below without a word, and a quarter of an hour later came up through the galley hatch with a tin mug of hot milk, honey and black pepper, which was what he gave to the sick, the homesick and the young, and he had never told Arian which of the three he took him for.

He had brought him the same on the night he turned eighteen.

That had been in September, with the ship running under the sea somewhere off the coast, and nobody aboard had said a word about it. Durmstrang did not keep birthdays, and the Contis had never kept Arian’s. At Chernoyar it had been marked once a year in the family register by the steward, in brown ink, under Dario’s, and that was all. Enzo had come down to the hammocks after the lamps were turned low and set the mug on the sea chest beside him, and gone away again without a word. When Arian looked again there was a heel of black bread beside it with a candle stuck in the middle of it, already lit, and in the next hammock Mirek, eleven years old, a week at sea and green with it, had his eyes screwed shut and was pretending to be asleep. Arian never learned how he had found out.

He had lain in his hammock and watched it burn. In time Mirek had snored in the next hammock, and somewhere far above them the sea had gone on over the ship, black and endless, and the candle had burned down into the bread with its flame standing up as straight as a lance in the still air under the sea. He had watched it until it went out. Then he had eaten the bread.

In the morning, at breakfast in the great cabin, Enzo had looked at him once across the table, a long look, and said one word in Russian, as a clerk reads a date off a deed.

“Vosemnadtsat.”

Eighteen. By the law of the house, which was older than the ship and older than Durmstrang, a son of the Contis who had come to eighteen might stand up before the family and challenge the heir for the headship, and the family was bound by whatever came of it. Nobody had done it in a hundred years. Arian had no wish to be the head of anything. It had never crossed his mind that any of them would believe it of him. All his life he had wanted only that the house should look past him at Dario, as it always had, and leave him the birch woods and the dark and the deer. But their father was a hard man to please, and pleased by little, and what little had ever pleased him had been Arian. He had been pleased by the bear at five, and the birch woods, and the duelling yard, and the Institute’s returns, which were read aloud at table every summer with Arian’s name at the head of the Dark Arts. The house watched its heir, and their father watched the younger son, and Arian had wanted neither. That his father had ever been pleased with him was the one thing Dario had never forgiven him.

But Rocco and Vittorio knew the law as well as he did. They had not spoken to him once all that morning. Rocco had looked at him across the table for a long time with his chin on his hands, and then he had gone to write to Dario before the bread was cleared, and Dario’s letters had come more often since, and every one of them had been read aloud.

He drank the milk. It burned all the way down, the pepper and the heat, and he was glad of it.

He thought of the boy behind the bow window, laughing, with his family around him. He thought of his own candle in its heel of bread, under the sea, with nobody but a seasick boy to see it burn. And he found that he was glad. He was glad as he had not known he could be glad of anything he did not have himself, fiercely, as a man is glad on a winter night of a fire in another man’s house, that somebody had it, and that it was the boy.

Enzo had come up without a lantern. He stood at the rail beside Arian in his travelling cloak, with the snow settling on his shoulders, and looked out across the water towards the castle. A silence between the two of them had never needed filling, and this one lasted until the castle clock struck the quarter. When Enzo spoke it was in Russian.

“I have been to the Headmaster. I woke him. He was not pleased to be woken, and he was a good deal less pleased by the time I had done, and he has remembered that a champion who stands all night on a frozen deck does Durmstrang very little credit. There is no more middle watch.” The snow went on settling on his shoulders. “Your cousin may write to your brother if he likes. I do not think your brother will answer him on this. He has never yet thought it worth his while to quarrel with me.”

Arian stayed at the rail.

Enzo turned to him. He looked at the snow on Arian’s shoulders, which had not melted, and at his face, and then away again at the castle, and beyond it, at the dark shoulder of the hill with the village behind it.

“Idi. Go where you like.”

Arian went.

Instead of going below he went down the gangway into the snow and up across the lawn towards the castle gates.

It was the better part of two miles around by the castle gates and down the hill road, and he went that way in the dark with the snow to his knees and the ermine inside his cloak against his chest, where it would not crush, and he felt nothing of the cold or the snow, nothing but the laugh still in his ears. Nothing moved on the road when he came down it. The gates of the grey house were shut but not locked, and he let himself in and went up the drive between the yews, and the house stood at the end of it with not a light in any window, and snow on its step and on the iron knocker of its door.

Arian hung the ermine from the knocker by a twist of rush, so that it lay down the dark wood of the door like a white glove on a black sleeve.

Then he stood back in the snow and looked at the door, as he had looked at the pale blue door of the carriage. Behind it, somewhere up the stairs, the boy was asleep. Arian could not see the window from there, and had no need to.

He walked back up the road as the grey came up behind the mountains, and he was on deck before six, where Enzo still stood at the rail in the snow, looking at the castle. His uncle glanced once at Arian’s boots, and went below.

*   *   *

In the morning Andrew opened the front door of the house in his dressing gown, to see what the weather had done in the night, and walked face first into a stoat in full winter ermine, hung from the knocker.

He stood on the threshold in the snow with the white fur against his cheek and the black tip of its tail against his nose, and stayed there a long moment. He had stood behind three foreign coronations for the Ministry, and he knew ermine when it touched his face. It was the fur that lined the robes of kings. His face, which the Ministry had never seen change, slowly went the colour of the snow.

Behind him on the stairs Caleb had stopped, with one hand on the banister, and behind Caleb was Eleanor in her wrapper, and behind Eleanor, a step higher, in her stockinged feet, Cecily.

Caleb came down the last stairs and past his brother onto the step. The snow had stopped. The drive went down between the yews to the gates white and untrodden in the early light, except for one line of footprints, deep, that came in at the gates and up the middle of the drive to the door, and went away again the way they had come, and he studied the footprints for some time. Then he turned to the ermine. It had been taken cleanly, and there was not a mark on the white of it.

“One does not hang ermine on a gentleman’s door, monsieur,” he told the footprints, very low.

“Ermine,” Andrew informed the knocker. “The Contis have sent us ermine. On a de Blanchemer door, like a hare in a game larder.”

“Not us, Andrew,” Eleanor murmured from the stairs.

Andrew tied the cord of his dressing gown again, although it had not come undone, and went back in to dress.

Eleanor took the ermine down off the knocker herself, laughing, and turned it to the light with the eye of a woman who knew to a Galleon what ermine was worth. Then she held it up against Caleb’s throat and considered him with her head on one side.

“Winter white,” she pronounced. “Not cream, and not that hard blue white they sell in the rue de la Paix. The Conti boy has excellent taste, my darling. He knows which white suits you best, and I have clients of ten years’ standing who could not tell me that.”

She put it into his hands and went in. Caleb stood on the step with it, entirely at a loss.

Cecily had come down the stairs behind him. She stood in the doorway in her stockinged feet in the cold and looked past him at the line of footprints going away down the drive. She stood beside him in silence until her feet were cold, and then she went back in.

At breakfast Andrew wrote three letters before his coffee was cold, and sealed them, and gave them to a clerk to take down to the village by the first owl.

“To the Minister, to the Committee and to Professor Dumbledore,” he informed the table, as if somebody had asked. “I have asked to be relieved of the Committee until the Tournament is over. I shall take rooms at the castle. Professor Dumbledore will be very kind.”

Eleanor put down her cup. “Since when?”

“Since a quarter past seven.”

“Until when?”

Andrew buttered his toast very evenly and did not answer, and Matilda looked across the coffee pot at Caleb, and said nothing, since she had said it already by the fire.

“Mon frère,” said Caleb. “It was only a stoat.”

“It was ermine,” said Andrew.

The Minister’s answer came back before the toast was finished. It came by the Minister’s own owl, a grey old bird with a crooked foot who was known to every department in the Ministry and dreaded by all of them, and it was one line long, in the Minister’s own hand. Andrew read it twice, and folded it in four, and put it away inside his robes.

“Well?” said Eleanor.

“The Minister has been very kind,” Andrew said. “I am to be in Paris at eight tomorrow morning, and on every morning that the Committee sits.”

“And between times?”

Andrew buttered a second piece of toast, although he had not eaten the first.

“Between times,” he said, “I shall contrive.”

*   *   *

Eleanor and Matilda went back to Paris after luncheon, by Portkey from the glasshouse, because the winter collections would not make themselves and the Department would not wait for anybody. Matilda had put her scarlet on again for it, and with it the face the Department paid for. She kissed the twins on the forehead, Cecily first, because Cecily was eleven minutes older and would have noticed. Then she put her arms around both of them at once, scarlet and all, and held them for a good deal longer than the Department would have thought proper. “On the twenty-fourth I shall be on duty, and an Auror on duty does not kiss anybody,” she told them, into Caleb’s curls, “so I have done it now. Je vous embrasse, both of you. Until the twenty-fourth.” Then Eleanor kissed them at the door, and then Andrew, who bore it, and then she took Caleb’s face in her two gloved hands and looked at it for a long moment, as she looked at a length of silk she was deciding how to cut.

“Write to me,” she told him. “Not to Maman. Maman will only worry, and Andrew will read it over her shoulder.”

“There is nothing to write, ma chère.”

“Then it will be a very short letter,” said Eleanor, “and you may write it on Tuesday.”

The sleigh came around to the door when she had gone, and they went down into the village first, because Caleb had promised Ambrose Fennick a better quill than the one he had borrowed on Halloween morning, and he bought him an eagle feather with a silver nib at the quill shop in the High Street. Andrew stood outside the shop in the snow and watched the door the whole time, and looked once, very hard, up and down the street, where there was nobody.

In the sleigh Andrew sat facing the twins with his despatch box open on his knees, writing to the Committee again on the lid of it, and every so often he glanced up at Caleb, and down at the lid, and wrote something more. Cecily sat beside her brother. She had not spoken to him all the way down to the village. She kept her silence all the way back up the hill, past the gates of the house and on towards the castle, with the snow coming off the trees in soft falls as the runners went by and the castle getting bigger over the horses’ heads. Somewhere before the castle gates she fell asleep, as she had fallen asleep against him in every carriage of their lives, with her head on his shoulder and her new gloves in her lap.

Andrew looked up from his despatch box and saw it, and his face eased. He made no remark. He reached across and drew the rug up over both of them, and tucked it in at Cecily’s side and at Caleb’s, and went back to his letter.

*   *   *

At a quarter to midnight the carriage was asleep, and Cecily with it, behind her blue curtain, with Libatius Borage open on her chest. Caleb lay in the bottom berth and listened to the frost tick on the roof, and at last he got up and put his cloak on over his nightshirt and went to the door, because he had gone to it every night for a fortnight and his feet did not know how to stop.

He opened it, and the light was there.

It stood at the foot of the castle steps at the top of the white lawn, held up at the height of a man’s shoulder and quite still, as it had stood on the Tuesday and the Wednesday after Halloween, and as it had not stood since.

Caleb watched it for a long moment. Then he went back in for his boots.

The snow had been swept from the gravel at the foot of the steps, and Arian stood on it with his wand lit and the snow lying unmelted on his shoulders. He looked as if he had not slept since Friday. Very likely he had not.

“Bonsoir, monsieur.”

“Good evening.”

“You are not on your ship.”

“No.”

“Andrew has not been so put out since the Comte.”

“Good.”

Caleb laughed out loud, since there was nobody on the steps of Hogwarts at midnight to hear but the boy in front of him.

“You said my name.”

“Yes.”

“It was the first time.”

“I know.”

“You said it as if —” Caleb stopped.

Arian waited.

“I have not given you permission to call me by name, monsieur,” Caleb said instead.

Arian let the silence answer for him. He only looked at him, steadily, with the light held low between them, and Caleb felt the answer go into his face and down his throat and lower still, into a place he had not known a look could reach, and stood there in the snow with his cloak hanging open over his nightshirt and let it.

Arian’s eyes went to the cloak, and to the open neck of the nightshirt under it, and the bare throat above that. Then he put out his free hand and found the clasp of Caleb’s cloak, which Caleb had not troubled to fasten, and fastened it.

He did it slowly. His fingers were hot, and clumsy as Caleb had never seen them, and the backs of them lay against the hollow of Caleb’s throat while they worked, and Caleb felt them there as plainly as he had felt Arian’s look on the stair. Andrew had done up his buttons in the carriage on Halloween. It had not felt in the least like this. When the clasp was shut the hand stayed where it was, with the knuckles against his throat and the thumb resting on the pulse under his jaw, which was going like a bird’s.

Caleb did not step back. He found that he had tipped his chin up, a little, to make the clasp easier, and that he had done it on purpose, and that he was not sorry.

“Monsieur,” he said, in a voice he did not know.

Arian bent his head and kissed him.

He did it with his hand still at the clasp, so that his knuckles stayed in the hollow of Caleb’s throat and his thumb on the pulse the whole while, and he did it slowly, which he had not done before. On the tower and under the stair and among the law books he had kissed Caleb as if the castle might take him away before he had finished. Tonight he did not seem to think anything could. The snow came down on them both, on the black hair and on Caleb’s lashes, and Caleb stood on the swept gravel at the foot of the steps of Hogwarts in his nightshirt and his boots and let himself be kissed, and kissed back, slow for slow, and felt his own pulse go wild under that thumb and knew that Arian could feel every beat of it, and did not care in the least what it told him.

Above them the bolt of the great doors went back with a noise like a gun.

Arian lifted his head. He stayed where he was, and so did the hand.

The great doors of the castle opened.

Andrew stood at the top of the steps with a lamp in one hand and his despatch box under his arm, fully dressed, in his grey Ministry robes, as if it were the middle of the afternoon. He had dined with the Headmaster and stayed on, putting off his Portkey to Paris by quarter-hours, and the last of them left from the gates at half past twelve. He looked down the steps at his brother in a cloak and a nightshirt, and at the boy in red, and at the hand.

“Caleb.”

The hand remained. Arian turned his head and looked up the steps at Andrew, quite openly, with his knuckles still in the hollow of Caleb’s throat, for as long as it took Andrew to come down one of them. Then he took it away, as unhurried as if nobody had come out at all.

“Bonsoir, Andrew,” Caleb said, very brightly.

“It is ten minutes past midnight.”

“Is it? I had not noticed. I came out to look at the snow.”

Andrew looked at the snow, of which there was a great deal, and at Arian, who had turned to face him and stood quite still at the foot of the steps.

“Monsieur von Conti.”

Arian gave him the shortest of bows. “Monsieur de Blanchemer.”

“My brother is going to bed.”

“I understand.”

Andrew came down the steps. He was in no hurry. He came down them as he came down the length of a council chamber, and at the foot of them he gave Caleb his arm, and Caleb took it, because there was nothing else whatever to be done, and they went away down the white lawn together towards the carriage with the lamp swinging.

Halfway down, Caleb heard the step behind them.

It came down the lawn a few yards behind them, steady and unhurried, and made no effort to be quiet, and when Caleb looked back over his shoulder the wandlight was there, a good deal nearer than the distance it had kept in October, and coming no further off for Andrew’s lamp.

“He is following us,” Andrew said, without turning his head.

“Yes, Andrew. He does that.”

“Every night?”

“Not lately.”

Andrew walked on a few paces in silence.

“I see,” he said. “I shall need a larger lamp.”

At the carriage steps he stopped, and looked at the clasp of Caleb’s cloak, which was fastened, and at Caleb’s face, and said nothing about either, which was a great deal worse than anything he might have said. Then he kissed Caleb on the forehead.

“Bonne nuit, mon petit. I shall see you at breakfast.”

“You will be in Paris at breakfast, Andrew.”

“I will be wherever I am required,” said Andrew.

Caleb went in. He did not go to bed. He knelt at the little round window, cleared a place in the mist with his sleeve, and looked out.

Andrew stood at the foot of the carriage steps in the snow with his lamp. A few yards up the lawn Arian stood with his wand lit. Neither of them moved. They stood there in the white of the lawn under the stars, the lamp and the wandlight, and neither of them looked as if he meant to be the first to go.

Then a third light came down the lawn from the gates at a run. It was the clerk, with a lantern in one hand and a watch held out in the other, and Caleb watched him arrive at his brother’s elbow and speak. His brother did not move. The clerk spoke again and held the watch up higher. Still he did not move. It was only when the clerk started bowing that Andrew went. He went up the lawn towards the gates at the pace of a man who has a Portkey in three minutes and will not be seen to run for it, with the clerk going on ahead of him and coming back and going on ahead again, and at the top of the lawn he turned around and held the lamp up once towards the boy in red. Caleb had seen him lift a hand so across a council chamber, to a delegation he meant to see again in the morning.

Arian inclined his head to it.

Caleb put his face down on his pillow and laughed until the berth shook.

“Caleb,” said the curtain above him. “Some of us are trying to sleep.”

When he looked again, a long while after, the lawn was empty. There were three lines of footprints in the snow. One went down to the lake, and one went up towards the gates with the third running rings around it all the way, and as far as Caleb could see from the window, only the clerk had hurried.
