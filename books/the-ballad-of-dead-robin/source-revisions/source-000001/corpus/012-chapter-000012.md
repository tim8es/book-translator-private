He stood in the center of a room that felt less like a living space and more like a museum of dark arts. The walls were steeped in shadow, lined floor to ceiling with shelves crowded with artifacts—some of them painfully familiar to Tim. Others hummed with a wrongness he could feel in his teeth.

Books filled a tall cabinet near a heavy desk, their spines marked with titles written in languages Tim didn’t recognize, letters twisting in ways that made his eyes ache. Candles burned low, their light wavering unnaturally, casting warped shadows that crawled across the walls. The air smelled of old parchment, dust, and something faintly metallic.

Klarion sat behind the desk, lazily stroking the cat curled in his lap. His gaze never left Tim—dark, bottomless, and far too amused.

“I think we have something to discuss, little bird,” Klarion said, his smile thin and cutting, like a blade drawn just enough to warn, and Tim felt that phantom cut on his skin.

“Alfred,” Bruce called out, uncertainty threading through his voice as his eyes fixed on the firearm in Alfred’s hands. The sight of it felt wrong in a way he couldn’t immediately name.

The butler looked up, his expression composed—almost serenely so—as if he were holding nothing more dangerous than a teacup.

“Yes, Master Bruce?”

Bruce exhaled slowly, the breath dragging out of his chest as his brow furrowed. He studied the man he had long since stopped thinking of as anything less than a father.

“Why do you need a gun?”

Because if Alfred Pennyworth had taken up arms, Gotham itself ought to be afraid—not of the Dark Knight, but of who was behind him, always guarding from shadows.

“I believe the answer is rather obvious, Master Bruce,” a muscle in Alfred’s jaw twitched, just barely. For the briefest heartbeat, Bruce could have sworn he saw something flash behind those calm eyes—anger, cold and razor-focused. It vanished almost as soon as it appeared. “I intend to go after your son. I owe him at least that much.”

We all do, hung unspoken in the air.

“You know, Alfred,” Jason’s voice cut in from the side, disbelief written openly across his face, “you’ve always been my favorite.”

“Jason!” came an immediate, disapproving protest. Alfred merely lifted his chin with practiced dignity.

“Thank you, Master Jason. I’m flattered.”

Despite the careful restraint in his tone, Bruce caught it—the faintest upward curve at the corners of Alfred’s mouth. A ghost of a smile.

“Are we just going to ignore the fact that Alfred picked up a gun?” Duke asked, as level-headed as ever.

Bruce felt a familiar swell of quiet admiration. Out of all of them, Duke, along with Damian, who was still finishing school, was the only one in their family, who decided to go to the university, choosing criminal psychology with the same seriousness he brought to everything else. Bruce was proud of him. Truly proud. And still, a selfish part of him had hoped—had prayed—that at least one of his sons would choose a different life.

A life untouched by Gotham’s horrors.

But after Tim’s death, Duke had thrown himself fully into criminology, burying his grief beneath case studies and theories, convinced it was his responsibility to save those who could still be saved. As if redemption could be earned through vigilance alone.

Bruce opened his mouth, ready to stop Alfred, to pull him back from what felt dangerously close to a witch hunt, but the heavy door to the Batcave creaked open instead. Damian appeared at the top of the stairs.

He descended quietly, each step measured, controlled, his presence slipping into the Cave like a shadow. He ignored every single one of them. Not a word left his mouth, and Bruce hated how familiar that silence had become.

Since Tim’s death, Damian had withdrawn into himself, growing more distant by the day. First, it was a missed dinner. Then lunch. A full week passed since Bruce had last seen his son sitting at the table. Then another.

Until Robin disappeared.

Damian had stopped wearing the Robin mantle. Stopped trying to prove his right to belong.

He still patrolled the city, but the spark was gone. No sharp defiance, no reckless faith in colours. He moved through Gotham without color or name—no longer as Robin.

He had become just another shadow of the city, appearing without a sound and vanishing the same way. He followed in the Dark Knight’s wake, step for step, close enough to disappear into Bruce’s silhouette, and that frightened Bruce more than any enemy ever had.

Above all else, he never wanted his son to become like him.

And now all Bruce could do was watch as Damian headed for the armory, calmly selecting his katanas as if no one else existed in the room. His movements were precise, practiced, disturbingly detached. There was cold and unwavering resolve in his eyes, and something in Bruce’s gut tightened, a quiet, poisonous premonition settling in.

“Damian,” Bruce called.

One second passed. Then another. No response.

Bruce clenched his jaw, drawing a slow breath in through his nose and forcing it out again before trying louder.

“Damian.”

This time, Damian turned.

The katanas rested easily in his hands, their weight familiar. His gaze locked onto Bruce—flat, distant, burning with an emptiness that cut deeper than anger ever could. It stopped Bruce cold.

When had his son become this? When had he allowed this to happen?

Bruce couldn’t stop blaming himself. With every one of his sons, he had failed in some way. Every time, he made choices that later hollowed him out with regret.

And no matter how many nights he survived as Batman, it was this—standing across from his children—that left the deepest scars.

He was a terrible father.

Bruce Wayne was a terrible father because no matter how desperately he tried to keep his children safe, he had failed to save even one of them.

“Father?”

Damian’s voice carried a low, restrained fury—quiet enough to be controlled, sharp enough to make Bruce’s stomach twist in on itself. The Cave seemed to respond to it: the distant hum of generators felt louder, the shadows deeper, the air colder against Bruce’s skin. Damian stood perfectly still, waiting. Watching. And in his green eyes, a small, merciless flame burned frighteningly alive.

“You can’t go after him alone, Damian,” Bruce said at last, forcing the words out with a slow and measured breath. Damian turned his back on him.

Metal whispered softly as his fingers brushed over the weapon rack, the sound carrying too clearly through the Cave. He lingered over the knives, testing their balance with brief, intimate touches, as if committing their weight to memory.

“And who’s going to stop me, Father?” he asked, glancing over his shoulder.

The look he gave Bruce made him feel microscopic—pinned, examined, reduced to something fragile beneath a merciless lens.

“You?” Damian added. “I’m not Robin anymore.”

“You’re still my son,” Bruce said through clenched teeth, clinging to reason like a lifeline.

Damian slid one knife into his belt, the other into his pocket with deliberate care. He kept both katanas in his left hand. He didn’t reach for a mask. Robin’s suit was left untouchable.

“So was Timothy,” Damian replied evenly.

Then he started toward the exit, his steps firm, posture tensed. Bruce’s heart seized painfully in his chest, his thoughts scattering into nothing. Every argument, every plea vanished, until Dick moved.

He stepped into Damian’s path, blocking him just in time, and only then did Bruce realize how close he had come to losing another son.

“Move, Grayson,” Damian said. He didn’t raise his voice. He didn’t shout. It was an order, far more terrifying for how calm it was.

“Dami, just—” Dick said, dragging a hand over his face. He looked as exhausted as Bruce felt, dark circles carved deep beneath his eyes, shoulders slumped from too many sleepless nights. But stubborn as ever, he planted himself in front of Damian anyway. “Where are you even going?”

Damian didn’t hesitate.

“To kill the witch.”

The words landed softly. Precisely. Like a verdict already passed.

Jason choked on a sharp breath, then let out a short, humorless laugh, wiping his mouth with the back of his hand. A crooked, almost triumphant grin split his face as he shot Bruce a look that said see? Bruce’s head snapped to the side, a spike of panic jolting through him, and he took a few unsteady steps forward, boots scraping against the Cave floor.

“We don’t know where Klarion could’ve taken—” Bruce started, then faltered. “—Tim.”

The name tasted wrong on his tongue. Sour. Profane. Like he was dragging it through mud just by saying it aloud. He hated how desperate it sounded, how it clung to him like a prayer he didn’t deserve to speak.

Damian’s voice cut through the moment.

“The coffin was empty.”

Something inside Bruce shifted. Not hope.

Something worse.

Something sharp and feral, crawling up his spine and settling behind his eyes.

“Timothy’s body wasn’t there,” Damian continued, stepping closer. The lights overhead caught in his eyes, turning the green into a predatory gleam. He looked straight at Bruce. “Your son wasn’t in that grave, Father.”

The words burrowed deep, stripping flesh from bone.

The Batcave fell into a viscous, suffocating silence. Even the machinery seemed to be quiet, the constant hum fading into a distant ringing in Bruce’s ears. He could see it happen—how every one of them stiffened, how disbelief flared for a heartbeat before curdling into rage. Hands clenched. Jaws tightened. Breath turned shallow.

Bruce couldn’t stop shaking.

His vision blurred at the edges, the lights smearing into pale halos as the tremor spread through his arms, his chest, his legs. He pressed his teeth together, but it did nothing to steady him. Control—something he’d built his entire life around—was slipping through his fingers, grain by grain.

The fog in his head thickened.

And somewhere beneath it all, something ancient and merciless began to wake.

“You’ve all given in to your emotions,” Damian’s voice carved its way straight into Bruce’s skull, clean and ruthless, and Bruce instinctively straightened, clenching and unclenching his fingers as if that alone might anchor him. The words echoed off the stone walls, too sharp for the hollow space they filled.

“You’ve drowned yourselves in regret,” Damian continued, each syllable measured and deliberate. “In guilt. In repentance,” his lip curled as he turned sharply toward Bruce, baring his teeth in something that wasn’t quite a snarl but came frighteningly close. “And not once did you actually listen to him,” Bruce flinched. The expression on Damian’s face was revolting in its certainty. “You turned him into a punching bag,” Damian went on, his gaze snapping to Dick like a thrown blade. Dick froze where he stood, breath hitching. “You unloaded your rage onto him,” then Damian turned to Jason. “You see him as your absolution,” Damian said quietly. “A way to cleanse yourselves of your own failures,” his eyes fell on Duke, who stood rigid, stone-faced, hands at his sides, as if bracing for a sentence already passed. “You see the past, never Timothy himself.”

The Cave felt smaller with every word, the air pressing heavier against Bruce’s lungs. Damian stepped aside, circling past Dick, and this time, no one stopped him.

“My grandfather may be a villain,” Damian said over his shoulder, voice flat, almost bored. “But you are no better.”

At the mouth of the Cave, he stopped and turned, looked down at them.

“And neither am I,” he added, green eyes full of shame and sorrow. “But at least I asked for forgiveness without expecting it,” he said as he opened the door, leaving all of them behind. “Because none of us deserve it.”

Tim paced the room at an unhurried rhythm, fingers trailing along the spines of ancient books, eyes lingering on titles etched in flaking gold. He could feel it—the weight of a gaze burning into the back of his skull.

“Pretty dramatic kidnapping,” Tim drawled, plucking one of the books from the shelf. He wiped a layer of dust from its cover with his sleeve and flipped it open. The script curled across the page in unfamiliar symbols—something close to Persian, maybe it was Persian. He didn’t know the language, only recognized it from artifacts and manuscripts his parents had brought back from overseas digs. “But you left a witness. And you didn’t even tie me up,” he added lightly. “You should really work on your skills.”

“Perhaps I should have done it the way Ra’s did.”

The words hit like ice water.

For a heartbeat, Tim’s blood seemed to stop moving altogether. He forced himself to stay still, to breathe, turning slowly to face Klarion. The witch boy was lounging behind the desk, fingers idly combing through the fur of the cat in his lap, a smug, knowing smile tugging at his lips.

“What?” Klarion asked sweetly. “Did I stir up a few memories?”

This time Tim flinched. The book slipped from his fingers and hit the floor with a dull slap, pages splaying open.

“Soon, you’ll understand everything.”

“How do you know about them?” Tim demanded, pouring every ounce of willpower into steadying his voice. His heart hammered against his ribs. He had suspected for a while now that those dreams weren’t dreams at all, and now he had confirmation. What chilled him was that Klarion knew too.

And there was only one reason he would.

“Because I’m the one who left them for you,” the witch said with a crooked grin. He leaned forward, elbows settling onto the desk, fingers lacing together as he peered at Tim through them. His eyes were dark pools of amusement, far too attentive. Tim felt the unease coil tighter in his chest. “You were supposed to forget everything. But I decided that would be far too boring.”

“Boring?” Tim frowned, lips pressing into a thin line.

Klarion shrugged, tilting his chin slightly, as if discussing the weather. “I’m not particularly tied to Ra’s. At first, his little games intrigued me. Watching people struggle is fun, for a while.” His voice dulled with disinterest. “But he dragged it out. Too long. And I got bored.”

Tim bent down, retrieving the fallen book and sliding it back into its place onto the shelf. The paper rasped softly beneath his fingers. His thoughts were already clicking into alignment, pieces snapping together whether he liked it or not.

So his instincts hadn’t lied to him. Those dreams hadn’t been dreams at all.

“You said I was supposed to forget everything,” Tim said slowly, eyes never leaving Klarion’s face, tracking every twitch, every flicker of reaction. Klarion only arched a brow, mockery written plainly across his expression. “I thought this was another dimension. Or another time. Or both.” Tim swallowed, the air suddenly feeling heavier in his lungs. “But if what you’re saying is true, then…”

He didn’t finish the sentence. He didn’t need to.

The silence that followed felt deliberate—thick, expectant, as though the room itself was waiting for him to understand just how deep the trap went.

Tim faltered, his thoughts stalling, words dissolving on his tongue, because the conclusion his deduction was dragging him toward made no sense at all.

But the coffin had been empty…

“Oh, fascinating, isn’t it?” Klarion said, not bothering to hide the amusement curling through his voice. There was delight there, bright and cruel. Joy stripped of warmth, sharpened into something monstrous. “But if you truly want to know what happened to you,” he leaned back slightly, eyes glinting, “you’ll have to agree to a deal.”

Tim stiffened, studying the youthful face across from him. Smooth features. Almost harmless. A deliberate contradiction to the darkness in Klarion’s eyes, where something ancient stirred—knowledge layered upon centuries, watching him patiently. It was a sharp reminder of what Klarion truly was.

“And what exactly does this deal involve?” Tim asked evenly, forcing control into his voice as he stepped farther into the room, closer to the desk. The air felt colder here, heavy with old magic and dust. He knew nothing good ever came from bargaining with a devil.

But sometimes, the devil was the only one willing to stand on your side.

And refusing that kind of help was a luxury Tim had never been able to afford.

“What price are you willing to pay?” Klarion smiled then, baring his teeth. Tim froze. “What is your mind worth?”

A price.

What price had Tim Drake ever had?

What price was Red Robin worth?

Did it exist at all?

Tim didn’t have an answer.

But there was one thing he knew with absolute certainty.

“Take whatever you want,” he whispered, his face carefully schooled into indifference.

Tim Drake had already lost everything.

Stripped bare. Emptied out. There was nothing left for him to cling to.

Klarion sprang up like an overexcited teenager, scooping the cat into his arms and laughing under his breath. He skirted around the desk with a light, almost bouncing step before stopping directly in front of Tim—too close, invading his space. Tim didn’t retreat. He forced his shoulders to stay loose, his expression blank, even as his pulse jumped and a cold prickle crawled up the back of his neck. Every instinct screamed at him to put distance between them, but he held his ground, meeting Klarion’s gaze with a steadiness he didn’t entirely feel.

“Excellent!” Klarion chirped. “In that case, you’ll learn the price when your memories return to you.”

Something in his gaze made Tim doubt it would be that simple.

Dark energy bloomed in Klarion’s palm, thick and wrong, distorting the air around his hand. Tim instinctively stepped back, his fingers snapping to his belt on reflex, only to close on nothing. He had no weapon. Realizing it a second too late, he let his hands fall, his brows knitting together in frustration.

“Oh, don’t look at me like that,” Klarion said lightly, waving his free hand as if shooing away a flee. “I’m just trying to hold up my end of the bargain.”

Tim lifted his head, eyes tracking the shadowy glow in Klarion’s palm before searching his face for any sign of deceit.

He found only a crooked smile. And the longer it lingered, the more it scraped at his nerves.

“Before I let you touch me, allow me one question,” Klarion mockingly lifted a brow, but didn’t interrupt, granting Tim the floor with an indulgent tilt of his head. Tim felt his throat go dry, his voice sinking low and rough as if it might fail him, but he had to know. He needed to know. “Am I… am I the same person?” he asked. “The me now, and the me from before—are we the same? Am I still me?”

Tim caught himself holding his breath.

Klarion, cruelly deliberate, let the silence stretch. His dark gaze pinned Tim in place, and the longer it lingered, the more Tim felt it crawl under his skin, raising goosebumps along his arms. He knew how to deal with Ra’s—strategies, patterns, rules. Klarion was something else entirely. A wild piece on the chessboard. A move without logic. One that didn’t care whether the game survived him.

“Well,” Klarion drawled at last, tilting his head, long fingers idly stroking his chin, savoring the control he held, “how to put this… Yes and no.”

His eyes traced Tim from head to toe, slow and evaluative, like a child inspecting a new toy before deciding how to break it.

“You see,” he continued lightly, “death is terribly picky. It doesn’t like to give back what it’s claimed. You were supposed to die. Twice,” the words landed heavy. “And yet,” Klarion went on, almost cheerfully, “your heart is still beating. Your body still functions. Your mind is still intact.” His smile widened, sharp and knowing. “And so is your soul.”

He leaned in just enough for Tim to feel the wrongness of him, pressing into the space between breaths.

“That,” Klarion finished sadistically, “is where things get interesting.”

Tim dragged in a jagged breath, fighting the instinct to collapse into panic. Not now. Not here. If he broke, he would lose the only control he had.

“But if I tell you everything outright,” Klarion murmured pleasantly, “where would the fun be?”

He knew exactly where to press. And the worst part—it worked. It didn’t matter where Tim was from, which world or which life had spat him out.

He was still just a forgotten Robin.

“Begin,” Tim forced out through clenched teeth.

He didn’t move as the hand veined with thick, crawling darkness reached for his temples. The air turned cold, viscous, as if it had weight. The moment Klarion touched him, the world collapsed.

The room vanished.

Tim plunged downward, dragged into a lightless abyss, an ocean without surface or floor. Pressure crushed in from every direction, squeezing his chest, his skull, his thoughts. No sound. No up. No down. Just endless black swallowing him whole.

He turned, twisted—nothing. Emptiness stretched forever.

Then—

White.

A blinding rupture tore across his vision, searing through the dark, and pain detonated behind his eyes, violent and splitting, as if his head were being pried open from the inside. His body stopped obeying him. Muscles locked. His limbs felt bound, cinched tight by invisible chains that dug into bone.

He screamed.

The sound ripped out of him raw and animal, tearing his throat open as his chest convulsed. Tears burned down his face, hot and uncontrollable, as the agony spread, crushing everywhere at once.

Inside him. Through him.

Pain on pain on pain.

“Come now, detective,” Ra’s voice echoed from nowhere and everywhere, smooth and delighted. “You can’t hold out forever.”

Tim thrashed against nothing, against everything.

“I will have you, Timothy.”

The words sank into him like hooks. The pain spiked again, merciless, drowning thought, swallowing memory.

“You leave me no choice, detective.”

And the darkness closed in, eager and patient, waiting for him to finally break.

He couldn’t scream anymore.

Blood slicked his skin, warm and sticky, clinging to him in dark streaks. His throat was ruined, vocal cords torn raw, every breath scraping like broken glass. Air refused to fill his lungs. Each attempt was weaker than the last, shallow and desperate, as he felt life begin to drain out of him.

“Don’t worry, detective,” Ra’s voice purred, intimate and cruel. “This isn’t the end. We only need to convince Batman that his little bird is dead.”

Tim couldn’t think. Thought slipped through his fingers like water. The edges of the world blurred, vision tunneling as consciousness began to loosen its grip.

And yet—even at the threshold of death—he heard a familiar voice cut through the haze.

“You have seven days, Ra’s.”

“I had hoped,” Ra’s said in a falsely soft voice, “that this time I might persuade you.”

Tim barely registered the shift before the cold hit him. Chains bit into his wrists, his ankles, his chest—iron-heavy, leeching warmth from his skin. The chill sank deep, into muscle and bone, leaving him shuddering despite himself. Every movement made the metal scrape, sending a dull ache through his body.

Ra’s stood before him, calm as ever.

In his hand was a dagger—its blade thin and elegant, catching what little light there was and reflecting it in a muted, sickly gleam. It hovered close, close enough that Tim could feel the air move when Ra’s adjusted his grip.

“But it has been so long,” Ra’s continued, almost regretful. His gaze snapped to Tim’s face.

The look alone was enough to steal the breath from his lungs. Panic flooded him, sudden and violent, his chest tightening as if the chains had wrapped around his ribs instead. He tried to inhale and couldn’t—air stuttering uselessly at the back of his throat.

“You are no longer the Timothy I require.”

The words settled heavily, and in that moment, Tim understood something far worse than pain or death—

To them, he was already a corpse.

A flash.

He was running.

Running as hard as his body would allow, lungs burning, legs screaming, but the shadows chased him—stretching, multiplying, closing in from every side. No matter how fast he moved, something kept dragging at him, fingers hooked into his spine, yanking him back—

The ground slammed into him. Steel kissed his throat.

“Don’t struggle, little bird,” Slade murmured above him. “You’ve run enough.”

He was completely at Slade’s mercy.

No weapon. No suit. No armor—nothing but skin and fear. His attempt to break free was pathetic, useless, and then that familiar dark magic flared before his eyes.

It invaded.

It drove straight into his ribs, a violent, invisible force clawing inward, ripping through him as if it meant to tear his heart out by hand. Tim screamed, his body arching off the ground, writhing like something caught on a hook. Too strong hands pinned him down, crushing his shoulders, his wrists, his legs.

He could only scream.

And scream.

And scream.

Until his voice shredded itself raw. But the sound meant nothing.

Three burning gazes watched him from above, silent witnesses to his agony. Tears blurred his vision as he begged, sobbing, choking on his own breath.

“Dad…please…save me—”

The words broke apart on his tongue.

Because to Bruce, Tim was never a son.

He was just a soldier.

Tim’s eyes flew open.

Darkness.

Not shadow, not dimness—nothing. A solid, suffocating black. His breath hitched as panic surged, hot and immediate.

He was blind. He couldn’t see. He couldn’t move.

His body felt distant, unreal, like it had been packed in wet cement. He tried to inhale deeper, but the air felt thick, heavy in his lungs.

“Pull yourself together,” the voice came from somewhere to his left, or maybe from inside his skull. Tim twisted instinctively, or tried to, and froze. Someone was standing in front of him.

His reflection.

Or not a reflection.

It was Tim Drake. Older. Taller. His shoulders were broader, his posture steadier, like someone who had learned how to carry weight without collapsing under it. His voice was firm, controlled, but his eyes betrayed him. They were soft. Sad. Worn down by things Tim hadn’t lived through yet.

“Why did you agree to the deal?” the older Tim asked.

“I—” Tim opened his mouth, then shut it again. No words came.

Because he was confused? Because he was tired of being alone? Because he wanted answers—any answers—no matter the cost?

“Tim Drake always found the answers himself. Always solved the hardest cases with his own mind,” the words cut in from the darkness behind him.

Tim spun around. Another Tim stood there.

This one was closer to his own age—just a little older. A few centimeters taller. Longer hair falling into his eyes. If not for that, they could have been twins. But unlike the first, this one made no effort to soften himself.

His stare was cold. Unforgiving. Stripped of empathy.

“I chose death,” this Tim said flatly, “because I refused to let anyone take my mind,” he took a step closer. “So why did you let them?”

Tim stumbled backward, his heart slamming violently against his ribs. There was nowhere to go.

No walls. No ground. Just an endless black void that swallowed sound and distance alike.

He couldn’t hide from them.

He couldn’t run.

He couldn’t escape himself.

“It’s a deal with the Devil,” the older Tim exhaled, shaking his head. Dark strands slipped loose, falling into his eyes as he looked at Tim through them, his gaze heavy with something close to grief. “You didn’t sell your mind. And you didn’t sell your body. You sold our soul.”

“Our…” The word caught fast in Tim’s throat. He swallowed hard, his mouth suddenly dry, and looked at them—at himself—with open fear tightening his chest. “You mean…we’re the same…person?”

The two Tims exchanged a glance. The younger one let out a slow, exhausted breath, like someone who had explained this before. More than once. Maybe he had. Judging by time alone, he had been the first to die.

Ra’s had wanted to break Tim by killing him and placing his body at Batman’s feet.

No one searches for the dead.

And a Robin who had forgotten his own death would keep waiting to be saved. Waiting for someone who would never come.

Ra’s wanted to break Tim. To fracture his mind completely.

The older Tim had died believing that, after all those years, no one had come for him.

“Klarion has many abilities,” the younger Tim began, running a hand through his hair in a tired, restless motion. His fingers trembled slightly. “But even he can’t revive a dead body while keeping the mind intact. If he tried to bring our bodies back…” He hesitated, jaw tightening. “We’d be nothing but hollow puppets in his hands. Soulless. Meat he could discard whenever it stopped being useful.”

Tim nodded slowly.

He knew this. He had studied necromancy—how it worked, what it took and what it left behind. The theory was familiar.

But what all of this was pointing toward… It made his stomach twist.

The darkness around them seemed to press closer, as if listening. As if waiting for him to understand. And Tim found himself clinging to one desperate thought, over and over again.

Please. Let me be wrong.

But Tim Drake was never wrong.

“When Ra’s killed me, he killed the body, and Klarion took the soul for safekeeping,” Tim said quietly. He closed his eyes, for one fragile moment, hoping this was nothing more than a nightmare he would soon wake from. But the other Tim’s voice didn’t fade. It didn’t echo. It stayed. “He couldn’t revive the body. But he could take another one. One where the heart was still beating.”

“There are countless parallel universes,” the older Tim continued, his voice distant, almost vacant. “Endless variations of pasts and futures, all tangled together. But all it takes is a single anomaly, and everything starts to fracture. Klarion is powerful, but even he can’t move directly against fate,” his gaze drifted into nothingness, unfocused, as though he were staring through layers of worlds stacked atop one another. “There are universes that differ by the smallest details,” he went on. “So small they disappear in the larger picture. And those were the ones Klarion found.”

His lips twisted into a crooked smile, but his eyes stayed terrifyingly empty as he finally turned back to Tim.

“I didn’t remember any of this back then. But this body…” His voice lowered. “My body was swapped with the dead body of another Tim in the grave. In my world, where Ra’s kept me imprisoned, I was supposed to die. I should have died.”

The silence pressed in, suffocating.

“Apparently,” he added flatly, “my soul had already been broken. But my heart was still beating. And that was enough for Klarion to do what he did.”

The words settled like ash in Tim’s lungs.

It wasn’t a resurrection. It wasn’t survival. It was a substitution.

And somewhere, in some universe, a grave had never been empty at all.

“He didn’t defy death,” the younger Tim added. “He only took the body and handed death the souls that were meant to leave their worlds. That’s why no anomaly occurred.”

For the first time, the chill in his eyes dissolved. Regret surfaced there instead as he looked at both of them.

“You were already dead. Both of you,” he said quietly. “I’m sorry… I’m sorry I couldn’t stop it. Sorry you weren’t allowed a dignified end.”

“This isn’t your fault,” the older Tim said, stepping closer and resting a hand on his shoulder. “Besides,” he added gently, “Red Robin has always trusted only himself. Isn’t that right?”

Something bitter lodged in Tim’s throat. The sound that escaped him wasn’t a word—more like the broken whine of a wounded animal. Both Tims turned toward him, fixing him with those familiar blue eyes, the same ones he’d stared into countless times in mirrors.

“If we’re dead,” Tim whispered, his voice shaking, “if all that binds us is a single soul…then why are there three of us here?”

Pain bloomed behind his eyes. His stomach twisted violently, and the darkness crept back into his vision, swallowing the edges of the world.

“Because our mind is our greatest weapon,” the younger Tim replied, but his voice was already drifting, stretching thin, pulled away by distance. “Even sharing one soul, the mind can still distinguish us. It’s capable of far more than you think, Robin.”

Tim couldn’t see them anymore. The ground vanished beneath him.

He was falling—

falling and falling and falling—

Into a bottomless void, his body weightless, his thoughts unraveling. And yet, there was no fear. No panic.

“You’re not alone, Robin. We are both with you.”

The words followed him down into the dark.

“Timothy!”
