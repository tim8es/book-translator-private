“I don’t have much time,” Tim said on the screen, casting quick, restless glances over his shoulder. Then he stopped, still breathing hard. Even through the grainy darkness of the recording, Bruce could see the sweat on his forehead, the way it caught the light. “Klarion is working with Ra’s. He…” Tim swallowed, his eyes flicking uncertainly back to the camera. Bruce felt sick. The confusion on his son’s face made his stomach twist. Tim was always certain. Tim was always three steps ahead. His son. This was his son. And he looked scared. “…I don’t know exactly what he did,” Tim continued quietly, “but my memories—they’re all messed up.” His lips curved into a small, broken smile. “Or maybe I’m just losing my mind after all those years of torture. Maybe none of this is real. Maybe it’s all in my head. Maybe Dick was right…”

A ragged breath sounded off-camera near Bruce, but he couldn’t look away. He was terrified of missing even a second.

“For years,” Tim went on, voice steadier than it had any right to be, “Ra’s kept me locked away. Trying to break me. Trying to bend me. Trying to turn me into a pawn on his chessboard.”

The camera shook in Tim’s hand, slipping and cutting off half his face. There was a faint scuffling sound somewhere beyond the frame.

“I managed to convince him it worked,” Tim said. “I escaped.” A bitter huff of air left him. “But I didn’t expect him to send Slade after me instead of his men.”

He gave a short laugh, but there was no humor in it. None at all.

“I don’t think I’ll make it back to the Cave,” Tim admitted. “And Ra’s was right about one thing. No one ever came for me.”

Bruce clenched his teeth, rage burning his throat—at Ra’s, at himself. He had made so many mistakes, each worse than the last. Tim was his son, and he had never once said these words aloud.

Now he understood. And the realization was a punishment of its own.

“Red Robin was just a soldier,” Tim said softly. “Maybe it’s time I meet my end like one.”

Bruce’s hands trembled at his sides.

“This all started because of me,” Tim continued. “Ra’s is obsessed with my mind. With pulling me into his game,” he lifted his head then, and for the first time, relief flickered across his face. Bruce’s heart began to pound, faster and faster, because he knew. He knew what his son was going to say. “But don’t worry,” Tim said quietly. “I know how to end this match.”

A pause. Bruce felt his blood go cold, the world narrowing to the sound of his own pulse pounding too loud in his ears, every muscle in his body going rigid as his mind raced ahead to outcomes. “After all…a dead Robin is no use to him.”

Bruce caught sight of it from the corner of his eye—his sons frozen in place. Dick stood unnaturally still, like a wire pulled too tight. His jaw was clenched hard enough that the muscles along his neck stood out, breath shallow, uneven. His hands flexed at his sides as if he didn’t trust them not to shake. There was a wild, fractured look in his eyes. Jason looked worse. His face had drained of color, leaving him hollow and sharp-edged, eyes glassy and unfocused.

Alfred lifted a trembling hand and covered his eyes, breath hitching despite his effort to stay composed. His shoulders sagged under a weight he had carried too long, the lines on his face deepening as if grief itself had etched them there. It wasn’t fear in him so much as devastating, absolute remorse—the terrible certainty that he had failed a child he was meant to protect.

Bruce felt everything.

He felt the pain, sharp and tearing. The grief, old and new, folding in on itself. The rage—white-hot, directionless. And beneath it all, the unbearable, treacherous relief that his son was alive.

He wanted vengeance. He wanted justice. He wanted blood.

And for the first time in his life, the Dark Knight no longer knew where his morals lay.

“Hey, Bruce,” Tim’s voice rasped suddenly from the screen. Bruce lifted his chin and straightened his back. Tim’s eyes were dull, rimmed red, tears suspended but never falling. A soft smile pulled at his mouth, practiced and careful, like armor that no longer fit. “I’m guessing you’re watching this right now.”

He knew. He had always known they would find it. He hoped they would find it.

“I don’t know what happened to me,” Tim continued quietly. “I don’t know what’s going to happen to me.”

Bruce’s chest tightened, breath turning shallow.

“But I want you to know something,” Tim hesitated, just for a second. “I never blamed you.”

Bruce’s eyes flew open. Something rotten lodged itself in his chest, flooding his lungs with poisonous air. He couldn’t breathe. Couldn’t move.

“When I became Robin, I knew what I was signing up for,” Tim said. “You were clear about your boundaries. And even if I couldn’t help thinking of you as my father…I never expected to be your son.”

The words were gentle. But to Bruce, they were merciless.

“Tim Drake is loveless, unloved by everyone,” Tim went on, as if stating a simple fact. “So, it’s okay, Bruce.”

It wasn’t okay. It had never been okay.

Tim’s expression softened then, and Bruce felt like his ribs were lodged in his throat, crushing any sound before it could escape. Even now, standing at the edge of death, Tim was still thinking about others. Still making room for their comfort at the expense of his own.

“When all of this is over,” Tim said softly, “just forget me.”

Bruce’s hands curled into fists.

“Red Robin was born from betrayal and hatred,” Tim continued. “That’s not something a Robin should carry,” a faint, hollow breath left him. “Jason was right,” Tim said at last. “I was always just a cuckoo bird in the nest.”

The screen flickered. And Bruce Wayne shattered in absolute, silent ruin. He turned cautiously toward Jason, who looked no better than he felt.

His face was ashen, his eyes were haunted—almost glassy, like something had already left him behind. And Bruce was certain he caught a quiet, broken “Baby Bird,” breathed out with pure bitterness and grief.

“And Tim,” Tim suddenly said from the screen. Bruce snapped his gaze back so fast he heard a crack in his bones. Tim looked different now—serious. The smile was gone without a trace, stripped away as if it had never existed. “No matter what happens,” Tim said firmly, but gentle, “don’t agree to a deal,” Bruce’s breath caught. Then Tim spoke words Bruce had never heard from him before. “Please…”

The video cut off abruptly, the screen going dark with a soft electronic click that sounded far too loud in the silence.

It left them drowning in regret and terror.

One thought echoed in Bruce’s mind, relentless and suffocating. Tim knew.

He knew something had already happened to him.

He knew something was going to happen.

And he hadn’t trusted any of them.

He had every right not to.

Bruce had killed his son the moment he let him put on the suit. The moment he let him believe he was nothing more than a soldier.

“A deal,” Jason said quietly. Bruce turned toward him slowly, mechanically, like someone else was pulling the strings. He noticed then how Jason’s eyes gleamed with a sick, toxic green under the light. “He mentioned a deal,” Jason continued, voice tight. “What deal?”

“Whatever it was,” Duke said slowly, thoughtfully, a faint crease appeared between his brows, “it scared him.”

Bruce understood the look. Tim had always been composed—focused to the point of detachment. He never wore his emotions on the surface. Of all his sons, Tim was the only one Bruce had never been able to read. Never predict.

“None of that matters,” Dick cut in suddenly. He turned on his heel, sweeping the Cave with a sharp, restless glance, like a predator pacing a too-small enclosure. “He was ready to handle everything alone,” Dick continued, barely holding himself together, “even if it cost him his life, just so he wouldn’t become another problem for us.”

By the end of it, his words came out in a low hiss, feral and restrained, and Bruce watched him carefully.

“Where is he now?” Dick demanded. The question hit the room like a shock of ice water.

“And where’s Damian?”

They all stared at one another. Even Alfred looked momentarily lost, his composure fractured, and at that moment Bruce realized they made another mistake letting Tim slip from their fingers.

“Shit,” Jason swore, dragging a hand roughly down his face. Bruce moved instantly, crossing to the computer, already pulling up tracking protocols, already knowing—deep down—that neither Tim nor Damian would still be anywhere on the grounds of the manor.

But then Duke spoke, and Bruce felt a fragile hope flaring in his chest.

“I think,” he said, and every heartbeat in the room seemed to lean toward him, “I know where to look.”

A punch. Then another. The chain shrieked with each impact, metal crying out as the heavy bag swung wildly.

He didn’t slow. Right hook. Left. A knee driven in with brutal precision. A pivot, then a sharp kick that snapped through the air. The chain gave way with a tortured scream. The bag hit the floor hard enough to shake dust from the rafters. The echo dragged on, long after it should have died.

Slade straightened. He stared down at the fallen weight, breathing steady. No exhaustion. No satisfaction. Only heat—muscles coiled too tight, fists trembling with the need to be used again.

Then the polite clapping filled the room. One slow clap after another, each sound landing a second too late, as if it didn’t quite belong in the room.

Slade turned. The witch’s boy stood in the doorway.

Klarion was smiling, but it never reached his eyes. They were too bright, too aware, following Slade the way a cat tracks something already caught. He applauded like an audience of one, head tilted, as if savoring every detail.

“That was good,” he said with a fake kindness. “Very good. They weren’t lying,” Klarion added. “Age really doesn’t wear you down.”

He stepped into the hall.

Slade frowned and turned his back to him. He didn’t share Ra’s faith in this walking calamity. But he also knew better than to dismiss it outright. Not when he was so close to what he wanted.

Behind him, Klarion hummed quietly.

“Oh? No greeting?” he asked, drifting after him. He didn’t follow directly—never quite in Slade’s blind spot, never quite far enough to be ignored. “That hurts. And here I was thinking my news might finally make you happy.”

Slade stopped at the weapons rack. Something cold crept up his spine. He turned sharply, fingers closing around the sword’s hilt, lifting it just enough to be ready.

Klarion’s smile widened a fraction. He didn’t flinch at the violence in Slade’s expression. Didn’t blink. Just watched, looking even more pleased, as if this reaction, too, had been expected.

“Relax,” Klarion said lightly. “If I wanted you dead, you’d already be so.”

The silence afterward felt heavier than the noise had been.

“News?” Slade asked coolly. “What kind of news would you bring to me, and not to Ra’s?”

Slade wasn’t an idiot. He trusted neither of them. His loyalty belonged to no one but his own reputation.

“Oh, nothing special,” Klarion said, tilting his head. He smiled wide, teeth bared as his dark gaze gleamed with something bottomless. In that moment, Slade saw the demon for what it truly was. “I just thought you’d like to know that your little bird has left the nest…and gone hunting for Ra’s head.”

Ice flooded Slade’s veins. His fingers tightened around the sword’s hilt until his knuckles went white. One second. Two.

He moved.

The distance vanished in a blink, steel flashing as he brought the blade up to Klarion’s throat. The witch only kept smiling, irritation flickering through his amusement, as if the entire thing were nothing more than a game to him.

“What do you know?” Slade snarled through clenched teeth. He was careful. No one could know about his true plan. No one.

“What do I know?” Klarion arched a brow, an exaggerated show of thought flickering across his face. “Oh, I know many things.”

He flicked his hand idly. Sparks danced between his fingers—alive, eager—

Slade shoved the blade closer to Klarion’s throat. The smile vanished at once.

“I don’t like games that involve threats,” Klarion said coldly. The world lurched.

In the next instant, Slade was slammed back against the wall by a surge of dark magic. The impact rattled his bones. His sword was still in his hand, but his arm refused to move, locked in place as if encased in stone.

Klarion stepped closer, invading his space, watching with quiet fascination as Slade strained uselessly against the spell. The witch’s eyes gleamed, drinking it in. Slade ground his teeth.

“Let me go, witch.”

“How about no?” Klarion replied easily. “You started this. And here I was, coming in good faith.”

“Don’t insult me,” Slade shot back flatly. “You’re playing dirty.”

“Aren’t we all?” Klarion lifted a brow. With a careless flick of his hand, the magic released him. Slade staggered forward, but did not attack again. One look at Klarion was enough to understand: whatever stood before him was not human. Not even close.

“I’m doing what I’ve always done,” Klarion said, already turning toward the exit, his interest in the conversation visibly fading. “I sow chaos. The rest hardly matters to me.”

At the doorway, he paused. Slowly, he turned back, fixing Slade with a demonic grin. The kind that raised the hair on Slade’s arms despite a lifetime of fearlessness.

“Except power. Power is everything.”

Tim knew it was already too late.

Klarion had found him first. Klarion had patiently and cruelly waited and struck at the perfect moment. And before Tim surrendered what remained of his soul into the witch’s hands, there was one thing he still owed the world.

Vengeance.

For them. For himself.

For Red Robin.

There was nothing left for him to cling to. No future to protect, no life to bargain with. If this was the end, then he would finish this ballad on his own terms.

“You don’t have to come with me, Dami,” Tim said evenly, eyes fixed on the glowing coordinates projected in front of him as the aircraft cut through the dark sky. He calculated their arrival precisely—two hours until they reached the lands of Nanda Parbat. Two hours until everything ended. “This isn’t your war.”

The low hum of the engines filled the cabin, vibrating through the floor and into Tim’s bones.

“I swore to spill blood for you,” Damian replied. His voice was muted by the headset, but unwavering. “And I keep my promises, Timothy.”

Tim glanced at him sharply. Damian sat rigid and composed, shoulders squared, back straight, chin lifted with the quiet pride of a warrior who had already chosen his fate. When he felt Tim’s gaze, Damian turned toward him, only for Tim to look away again, returning his attention to the coordinates, his eyes empty as they fixed on the projected route ahead.

“I doubt Bruce will be pleased with your decision,” Tim said at last. His tone was calm, but the bitterness slipped through anyway, sharp and undeniable at the mention of Bruce’s name.

He knew the truth. He had always known it.

He wasn’t really family. Bruce had never seen him as a son. Bruce had never wanted him.

Tim had burst into his life without asking for permission, driven by nothing but desperation—desperate to save Gotham, desperate to save Batman. Hoping that the presence of a Robin might return even a trace of color to the city’s darkened streets.

He had never expected Bruce to accept him. But the hatred—the distrust in Bruce’s eyes when he looked straight at Tim—that had made something boil, hot and feral, beneath his skin.

Tim Drake had been a loyal soldier. He had hoped—God, he had hoped—that at least his work meant something.

But it turned out Red Robin meant nothing to the man he had called his father.

“My father lost the right to decide for me the moment he failed to protect you,” Damian said simply. Tim’s fingers froze above the control panel.

For a fraction of a second, the mask of indifference cracked. Just enough for Damian to notice. Then Tim forced himself to breathe in slowly through his nose, eyes sliding shut as he steadied himself. He could feel the weight of Damian’s burning stare.

Damian didn’t look away. He watched Tim as if it were a mission of his own, as if vigilance alone might keep him alive. The intensity of it pressed down on Tim’s chest, heavy and inescapable.

“And besides,” Damian added, his voice tightening just slightly, the edge beneath the control finally surfacing, “I have unfinished business with my grandfather.”

The engines roared on, the aircraft carrying them forward the lands where no one walked untouched.

“Of course,” Tim replied emptily.

Silence stretched between them. Some time passed before Tim spoke again, his voice even, final—not a suggestion, but a verdict. A statement.

“But Ra’s mine. This is my game, and I intend to finish it myself.”

Damian nodded once.

“I will be your pawn, Akhi.”

He leaned back against the seat, spine straight, shoulders settling into practiced composure. The tension in his jaw loosened, the sharp crease between his brows slowly smoothing out. He sat beside Tim like a true prince—contained, dignified, aware of his place.

And yet this time, Tim felt no arrogance.

Damian really has changed, the thought crossed his mind, unbidden. Tim’s lips curved into a faint, almost fragile smile.

His younger—or perhaps now, older—brother had accepted him as his own.

It was a shame Tim had no time left. It was a shame he would lose what he wanted most.

“Hey, Dami,” Tim said, hiding the smile behind a carefully blank stare as he turned his chair to face him fully. His gaze slowly traced Damian from head to toe.

A dark green tunic clung to his frame, embroidered with fine gold patterns that caught the dim cabin light. His wrists were tightly wrapped in bindings—reinforced, worn in, a quiet confession that his true weapons were his katanas. A matching green cloak rested across his shoulders, its edges barely stirring with the aircraft’s motion. Several daggers were strapped at his waist, and Tim had no doubt more blades were hidden in the folds of his trousers. Even his footwear—soft and flexible—looked more like ballet slippers than anything he’d worn as Robin.

This wasn’t League attire. Tim knew that for sure. It felt older. Folk-like. Ancient.

“Why aren’t you wearing the Robin suit?”

Damian’s body reacted before his words ever could.

The muscles in his shoulders tightened, subtle but unmistakable. His fingers curled briefly against the seat, nails pressing into the material before he forced them to relax. When he lifted his gaze to Tim, the green of his eyes had darkened, weighed down by something heavier than anger.

Pain. Long-standing. Unresolved.

There was longing there, too, quiet and aching.

Damian held Tim’s stare, unmoving, but the tension bled through the rigid line of his posture, through the way his breath slowed, measured, as if each inhale had to be controlled.

And Tim understood. Even before Damian spoke.

“Because the real Robin is dead, and I’m not worthy of carrying his name.”

The words landed wrong, and for a moment Tim couldn’t make sense of them. His mind stalled, caught between disbelief and something dangerously close to grief.

Damian, clearly reading the shock on Tim’s face, slowly leaned forward. He clasped his hands together, fingers locking tight, and rested his elbows on his knees. When he lifted his head, his eyes were level with Tim’s.

Tim didn’t move.

He was afraid that if he did—if he breathed too loud or shifted his weight—the moment would shatter. Like glass under pressure. Like something he wasn’t meant to witness. He hated feelings. They made him to feel naked and vulnerable.

“When you disappeared, I thought it was my chance,” Damian continued. “Finally, no one stood in my way. I could take what was rightfully mine,” Each word scraped against Tim’s ribs.  “And then I saw your body,” Damian said, regret hollowing out his voice. “When I understood what my grandfather had done to you…when I realized the one person who, despite all my attempts to kill him, kept trying to teach me how to be better was gone—”

Tim felt something twist painfully in his chest. He noticed, Tim thought, stunned. He remembered that.

“I couldn’t wear that name anymore,” Damian went on. “Because Robin is hope. And there is none left in me.”

Damian lowered his head and Tim saw the tears. Real tears. Uncontrolled. Heavy with grief.

The sight terrified him. Damian’s grief wrapped around him like heat and ice at once, and Tim didn’t know whether he wanted to step closer or vanish entirely.

“The al Ghuls believe in blood ties,” Damian whispered. “But what is the worth of those ties if they strip you of your real family?”

His lips trembled. His hands clenched harder, fingers blanching white. Damian fought it, Tim could see him fighting it, but one lone tear escaped, sliding down his cheek.

And with it, something inside Tim collapsed. It felt like his heart dropped straight into his stomach, leaving behind a hollow ache that made it hard to breathe.

“I only wanted to be loved,” Damian said, his voice barely there. “But I didn’t know how to earn it without blood. My grandfather was only proud of me when I killed.”

He fell silent, staring past Tim’s shoulder, somewhere far away. Then, barely moving his lips, Damian whispered.

“I wanted you to be proud of me too.”

Tim’s thoughts went completely blank. He had no strategy, no plan. No careful emotional distance.

Just the crushing, unbearable realization that Damian hadn’t been chasing a title.

He’d been chasing him.

“Do you think Jason would be proud of me?”

For a heartbeat, his lungs forgot what they were supposed to do. His fingers went numb, a faint buzzing crawling up his arms, and he had to swallow twice before the tightness in his throat eased enough to let air back in. Fate had never been kind to Robins—each of them was a tragedy written in blood—and Damian was standing right in the middle of his own.

“How about a deal, Dami?” Tim said instead, his voice soft and quiet. He leaned closer before he could talk himself out of it. Tim reached out and laid his palm over Damian’s clasped hands. They were warm. Tense. Rigid like a coiled blade.

Damian flinched at the contact, just barely, but Tim caught it. Saw the way his shoulders hitched, the way his breath stuttered before he forced it steady. Shock flickered across his face.

Tim smiled lightly, though his jaw ached from holding it in place.

“When all of this is over,” Tim continued, “you’ll let yourself wear the Robin suit.”

“Akhi—”

“No. Listen to me.” Tim tightened his grip, thumbs pressing into Damian’s knuckles, anchoring both of them. His pulse was loud in his ears now, a steady thud he clung to. “You don’t have to be Robin. You don’t have to follow Batman. But I want you—just for one night—to give Gotham its hope back.”

Damian’s gaze wavered. His lips parted, then pressed together again. Tim saw the conflict ripple through him in small ways: the tension in his neck, the way his fingers flexed beneath Tim’s palm before slowly, reluctantly loosening.

Tim exhaled through his nose, lips thinning, his voice turning serious. “Damian, you are worthy of being Robin. Worthy of the name every one of us carried.”

Then, just as quickly, the weight lifted, Tim’s mouth quirking into a crooked smirk, something familiar and achingly him.

“And besides,” he added, “if you want my forgiveness, you’re obligated to do it.”

Damian’s brows snapped together immediately.

“That’s blackmail, Akhi.”

Tim shrugged, releasing his hands and turning back toward the screen. The absence of contact made his fingers ache, a ghost sensation lingering in his palm. “It’s called blackmail only when the outcome is either me gaining something or me taking something from you,” He glanced back over his shoulder, raising an eyebrow. “This is just a nudge in the right direction. Call it therapy.”

Damian huffed despite himself. But the edge was gone now. His shoulders settled against the seat, tension bleeding out of him in quiet increments.

“If that’s what you want, Timothy,” he said at last.

“I want coffee and Alfred’s cookies,” Tim muttered, paying attention to the coodinates.

There was a soft rustle behind him, barely noticeable, and he ignored it—right up until a metal lunch box appeared in front of him.

“…What’s this?” Tim asked.

“Forgive me, Timothy. There is no coffee,” Damian said solemnly, pressing the container into his hands, “but I brought the cookies.”

Tim blinked. Once. Then again. He stared down at the box in pure astonishment as if not believing it was real.

“You…does Alfred know you took his cookies?” was the first thing Tim managed to ask.

“I am certain Alfred will be pleased that I took them,” Damian replied without missing a beat.

Tim lifted his gaze.

Damian was already sitting back in his seat again, posture loose, expression carefully bored, as if he hadn’t been crying just minutes ago. As if Tim hadn’t seen it. As if Damian hadn’t trusted him with his feelings.

Tim drew in a slow breath, steadying himself, then said in a quiet voice.

“Thank you, Dami.”

“You’re welcome, Akhi,” Damian answered.

And for a moment, with cold metal against his palms and the familiar weight of Alfred’s cookies in his hands, Tim felt something dangerously close to hope.

Duke felt it again—that subtle wrongness crawling under his skin. The same feeling he’d had right before Tim disappeared.

They’d spent a lot of time together back then. And even though Tim was guarded, careful with every word, things still slipped sometimes. Small phrases. Half-thoughts. Questions that lingered long after they were spoken.

Duke remembered one night in particular.

“Duke,” Tim had asked during patrol, his voice steady but too deliberate, “if I suddenly disappeared…what would you do?”

Duke rarely patrolled Gotham at night, but that evening Tim had asked him to come along. And Duke hadn’t been able to say no.

The question made him frown, his steps slowing as he turned toward Tim. The cowl hid his eyes, swallowed whatever expression might have given him away, and that uncertainty set Duke on edge. Gotham hummed below them—sirens in the distance, the wind scraping between buildings, neon bleeding weak color into the streets.

“I think you already know the answer, Tim,” Duke said.

He stepped up beside him at the edge of the roof, boots planted firmly on the concrete, and looked out over the city’s dark sprawl. The night air was cold against his face, sharp and grounding.

“I’d do everything I could to find you.”

He’d wanted to ask what had prompted the question. The tension in Tim’s voice hadn’t been subtle. There had been doubt there—quiet, restrained, but heavy all the same.

But Duke also knew Tim.

Push too hard, and Tim would vanish—not physically, but inward. Slip through your fingers like smoke, leaving only the echo of what you hadn’t asked in time.

So Duke had stayed silent.

And now, standing in the present with that memory pressing against his ribs, he understood what his instincts had been trying to warn him about all along.

“And if I asked you not to look for me?” Tim asked suddenly.

For a heartbeat, Duke thought he’d misheard him, that he had misunderstood the words. But even without seeing Tim’s eyes, Duke could read the tension in the set of his shoulders, the rigid stillness of his posture. This wasn’t a careless question. This was deliberate.

“Red…” Duke breathed, the word slipping out before he could stop it.

His thoughts scattered. He was terrified of crossing a line—terrified because he still didn’t know where he stood in this family. He didn’t know where Tim placed him. Duke thought of Tim as his brother, without hesitation. But did Tim feel the same?

“You can’t ask me something like that, Tim,” Duke said finally, his voice rough around the edges. “You’re my brother.”

He didn’t hide the desperation in his tone. He didn’t try to soften it. Tim needed to understand what those words did to him, how they hollowed him out, how they tightened something painful in his chest.

Silence stretched between them.

The city seemed to hold its breath. Gotham’s streets below fell into an unnatural quiet—no sirens, no distant shouting, just the low hum of the wind sliding between buildings. Time itself felt suspended, fragile. And still, Duke couldn’t shake the feeling that something was wrong. Deeply wrong.

“You misunderstood me, Duke,” Tim said at last. He turned to face him, his movements controlled, his voice serious. “I’m asking you because you’re my brother. You need to trust me.”

Duke frowned, confusion and dread tangling together in his gut.

“Trust you with what, Red?” he asked quietly, the question trembling despite his effort to keep it steady.

Tim’s lips curved into a fleeting smile. A smile that didn’t reach his eyes, still hidden behind the mask.

“That I’ll win this game,” Tim said.

And Duke realized, too late, that Tim had already decided how it would end.

“And If I stop being myself,” Tim said quietly, staring out over Gotham, “then whatever’s left isn’t the thing you’re meant to save.”
