***Caleb Whitmore.***

Playboy. Troublemaker. Master of Scandal. The Youngest Headache of the Crown.

When Arian first looked into his Prince's background, he almost couldn't hold back a deep sigh.

There were photographs. Far too many of them.

Caleb leaving parties at unreasonable hours, Caleb laughing with strangers, Caleb leaning against expensive cars with his tie hanging loose around his neck, Caleb at university functions with a champagne glass in one hand and an expression of complete amusement on his face, Caleb photographed beside women who had names Arian recognized and women whose names he did not.

The British press seemed particularly fascinated by him.

Perhaps because he was a Prince. Perhaps because he was young.

Or perhaps because Caleb seemed to possess a rare and infuriating talent for finding trouble wherever he went and then smiling as though he had nothing whatsoever to do with it.

Arian had spent an entire evening reading articles about him, although he didn't have much time to spare for such trivial things.

He did it anyway. And he should have known better.

The first article called Caleb ‘the Crown's favourite rebel’. Another described him as ‘Britain's most eligible bachelor’. A third, written with considerably less affection, referred to him as ‘the youngest menace the monarchy has ever produced’.

Arian found that one almost charming.

The gossip columns were worse. According to them, Caleb was spoiled beyond reason, impossible to discipline, hopelessly flirtatious, chronically late, dangerously impulsive and possessed of an ego so enormous that it required its own postcode.

He had supposedly once left a diplomatic dinner because he was bored. He had supposedly shamelessly climbed onto the roof of a royal residence after losing a bet, and then he had supposedly stolen a horse when he was seventeen.

That last one turned out to be untrue, however.

He had borrowed it, as he explained later during a press conference. Without permission and just for one day.

There was apparently a distinction.

Arian leaned back in his chair as he read the report, one eyebrow raised.

"How entertaining.”

His consigliere, who had been unfortunate enough to witness the research process and the perfunctory comments leaving his boss's tightly clenched teeth, had wisely said nothing.

Arian continued, mentally noting every new version of the Prince he found in texts:

Caleb was *moody.*

Caleb was *childish.*

Caleb was *arrogant.*

Caleb was *reckless.*

Caleb was *spoiled.*

Caleb was *a flirt.*

Caleb was *difficult.*

Caleb was apparently everything a sensible man should avoid—no, scratch that. Every sensible *human being* should avoid.

So Arian should have been discouraged from the start, because an anomaly like Prince Caleb could only bring storms to his already muddy waters. He would bring trouble, mess and chaos.

Instead, he found himself increasingly curious. For some apparent reason he didn't yet understand but was in no hurry to look for.

He read interviews. Watched old footage. Looked through photographs that had nothing to do with scandals and everything to do with the ordinary life of a Prince who had grown up beneath constant observation.

In some photographs, Caleb looked bored while standing next to his siblings, all perfectly positioned, perfectly clothed.

In others, amused—those usually pictured young Caleb on horseback or sitting in the audience during military parades.

Sometimes he looked almost painfully young. Mostly when his face seemed to scrunch in discomfort when too many flashes blinded him, or when he looked overwhelmed with too many hands reaching out waiting to be acknowledged.

And sometimes there was something in his expression that made Arian pause for a little. These types of shots were taken from afar, slightly blurry, angled as if taken secretly.

There was a kind of distance on Prince's face. Aloofness.

But it didn't seem to be that kind the royals usually were born with; it wasn't superficial vanity or an ego inflated by his origins.

Caleb had grown up inside a palace. That explained some things, but it did not explain all of them.

Arian was particularly interested in the reports describing Caleb as ‘spoiled rotten’.

The Prince had access to almost anything money could buy since the day he was born into the royal family. Cars, horses, clothes, jewellery, travel. The finest food in Britain. He had been raised by servants and surrounded by people whose occupation was, quite literally, making his life easier.

So it was only natural that Arian expected arrogance. He expected demands and the sort of entitlement that came naturally to men who had never heard the word ‘no’ without someone immediately apologizing for saying it.

So when Caleb finally arrived at the Conti estate, Arian was prepared.

He had catalogued every version of the Prince the press had ever manufactured, and he had told himself that there would be nothing left to surprise him.

He was wrong within the first three minutes.

The British Prince arrived not in the polished, camera-ready composure Arian had seen in a hundred photographs, but windblown and faintly rumpled, his tie askew, his expression somewhere between fury and disbelief, as though he had spent the entire drive from the airport rehearsing several different speeches and discarded all of them the moment he stepped through the door, choosing violence.

Arian could understand that; the Prince wasn't here willingly, after all.

"I do hope," Caleb exclaimed, before anyone had thought to offer him so much as a chair, "that someone here intends to explain why my passport was confiscated and shredded at the border like I was smuggling something more interesting than myself!"

His voice carried across the dining hall, bright and clipped and entirely unbothered by the size of the room, or the number of armed men standing quietly along its walls, or the fact that the man seated at the head of the table happened to be the single most dangerous person in Sicily.

Arian did not stand. He rarely stood for anyone. But his eyes stared at the person before him, not leaving since he came in.

"Prince Caleb," he greeted, gesturing to the empty seat across from him. "You're late for dinner."

"I was *detained,"* Caleb said, dropping into the chair with far more grace than his tone suggested, his eyes falling heavily on Arian, "by your customs officer who seemed personally offended by the existence of my cheekbones. Do you know what that's like? Being denied entry to a country on the grounds of being *too* punchable?"

Arian’s brow rose in spite of himself, and for a second he was too stunned to speak. Well—bold he was, that's for sure.

"I imagine it happens rarely,” he replied smoothly, watching the man from a new angle.

"Constantly, actually. It's a curse." Caleb reached for the wine before anyone poured it for him, inspected the label with theatrical scrutiny, and made a small sound of approval. "Well. At least someone in this house has taste. And who may you be, mister…?"

Arian watched him with the particular stillness he usually reserved for men he had already decided to kill. Gone was patience.

“Arian Conti. I'm the Godfather of the Conti family. And you're in my house.”

Caleb did not seem to notice the glare, or did not care.

"So," the Prince continued, swirling his glass, "it seems you're the boss here. I confess total nescience upon the subject of Sicilian hospitality, but I would gladly follow your lecture, Godfather, should you feel inclined to explain why I'm here, why my documents were destroyed, and why I appear to have been delivered to your dinner table like a rather ill-tempered parcel."

A faint sound escaped Arian's consigliere near the far wall—something between a cough and a laugh, quickly smothered.

Arian did not smile. But something in his chest shifted, unfamiliar and irritating.

"You're here," he said, slowly, "because your family and I have reached an arrangement."

"Ah. Splendid. And what exactly, pray tell, that arrangement is, Godfather?”

Caleb asked that question with a smirk that hadn't left his lips since he arrived, and it made Arian's blood boil from irritation. He would admit it—the Prince really was a professional rage baiter, and yes, he had a very punchable face. No matter how pretty it was.

And Arian felt an immense urge to wipe that smirk off those lips.

So he stood up, from his seat and calmly approached the young man, with satisfaction observing the slowly crumbling bravado inside those dark eyes.

And when he was there, the closest he has ever been to the Prince, he leaned in, close, very close, and whispered into the Prince's ear—

“You're here, dear Caleb, to marry me. I'm your future husband.”

For a moment, Caleb didn't move and Arian took that opportunity to study him.

The photographs had been accurate about one thing.

Caleb was beautiful.

More beautiful in person, unfortunately.

His eyes were dark and bright beneath thick lashes, his features softened by the faintest trace of mischief, and there was something almost unfair about the ease with which his mouth curved when he smiled.

But even though the smirk remained on his lips, it had gone strange—frozen there, no longer quite reaching his eyes, as though his face hadn't yet received the message his mind had.

"I'm sorry," he blurted, voice light, almost pleasant. "I don't think I heard you correctly. It sounded, for one horrifying moment, as though you said ‘husband’.”

"I did."

Caleb turned his head, just slightly, so that his face was inches from Arian's, close enough that Arian could see the exact moment understanding won out over disbelief; could see it move through him like something physical, tightening his jaw, flattening the easy amusement from his eyes.

"Ah," Caleb murmured softly. His lips trembled. “Brilliant.”

He said it the way a man might say it standing at the edge of a cliff.

"Forgive me," he continued, slightly less confident, "but I believe there's been some clerical error. I was under the impression I was sent to Sicily to smooth over an unfortunate diplomatic incident. Not to be married off to a man I met four minutes ago, however charming his dinner conversation and armed waiters.”

Arian smiled, finally regaining some grip over the unruly Prince.

"There's no error. It's regarding your brother, Andrew.”

"There's *always* an error where my family's concerned, Mr. Conti. It's practically a royal tradition."

"Your family made the decision on your behalf."

"Of course they did. Absolutely brilliant. My brother maims one of your men in Sicily, and the Crown's solution is to hand you a spare Prince like a fruit basket," Caleb laughed, short, humorless, more breath than sound. Then, he looked up, his dark eyes half hidden beneath his long eyelashes and chestnut hair.

"I don't know what you were told about me, Godfather," Caleb said, quieter than before, "but I'd hate for you to labor under the impression that I'm easily handled. And sorry for disappointing you, but I cannot be your bride. I'm a man, after all.”

Arian looked down at the man, observing. His eyes screamed ‘I’m not afraid of you’, but his trembling lips, his tapping foot and slightly flushed neck whispered something completely different.

Fear. Anxiety.

*Oh,* thought Arian, not really able to stop the warm amusement filling his lungs. *I've caught you.*

He leaned in again, slow, until their eyes were almost at the same level, and watched Caleb's breath catch, just barely.

"Good," Arian said quietly.

Caleb blinked.

*"Good?"*

"I have no interest in easily handled things." Arian's voice was low, unbothered, almost gentle. "And I'm aware you're a man. It doesn't change anything. So you have only one option—marry me. Be my partner in crime, Caleb.”

“Are you insane?!”

“Probably.”

For a long moment, Caleb only stared at him, some furious, fascinated calculation working behind his eyes, as if he was seriously searching for a physical sign of mental illness hidden behind Arian's eyes.

He wouldn't find it, Arian thought, sitting back in his chair, the bewilderment on Prince's face a feast for his tense nerves.

"Welcome to Sicily, dear Caleb."
