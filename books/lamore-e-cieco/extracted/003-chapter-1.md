## Chapter 1: Surrounded

Arian hated weak things.

He loathed the sound of a frightened voice, hoarse from pleading. The trembling of hands too powerless to fight. The wet sheen of tears welling in eyes as the realization dawned that weeping was futile in the face of power.

He couldn't stand the look of pitiful sorrow on the furrowed faces of the weak as they gazed up at someone stronger.

As if fear made them innocent.

As if defenselessness absolved them of the shame that accompanies it.

Arian had no patience for any of that. Nor the courage.

Weakness had never brought him mercy. Instead, it brought him bruises. Laughter. Blood under his fingernails and dirt on his knees.

He remembered being small. He remembered being afraid.

He remembered how much he had hated his mother for leaving him like it.

And then how much he hated himself for hating her.

That was the part no one understood.

Arian had not despised the boys who hurt him nearly as much as he had despised the boy who allowed himself to be hurt, to be made weak.

His mother used to tell him that there was nothing shameful about being afraid.

She was wrong.

At least, that was what the Conti family taught him, proving it day after day, constantly. Until he forgot what afraid even meant.

His mother had never belonged to the Conti.

That was the first sin engraved in Arian's skin right after he was born.

She had not been born a Conti. She had not carried the name, the blood, or the history that mattered so much to the family. She had been beautiful, clever, and entirely unsuitable.

And she had given the Family a bastard son.

He learned the word before he understood its meaning. Bastard. Unwanted. Stranger. Beast.

It followed him through the corridors of the Conti estate, whispered behind doors, spat between clenched teeth.

His name was Arian, but it was a name his father's legitimate wife never used when speaking to him directly.

She called him other things.

His father's worst *mistake.* The *stray.* The *thing* which should never have existed.

Arian's mother tried to shield him from it, hiding him in her warm embrace of bruised arms. She sang to him, she hugged him, she told him he matters.

And he actually believed her, trusted her protection, and was ready to fight the world with her.

But—

She failed.

Perhaps no one could have protected them in that house.

The story they told him was simple.

Wolves.

They found what was left of her in the woods, a mess of flesh, blood and scattered memories that belonged to no one at all.

He was young; young enough to believe them, to not question the satisfied smirks and pitiful looks thrown his way.

For years, he dreamed of teeth. Claws. Blood staining the snow.

He imagined his mother running through the forest, terrified and alone.

He never questioned why wolves would come so close to the Conti estate or what his mother was doing in the forest.

He never questioned why there were no witnesses that could've helped her.

He never questioned why his father's wife had looked almost peaceful when she heard the news.

He was a child, and children tend to believe the stories adults give them.

Until they learn that adults lie.

Arian learned that lesson later. Too late, perhaps.

By then, his mother was already dead, his father's affection had become a dangerous secret, and his older brother had grown into the heir everyone believed Arian could never be.

His brother was *legitimate.*

Arian was **not.**

His brother was *loved.*

Arian was **tolerated.**

His brother was the *future* of the Conti family.

Arian was the reminder of his father's **betrayal.**

So Arian learned to survive.

He learned to keep his head down when necessary and his eyes open always.

He learned not to fight back when the other boys pushed him into the dirt.

He learned not to react when they called him bastard.

He learned to endure bruises without making a sound.

And eventually, he learned something far more important.

No one protects the weak.

His father might have loved him in his own ways.

But love had never saved his mother.

And it would not save him.

So Arian stopped waiting for someone to save him, and became the kind of person who did not need saving.

Then came the day his brother betrayed the family.

A betrayal against the Contis was not something that could be forgiven, no matter if it was the next heir or a complete stranger.

Everyone knew that, and respected that one merciless, unbreakable rule.

Except their father.

The Don of Conti family still saw his son.

His heir. His beloved firstborn.

So he hesitated. Only for a moment.

But in the Conti family, a moment of mercy could cost a life.

And his brother never hesitated when it came to collecting lives.

He killed their father.

Arian was there.

He remembered the sound more than anything else.

The gunshot piercing the high-ceiling room, the tremble of stained glass windows, the heavy thud of the Don's body hitting the marble floor, the last strained breath leaving his bloodstained lips.

And the silence afterward.

For the first time in his life, Arian understood something with absolute clarity. When his brother turned around and their eyes locked, crazed, bloodshot, Arian knew.

There would be no mercy.

His hand did not shake when he raised the gun.

Perhaps that was the moment the frightened boy finally disappeared, crushed under a wave of adrenaline and survival instinct.

His brother looked at him and Arian pulled the trigger.

One shot. One bullet. One second.

And the Conti family became his that night.

He was the youngest Godfather in its history.

They called it an achievement.

A victory.

A testament to his strength.

They praised the coldness of the man who had killed his own brother without blinking in the name of honour.

They did not know that he had spent the entire night afterward puking himself raw.

They did not know he had washed the blood on his hands until sunrise, until his fingertips bled, scraped to the bone.

In the Conti family, love was dangerous.

Mercy was fatal.

And weakness was a luxury no one survived.

So Arian buried it all.

He buried fear first.

Then tenderness.

Then trust.

By the time he was worthy enough to inherit the Conti name, there was nothing left worth breaking.

He learned that mercy was remembered only when it was withheld.

He learned that people respected monsters because monsters did not need anything from them and yet they could take everything.

So he became one.

Cold.

Untouchable.

The Conti name became something people lowered their voices around.

And Arian liked it that way.

He had spent years building walls so high that even those closest to him could not see over them.

He was safe behind those walls. Nothing reached him there. Nothing hurt him there. Nothing mattered enough to hurt him.

But—

One day, he brought home a *Prince.*

A beautiful creature with feisty eyes and mischievous smile, and hands that reached for what was not allowed.

*I'll conquer you*, he thought then, staring into those dark shiny eyes. *I'll tear you down to my world, and strip you of your light.*

But the Prince only smiled.

And somehow, that was enough.

Brick by brick, wall by wall, the Prince dismantled everything Arian had spent a lifetime building as if it was made out of paper.

And when Arian finally realized what had happened, there was nothing left of his fortress but ruins.

Nothing left of his carefully guarded heart but a single name.

Caleb.

The Prince he had intended to conquer.

The Prince who had conquered him instead.

And perhaps the cruelest part was that for some unknown reason Arian did not want his fortress back.

For the first time in his life, he had found a weakness he did not wish to destroy, he did not despise.

A weakness that gave him strength.

A weakness he would protect with his life.
