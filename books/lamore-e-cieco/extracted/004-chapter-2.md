The first time Arian Conti's heart skipped a beat was in a much more unusual setting than he would have ever expected.

It wasn't during a business meeting where millions of euros changed hands with nothing more than a signature, nor was it in the aftermath of some bloody dispute between rival families, when the air still carried the metallic scent of violence and every man in the room waited to see who would be the last one standing.

No.

It happened beneath a cloudless afternoon sky, while the royal family's flags moved lazily in the summer wind and a game of polo unfolded across a green field before him.

Arian sat beneath the shade of the pavilion with one knee resting over other, a crystal glass of champagne untouched between his fingers, while servants moved quietly around the guests with silver trays and the King of England sat beside him as though they were discussing nothing more consequential than the weather.

The scene was almost absurdly peaceful.

"So," the King said, turning slightly toward him, his expression carefully pleasant, "I'm glad we've reached an understanding regarding the recent events, Mr. Conti."

Arian glanced at him, the dark sunglasses hiding his eyes.

"Have we?"

The King's smile tightened almost imperceptibly, but Arian noticed.

The King was an older man, dignified in the way only men who had spent decades being obeyed could be, but Arian had met enough powerful men to know that authority and confidence were not always the same thing.

Today, the King looked confident. He also looked tense.

Arian understood why.

A member of the Conti family had been injured in Sicily, and unfortunately for the Crown, the man responsible was not some insignificant tourist or drunken aristocrat who could simply be quietly removed from the picture.

It was Prince Andrew.

The heir to the British throne. The future King.

The scandal had been contained, for the moment, but scandals had a habit of growing teeth when given enough time, and the British royal family had no intention of allowing one foolish incident in Sicily to become the story that followed Andrew for the rest of his reign.

So they had offered Arian compensation.

Not money. Money would have been too simple and useless for a Godfather of one of the most prestigious mafia families in the whole Italy.

They had offered him marriage instead.

One of the King's daughters, no less.

A connection between the Conti family and the British Crown that would bury the incident beneath enough ceremony, diplomacy and carefully chosen photographs to make it disappear.

And, from a purely political perspective, Arian had to admit that the arrangement was tempting.

Very tempting.

There was another marriage waiting for him in Sicily, one he had been refusing for longer than his advisers considered wise. The Caruso family had been pressing him toward an alliance for months, and their persistence had become increasingly difficult to ignore.

Valentina Caruso was beautiful, intelligent and perfectly acceptable by every traditional measure that mattered to the mafia families of Sicily.

She was also a Caruso. That alone was enough for Arian to dislike the idea.

The Contis and Carusos had a history of alliances, rivalries and marriages that stretched back generations, and everyone seemed to believe that Arian's position as Godfather came with a responsibility to continue the tradition.

He disagreed. That marriage would only tie his hands and put a collar on his neck.

The problem was that refusing the Carusos too openly could be interpreted as weakness and dishonour for the tradition.

And Arian Conti did not tolerate being seen as weak.

A royal marriage, however, would change everything.

The Crown could offer the Contis legitimacy, influence and a connection powerful enough to make the Carusos reconsider how aggressively they wished to pursue their alliance.

It was not merely a marriage. It was a double edged knife. A very dangerous, but profitable one.

"So," the King continued, drawing Arian's attention back to him, "I believe the arrangement we discussed yesterday is quite satisfactory."

Arian's lips curved faintly.

"That is yet to be seen, Your Majesty."

The King exhaled through his nose.

"You have been given considerable freedom in the matter of choice."

"I appreciate that."

"You will have the opportunity to meet my daughters and make your final pick."

Arian's gaze moved toward the field.

"I understand."

The King frowned.

"You don't seem particularly interested."

"I am interested. I’m simply taking my time with observation."

That was the truth.

Arian was interested in what the marriage could accomplish. He was simply not interested in pretending that he had come to Britain in search of romance.

Love had never been part of his calculations.

Love made men careless.Carelessness made men vulnerable.

And Arian had spent his entire life learning not to be vulnerable.

So he would choose carefully.

He would take the daughter who offered the greatest advantage to his family, make the alliance official, and return to Sicily with the Carusos finally forced to accept that their plans for him had changed.

She had to be simple. Efficient. Predictable.

Arian liked predictable.

Then a sudden burst of laughter from the polo field drew his attention.

His eyes moved toward the players.

The King's daughters were riding together, their horses moving gracefully across the grass, their uniforms immaculate despite the speed of the game. Each one of them was beautiful, radiant and full of that noble blood that seemed to make them glow.

But among them was a young man.

Arian's gaze settled on him, his focus suddenly sharpening.

The young man guided his horse forward with effortless confidence, leaning low as he swung the mallet, his dark hair falling across his forehead despite whatever effort had been made to keep him presentable. His back was beautifully straight, his strong thighs holding his body in the saddle with such grace that Arian felt as if the Prince were gliding across the grass.

He struck the ball, and it flew cleanly across the field, hitting its mark perfectly.

The Prince straightened in the saddle, grinning cheekily.

That smile.

Arian went still.

The world seemed to slow around him and everything became distant.

Because suddenly Arian was standing on a balcony again.

There was moonlight covering the Palace grounds, and the air was warm, smelling like roses and rain.

Then, a person appeared. Barefoot. Completely drunk. Laughing like an idiot when he bumped into a tree and then scolding a rose bush for not catching him.

Hair tousled, cheeks reddened, shoes lost somewhere along the way.

It was him.

The man from the garden.

The beautiful fool who had wandered barefoot through the palace grounds the previous night and then collapsed onto the grass right beneath Arian’s balcony, as though the entire world belonged to him.

And when their eyes met, the fool actually smiled at him, eyes hazy with alcohol. And then—he winked at Arian, completely lost in his intoxication.

Only now he wasn't a drunken fool.

He was a Prince.

Arian's fingers tightened around the stem of his glass, and his throat turned dry.

The young man laughed again as one of the princesses rode past him, and there was something almost boyish in the expression on his face, something completely at odds with the careful elegance of the royal family surrounding him.

He looked alive.

Unrestrained.

Dangerously unafraid.

Arian had seen men twice his size beg for their lives without shame, but this Prince had looked up at a stranger standing on a palace balcony while half-drunk and had smiled at him as though he had nothing in the world to fear.

Arian had thought him foolish.

Now he wasn’t so sure.

"That is Caleb, my youngest.”

The King's voice interrupted his thoughts.

Arian looked at him. The King was watching his expression with an unmistakable hint of amusement.

"Your younger son?"

"Yes."

"Caleb."

"Caleb Whitmore."

Arian looked back toward the field.

The Prince had just ridden past the pavilion, and for the briefest moment, his eyes swept over the guests.

Arian wondered whether he would recognize him, and his body tensed despite himself.

He didn't. Caleb's gaze passed over him without stopping, as if he was just yet another face.

Arian felt an inexplicable disappointment.

"He's quite different from Andrew," the King remarked, a hint of something akin to displeasure in his hoarse voice.

Arian’s brow rose.

"I noticed."

The King gave a quiet laugh.

"Most people do. Especially taking into consideration the talent for creating troubles my younger son is known for."

Arian watched Caleb lean toward his horse, whispering something against its ear before patting its neck.

"He's not the heir."

"No."

"Then he has more freedom to misbehave."

The King was silent for a moment.

Arian did not miss the slight change in his expression.

There it was. The weakness.

Andrew was the future King while Caleb was not.

The King might love both his sons, but the Crown had already decided which one mattered more.

Arian understood that better than anyone.

Blood was not always enough.

And sometimes the person standing second in line had far more freedom than the person standing first.

His eyes returned to Caleb.

The Prince was laughing now, riding alongside one of his sisters, his posture relaxed and his smile bright enough to be seen even from the pavilion.

Arian remembered the way those eyes had looked beneath the moonlight.

He remembered the wink.

And suddenly the political equation he had been considering since his arrival shifted.

A royal marriage would solve his Caruso problem.

A royal marriage would strengthen the Conti family.

A royal marriage would give the Crown exactly what it wanted.

But a traditional marriage could also bring him trouble.

Arian's gaze lingered.

Caleb had stopped his horse near the edge of the field, speaking animatedly with one of his sisters, his hair disheveled from the wind and his cheeks slightly flushed from the game.

There was nothing polished about him.

Nothing restrained.

Nothing safe.

Nothing predictable.

Arian should have looked away.

Instead, he found himself thinking about the previous night.

About that smile.

That laugh.

Those eyes.

He had come to Britain prepared to choose a princess.

He had expected to make a practical decision, one that would benefit his family and finally free him from the Caruso marriage alliance that had been tightening around his throat.

Instead, fate had placed something unexpected directly in front of him.

Arian watched Caleb smile again, and something deep within his chest moved in a way he did not appreciate.

He did not know the Prince. He did not know his habits, his temper, his ambitions or his weaknesses.

He knew nothing about him.

And yet, for reasons Arian could not explain, he already wanted to know everything.

The King followed Arian's gaze across the polo field.

"You've been watching my children for the better part of the afternoon."

Arian's mouth curved slightly.

"I have."

"And? Which one of my daughters do you intend to marry, Mr Conti? Princess Matilda, Eleonor or Cecily?"

Arian leaned back in his chair, his gaze returning to the field.

Everything had been simple about the deal. Pick a woman. Marry her. Deal with her later.

Until last night.

Until a drunken young man had wandered barefoot through the royal gardens, argued with roses, fallen into the grass and looked up at Arian as though the Godfather of the Conti family were nothing more frightening than another stranger beneath the moon.

And now that same man was standing before him in daylight, dressed as a Prince, holding a polo mallet and laughing with his family.

Caleb Whitmore.

Arian's eyes narrowed slightly.

Perhaps the British Crown had offered him exactly what he wanted without realizing it.

The King cleared his throat.

"If you haven’t made the final decision, I can arrange for you to spend some private time with each of the Princesses."

Arian looked at him. Then, very calmly, he set his champagne glass down.

"No."

The King frowned.

"No?"

Arian stood.

The movement immediately drew the attention of several people nearby, but he paid them no mind.

His gaze remained fixed on the polo field.

Arian's expression softened into the faintest smile.

It was not a kind smile.

It was the smile of a man who had just found an answer to a problem everyone else had failed to solve.

"I didn't come here for a Princess."

The words were quiet.

Almost conversational. Yet they seemed to silence everything around them.

Arian's eyes remained on Caleb.

Then he turned his head toward the King, the man’s old face reflected in Arian’s dark pupils.

**"I came for the Prince."**
