## Chapter 5: Trapped

Chapter Notes

See the end of the chapter for [notes](#endnotes5)

When Arian returned from the Conti family council, the estate was already plunged into the darkness of night. The long corridors were deserted of servants and guards, the marble floors reflected the dim golden light of lamps, and beyond the tall windows, a Sicilian darkness stretched over the entire grounds, engulfing the gardens, fountains, and distant hills beneath a sky so black that even the stars seemed reluctant to disturb it.

It was a long evening.

The council meetings had become longer in recent months, which Arian suspected not so much because of the number of matters demanding his attention, but rather because of the unfortunate tendency of older men to confuse repetition with wisdom. After three hours of territorial disputes, financial reports, complaints about junior advisors who seemed to have forgotten respect for their elders, and the increasingly irritating question of what Arian actually intended to do about the proposed Caruso family marriage alliance, he had reached the point where the next person to mention Valentina's name would test the limits of his patience. Fortunately for everyone involved, no one did.

At least not after Arian looked at them.

He entered the office and closed the door behind him, greeting the silence that fell with a relief he would never have admitted aloud. He went to the cabinet next to his desk, took out a bottle of whiskey, and poured himself a generous portion into a heavy crystal glass, the kind he favored when the evening was particularly unpleasant.

He didn't add ice.

Arian rarely felt the need to dilute anything.

Glass in one hand, he loosened his tie, walked over to the window seat, and sat down, letting his head rest briefly against the dark leather as his tired gaze rested on the faint reflection staring back at him in the glass.

For a few minutes, he did nothing.

He didn't drink.

He didn't think about the council, the Carusos, the reports waiting on his desk, or the people who would arrive tomorrow, expecting him to make decisions on matters they themselves couldn't resolve.

Instead, rather uncomfortably, he thought of Caleb Whitmore.

The prince was somewhere else in the estate, probably asleep, though Arian had discovered in a surprisingly short time that Caleb's behavior couldn't be predicted with any certainty, especially since the young man had spent the evening alternating between threatening to stab him and making dry remarks about kidnapping etiquette. 

Arian's lips twisted slightly against his will.

He remembered the smile Caleb had worn throughout dinner—bright enough to seem genuine, yet casual enough that anyone who didn't know him could have believed the Prince was simply amused by his situation. He also remembered the slight tremor in the hand holding the wine glass, so faint another man might have missed it, but Arian had spent too many years studying people for signs of deceit, fear, and betrayal to miss something just because the owner tried so hard to conceal it.

Caleb was afraid.

He simply decided that fear wouldn't satisfy him; stubbornness, however, would.

There was something familiar about it. Arian took his first sip of whiskey and let the warmth spread down his throat, though the thought was already starting to pull him somewhere else, somewhere he didn't intend to go, because Caleb's laughter, his nervous flirtation, and that almost irritating reluctance to show anyone what lay beneath his polished British humor had stirred something old within him. He closed his eyes. The memory returned, as always, not gently and never in its entirety, but in fragments that seemed to be stored separately in his mind because piecing them together would require touching something he still feared.

First, he remembered the scent.

Even now, after all these years, it was always the most important thing.

Honey.

Thick, sweet, and cloying, its sweetness so unbearable it made him nauseous. It matted his hair and forehead, caked his eyelashes, and pooled in the corners of his eyes until every attempt to open them blurred the world into golden streaks of light and shadow.

For years after that, Arian couldn't stand the sight of honey.

Without explanation, he ordered it removed from meals, left the room where the servants were preparing honey-infused desserts, and once broke a glass simply because the smell hit him unexpectedly and, for one humiliating moment, transported him back to that afternoon with such force that he could almost feel the dirt under his cheek again.

He was ten years old then. Maybe eleven.

His exact age became uncertain over time, but he remembered being young enough that the boys around him seemed much larger, though in reality they couldn't have been more than a few years older than him. They were children in every way that mattered, except that cruelty had already taken root within them.

They called him a bastard.

They always called him a bastard.

Sometimes they used his name first, almost affectionately, and then added another word, as if the two words belonged together, and sometimes they didn't bother with his name at all, because everyone present understood perfectly who they meant.

Arian. Bastard. Don's mistake.

Unwanted proof that a woman who had never been Conti was carrying the child of a man who already belonged to someone else. A witch. A whore. A stray.

It took him a long time, but over time he learned not to react.

It was one of the first lessons he learned.

If he cried, they laughed.

If he fought, they beat him harder.

If he told his father, they became more careful where they hurt him, and the next day, they found another opportunity.

So he learned to endure.

Unfortunately, children like them never forgive someone who refuses to give them the reaction they expected.

That afternoon, they knocked him to the ground before he understood what was happening, and he remembered the sudden slap of his cheek against the ground, the sharp taste of blood in his mouth as his teeth dug into his lip, and then the weight of bodies around him as fists and boots struck wherever they could.

His ribs hurt the most.

He remembered it with uncomfortably sharpness.

Each breath became shallow, because taking too deep breaths made his chest ache, yet he tried to keep silent, pursing his lips as the boys laughed and called him weak, for there was something especially cruel about accusing a child of weakness while simultaneously proving he couldn't defend himself. 

Finally, perhaps out of boredom, one of them found a jar.

Arian remembered hearing the sound of breaking glass somewhere above him before he realized what they were about to do, and then the honey poured down on his head in one sticky, choking wave, covering his hair, face, and clothes, while laughter erupted around him.

"What a pathetic duo. A mother torn apart by wolves, and her bastard son devoured by bears!"

They said maybe the animals would like him more now.

They said that at least the bastard would finally be useful.

There were only two of them—a rather unusual combination. Usually, they attacked him in groups, taking advantage of their numerical superiority.

But this time, there was someone else. Besides his brother, who had always led the group of children, a new face appeared. It was a boy Arian had never seen before. A stranger. With blond hair and eyes as blue as the ocean in winter. Cruel. Cold. He looked at Arian with so much disgust and hatred it made him breathless. 

Arian never learned which of them had tied his wrists behind his back, though he remembered the rope cutting into his skin as he struggled, and by the time he realized they were leaving him there, he was too bruised, too exhausted, and too disoriented by the honey burning his eyes to do anything but listen to their footsteps disappear into the undergrowth.

For a moment, he thought they had simply abandoned him. That they wanted to teach him a lesson, and that someone would find him soon.

That seemed cruel enough.

But then he heard a growl.

Even now, he couldn't say for sure how close the animal had been when he heard it, for his vision had become nearly useless under the influence of blood, honey, and tears he stubbornly refused to acknowledge, leaving him only with hearing and instinct as something large moved in the undergrowth. The forest changed as the birdsong faded away.

The branches stopped rustling as they usually did.

Even the air around him seemed to grow heavy, as if every living thing had realized that something dangerous had entered the space before Arian himself did.

He tugged on the rope until his wrists ached.

Nothing worked.

He tried to stand, only managed to shift to one knee and immediately lost his balance as his ribs protested violently enough to fall back into the mud.

Then he heard breathing.

Deep.

Heavy.

Too close.

He couldn't see the creature clearly, only a huge, blurry silhouette moving somewhere beyond the honey-colored light, but when it moved forward, Arian realized it was large enough to kill him easily.

For the first time that day, he stopped pretending he wasn't afraid.

Tears welled up in his eyes against his will.

They ran down his cheeks and mingled with the honey, and Arian hated himself for crying even when his body betrayed him, because somewhere in his ten-year-old mind he had already heard the lessons the Conti family had taught him, each one repeated with merciless precision.

Weak things were hurt.

Weak things were abandoned.

Weak things died.

No one came for the weak.

Arian believed it. He believed it with the absolute certainty only a child can possess, because his whole life had been proven to be true.

So when the massive body of the animal lunged at him with a roar, Arian no longer cared, and with all the grief and fear of a ten-year-old, he closed his eyes, screaming.

Then a shot echoed through the forest. 

The sound was so sudden and violent that Arian flinched, hard enough to deepen the pain in his ribs, while somewhere ahead of him an animal let out a furious, terrified roar and fell into the mood, so loudly that Arian heard the branches snap under its weight. And he could feel its coarse fur touching his ankles. It was close. So close. 

For several seconds, he could not move.

He was too afraid that the animal would attack again, and too confused to understand where the shot had come from.

Then he heard footsteps.

Not heavy footsteps of an adult.  Smaller ones. Careful but quick.

Arian tried to lift his head, blinking desperately through the honey, and at first all he saw was sunlight breaking through the trees behind a blurred figure.

The person approaching him was a boy.

That much he knew.

He could not have been much older than Arian himself, perhaps the same age, perhaps younger, although memory had made that detail uncertain, and in his hands he carried a rifle that seemed almost absurdly large for someone so small.

Arian never remembered his face.

That was the cruelty of it.

Of everything he had carried away from that day, the face had been lost first.

He remembered only the shape of him standing against the light, the brightness of the afternoon behind his shoulders, and then the moment he came closer and Arian saw, through the golden blur obscuring his eyes, what he believed had been a smile.

He was beautiful.

Arian knew that much, although he could never have explained why.

Perhaps it was not beauty at all that he remembered, but gentleness, because the boy approached him without hesitation and crouched before him as though there was nothing strange about finding another child tied up and bleeding in the middle of the woods.

Arian instinctively tried to move away—all he knew was pain the hands could cause. 

But the boy reached for him anyway. And his hand was soft.

That was the detail Arian remembered most clearly.

Soft and warm against his own, so different from the fists that had struck him and the rough hands that had dragged him through the dirt, and when those fingers carefully brushed some of the honey away from his eyelashes, Arian found himself becoming completely still.

The boy leaned closer.

Arian could not see his features.

He could not remember the colour of his hair, the shape of his mouth, or even the sound of his footsteps after all these years.

But he remembered his voice.

Soft. Certain in a way that no ten-year-old boy should have been.

“Don’t worry,” he whispered. Arian had only heard that accent once before, when he’d accidentally overheard a conversation between his father and his guest a few days ago. “I’ve got you.”

Arian had not known what to do with those words.

Nobody had ever said them to him like that before.

Not with such certainty. Not as a truth.

Something inside him had simply given way.

Perhaps it had been the last of his resistance, or perhaps it had been the first moment in his life when he understood what it might mean for another person to look at him and decide that he was worth saving.

The boy began cutting through the rope around his wrists, and Arian remembered the sting as circulation returned to his hands, as well as the strange embarrassment he felt when the stranger knelt in the mud next to him, his white trousers completely ruined, without commenting on any of it. 

Arian had tried to speak.

He wanted to ask who he was. He wanted to know why he had come.

Most of all, he wanted to see him clearly.

He reached forward instead, his fingers finding the sleeve of the boy's clothes and holding on.

For one brief moment, the stranger allowed him to.

Then the forest erupted with voices. Their footsteps came quickly through the trees, followed by shouting that Arian could not make out until one voice rose clearly above the others.

"Young Master!"

The boy turned.

Arian's fingers tightened around his sleeve.

The men reached them within seconds, their voices urgent and frightened, and whatever happened next remained forever fractured inside Arian's memory because there were too many bodies, too many hands, too much movement, while the boy was suddenly being pulled away from him with an urgency that made no sense.

Arian tried to hold on.

He could not.

He was still tied to his own weakness, bruised and exhausted, and when the sleeve slipped through his fingers, the boy disappeared into the confusion of shouting men and moving shadows.

"Wait," Arian had tried to say.

The word came out too quietly.

He remembered hating that. He remembered reaching after him and grabbing only air.

And then he had felt something cold against his palm.

Arian opened his eyes in the present and stared at the item in his hand.

The silver dagger.

The boy must have dropped it when the men took him away, although Arian had never seen the moment clearly enough to know for certain, and by the time the woods became quiet again, the weapon had been lying beside him in the dirt.

He had picked it up.

Even as a child, his hands had closed around the handle as though he understood that this was the only thing left behind that proved the encounter had truly happened.

The metal had been cold. And yet it was the only thing that gave him warmth. Arian had held it against his chest until someone eventually found him.

He had kept it ever since.

For years, he searched for the boy.

Not openly, because Arian Conti had learned very quickly that searching for something precious gave other people the opportunity to use it against you, but quietly, through questions, rumours and men sent after pieces of information that never seemed large enough to lead anywhere.

A child with a rifle.

A silver dagger.

Bodyguards.

A voice calling him *Young Master*.

A boy who had saved a bastard child in the woods and then disappeared before Arian could even learn his name.

Nothing.

There had never been enough.

Eventually, the search became something private, almost shameful in its persistence, because Arian told himself that he was looking for the boy only because he owed him a debt, and the Contis paid their debts.

But the truth was less convenient. Vulnerable and dangerous. 

For one brief afternoon, when Arian had been a frightened child covered in blood and honey and waiting for something terrible to happen, someone had come for him.

Someone had looked at him and seen neither the bastard nor the weakness nor the mistake.

Someone had simply seen a boy who needed help.

Arian had spent the rest of his life trying to become strong enough that he would never need such help again.

He had built himself into something untouchable.

He had killed his brother, inherited his father's enemies, taken control of the Conti family and become a man whose name caused others to lower their voices.

Yet, sometimes, when the estate was quiet and there was no one nearby to witness it, he could still remember a warm hand against his cheek and a voice whispering words he had never forgotten.

*Don't worry.*

*I've got you.*

Arian raised the whiskey to his lips and finally drank.

Somewhere else in the estate, Caleb Whitmore was sleeping, or pretending to sleep, perhaps already plotting his escape and almost certainly deciding whether threatening his host with a knife had been sufficient for one day.

The thought drew the faintest smile from Arian.

Then he looked toward the darkness beyond his window.

For reasons he could not explain, the image of the Prince beneath the moonlight appeared beside the memory of a boy standing in the forest, both of them blurred at the edges by time and distance, both of them smiling at him in ways Arian had never entirely understood.

He dismissed the thought almost immediately.

It was impossible.

Caleb Whitmore was a British Prince.

The boy from the woods had been someone else.

And yet, as he sat alone in the darkness with the taste of whiskey lingering on his tongue, Arian found himself thinking of something that irritated him precisely because he could not explain it.

He had spent years searching for a boy he could not remember.

And now, without knowing why, he could not seem to stop looking at Caleb Whitmore. 

The sound that interrupted Arian's thoughts was so quiet that, under ordinary circumstances, it might have disappeared beneath the silence of the estate entirely, but Arian had never survived by treating unfamiliar sounds as harmless, and the moment the brief notification reached him from the other side of the room, his eyes opened.

For several seconds, he remained motionless in his armchair, his fingers still curled around the glass of whiskey while the remnants of an old memory slowly withdrew into the darker corners of his mind, replaced by the instinctive alertness that years of violence, betrayal and leadership had taught him never to abandon, particularly when he was responsible for a house containing one unwilling British Prince who had already threatened him with a knife during dinner.

The notification sounded again.

Movement detected.

Arian's gaze moved toward his desk.

He had ordered additional cameras installed throughout certain private areas of the estate only weeks earlier, and although none of them had been placed in locations where they would violate the privacy of his guests, he had made absolutely no attempt to hide the fact that the Conti estate was protected, watched and secured by more than guards at its gates.

Arian rose from his chair, carried his whiskey with him, and crossed toward the desk before opening his laptop.

The screen illuminated his face.

For a moment, Arian simply stared.

Then one of his eyebrows lifted.

Prince Caleb Whitmore was wandering around one of the private rooms.

Not wandering discreetly.

Not attempting to move silently or conceal his intentions.

He was very obviously looking for something.

Arian lowered himself into his chair again.

Caleb had his hands behind his back as he walked slowly through the room, his expression arranged into one of almost exaggerated innocence, although there was absolutely nothing innocent about the way his dark eyes moved across every corner with careful, calculating attention.

Arian watched him approach the bookshelf.

The Prince stopped. Looked at it. Then glanced slowly around the room, as though ensuring nobody could possibly object to what he was about to do.

Arian already knew what was coming.

Caleb pulled out the first book.

He looked behind it.

Nothing.

Then he pulled out the second.

Nothing.

The third.

Nothing.

Arian took a slow drink of whiskey while the Prince continued this process with a level of determination that might have been admirable under literally any other circumstances.

"Are you planning to inspect every book?" Arian murmured, despite knowing perfectly well that Caleb could not hear him.

Apparently, yes.

The Prince moved down the shelf.

One book.

Then another.

Then another.

By the time Caleb reached the end, Arian had finished nearly half his whiskey and had developed a reluctant respect for the sheer stubbornness involved.

Caleb stared at the bookshelf, offended. The bookshelf, unsurprisingly, offered no consolation.

Arian's mouth twitched.

Then Caleb moved on.

The flowers were apparently suspicious.

Arian watched as the Prince approached the large arrangement beside the window, bent over it and carefully parted the stems with his fingers, inspecting the leaves, the vase and the spaces between the flowers with such open distrust that Arian briefly wondered whether Caleb experienced finding something in the flowers that didn’t belonged to them before. 

After—obviously—finding nothing, the Prince glanced around the room. He moved toward the sofa.

Every pillow was inspected thoroughly. When that produced nothing, he pressed his hands into the cushions.

Arian stared.

The Prince frowned.

He checked underneath the sofa.

Arian took another drink.

Caleb straightened and looked around.

His gaze travelled slowly over the room. Then it landed on the curtains.

The heavy curtains beside the balcony were made of thick fabric and fell from the ceiling to the floor, providing enough shadows and folds that Caleb apparently decided they had immediately become the most suspicious objects in the entire room. Right after the flowers, of course. 

Arian watched as the Prince approached them.

Caleb took hold of one curtain, and shook it vigorously. 

Nothing happened.

Then he grabbed the other side and repeated the action, looking so focused that for a moment Arian was rooting for him to actually get a camera to fall out of the curtains.

"Good God."

The words left Arian's mouth quietly, shamelessly hidden by the walls.

Caleb stopped and placed both hands on his hips.

For several seconds, he stood in the middle of the room and looked around with frustration that seemed the most genuine emotion Arian saw on his face since the beginning of it all. 

He found himself smiling at the sight. He shouldn't have.

Caleb Whitmore was an inconvenience.

A political complication.

A hostage to an agreement that Arian had every intention of transforming into a marriage, whether the Prince currently approved of the arrangement or not.

He was not supposed to be entertaining.

And yet there Arian sat, the youngest Godfather in Conti history, a man whose name could silence a room before he entered it, watching a British Prince investigate a collection of pillows with the seriousness of a counterintelligence officer.

The thought might have amused him more if Caleb had not suddenly turned toward the painting.

Arian's smile froze.

The painting was large, old and deliberately unremarkable, occupying the wall opposite the windows in a position that had made it ideal for the small camera hidden within one of the carved details of its frame, although Arian had been reasonably confident that nobody would notice it without already knowing where to look.

Caleb walked toward it.

Arian sat forward, interested. 

The Prince studied the painting with his head slightly tilted, appearing at first to examine the artwork itself, before his eyes moved to the frame and remained there.

Arian's fingers tightened slightly around his glass.

Caleb stepped closer.

His hand rose.

Arian watched the Prince's fingertips move across the carved wood, slow and thoughtful, until they stopped.

For one moment, nothing happened.

Then Caleb leaned closer.

Arian could almost see the exact instant when he found it.

The Prince's lips parted slightly.

His expression changed.

And then Caleb looked directly into the camera.

Arian stared at the screen. Caleb stared back.

For several seconds, neither of them moved.

Of course, the Prince could not actually see him. Arian knew that.

And yet something about those dark eyes looking directly into the lens created the absurd impression that Caleb knew exactly where he was, exactly what Arian was doing, and exactly how much he had enjoyed watching him search through the entire room.

A slow smile began to appear on Caleb's face.

Arian leaned back in his chair and lifted his whiskey to his lips.

"Clever boy," he murmured.

The Prince's smile widened, as if hearing the complement. 

But there was nothing warm about the smile.

It was the smile of a man who had just discovered an advantage and had immediately begun considering all the ways he could weaponize it.

Caleb stepped closer to the painting.

His pretty face filled more of the screen.

"Right," he said, speaking directly to the camera in that perfectly polished British accent, his voice carrying the faintest trace of amusement. "So that's where you've been hiding. Subtle."

Arian's eyebrow rose.

Caleb glanced around the room theatrically.

"I'll admit, I was beginning to think you'd spent all that money on security simply to make the house look intimidating, which would have been rather embarrassing for you, wouldn't it?"

Arian's expression remained perfectly calm.

Caleb smiled.

"You know, I did check the flowers."

Arian stared.

The Prince pointed vaguely toward them.

"Very suspicious lot. I suggest changing the florist."

Arian took a drink.

Caleb leaned closer to the camera.

"But I suppose you've been having a lovely evening, haven't you, Godfather?”

Arian's eyes narrowed.

The Prince's smile turned positively unbearable.

"Watching me wander about your house, opening your books, interrogating your cushions, shaking your curtains about like an absolute idiot."

Arian's mouth curved despite himself.

Caleb saw the camera, not the smile, but he seemed to know.

"Yes, yes, enjoy yourself," he continued, waving one dismissive hand toward the lens. "You've got the technological advantage at the moment. Congratulations."

He paused.

Then his eyes brightened.

"Oh."

Arian immediately disliked that expression. He knew that expression.

Caleb Whitmore had just had an idea. A very bad one, most likely. 

The Prince continued looking directly into the camera, but the smile on his face became sweeter.

Arian had learned, within less than twenty-four hours of knowing him, that Caleb's sweetest smiles were almost certainly followed by trouble. He sighed, helplessly. 

"Actually," Caleb said thoughtfully, "since you're so terribly interested in what I'm doing..."

Arian lowered his glass, anxious for what was about to happen next.

Caleb reached for the collar of his shirt. Then, with every bit of deliberate mischief that had made the British press call him a scandal since he was old enough to appear in public, Caleb tilted his head slightly and looked directly into the hidden camera.

"Are you ready for the show, my future husband?"

Arian froze.

Caleb's smile widened.

"Do try not to blink, darling."

The Prince reached for the first button of his shirt. And undid it.

Then, before Arian could decide whether the correct response was anger, disbelief or the immediate removal of every camera from the estate, the Prince glanced directly into the lens again, his eyes glittering with amusement.

"Goodness," Caleb murmured. "You are quiet tonight."

Arian's fingers tightened around his whiskey.

He had spent most of his adult life surrounded by men who would rather walk into a burning building than deliberately provoke him.

Caleb Whitmore, apparently, had been sent to Sicily to restore the balance.

His first instinct had been to close the laptop.

His second had been to stand up, walk directly to Caleb's room and inform him, in no uncertain terms, that there were limits to what could reasonably be considered acceptable behaviour.

Neither instinct lasted particularly long.

Instead, Arian leaned back in his chair.

He lifted his glass of whiskey.

And he waited.

If Caleb intended to bluff, Arian wanted to see how far the Prince was willing to take it.

The shirt slipped from his shoulders.

Arian stared at the screen.

For one brief moment, he was simply surprised.

Not because there was anything particularly scandalous about the sight of a shirtless man, but because the Prince's appearance was not quite what the newspapers had led him to expect.

The photographs usually showed Caleb dressed for formal events, wrapped in tailored suits or ceremonial uniforms, and whenever he appeared in less formal settings there were always enough jackets, open collars and strategically positioned cameras to preserve the illusion of a prince who belonged more to headlines than to ordinary reality.

Here, however, there was no performance for photographers.

At least, not the usual kind.

Caleb was lean rather than heavily built, his body shaped by sport and an active life rather than the deliberate pursuit of strength, with defined shoulders and arms that suggested years of polo, fencing and whatever other activities the Crown had considered appropriate for a prince who possessed far too much energy to sit still for long.

Arian found himself, rather objectively, impressed.

He had never been particularly interested in men, and Caleb being beautiful did not somehow alter the fact that Arian's attention had traditionally belonged elsewhere, but he had spent too much of his life observing people to deny the obvious simply because it was inconvenient.

Caleb Whitmore was a very handsome man.

Arian already knew that.

Apparently, he was also well-built.

Noted.

Arian took another drink.

The Prince glanced toward the camera.

His smile had begun to look suspiciously strained around the edges.

Interesting.

Perhaps Caleb had expected Arian to react.

Perhaps he was bluffing after all.

Arian's mouth curved.

“Come on, Your Highness. Don’t give up.”

Caleb, once again unable to hear him, placed his fingers at his belt and undid it.

Arian sat forward slightly, brows raised. 

Caleb looked into the camera.

"Still watching, are you?"

Arian stared.

The Prince's fingers moved to the fastening of his trousers.

Arian's expression became increasingly difficult to maintain.

He had expected Caleb to remove his shirt. That was a simple provocation. Annoying, childish and entirely within the Prince's character.

But the trousers—

Arian watched Caleb undo them with slow fingers, as if giving Arian time to react.

Arian did not.

So the Prince pushed his trousers lower.

Arian stared at the screen.

This had ceased being amusing.

It had not become anything else, precisely, because Arian's concern was considerably stronger than any other reaction he might have been expected to have, but he was beginning to suspect that Caleb's confidence was not entirely the product of an elaborate joke.

The Prince was actually going to do this.

Arian narrowed his eyes.

“Try me,” he hissed, but the fury slowly building in his chest dulled the amusement.

The Prince stepped out of his trousers.

Arian's jaw tightened.

There were limits.

Caleb Whitmore, however, appeared not to have discovered them.

He stood before the camera in nothing but his boxers, his discarded clothes scattered carelessly across the floor while he glanced toward the lens with an expression of such exaggerated innocence that Arian immediately knew the brat was enjoying every second of this.

"My goodness," Caleb spoke then, tilting his head innocently, as if he wasn’t standing almost naked in another man’s room. 

The Prince placed one hand against his chest, and started to slowly descend lower, caressing the beautifully shaped muscles on his stomach. Then he laughed.

"You're more twisted than I thought, Godfather.”

And then he reached for the waistband of his boxers. 

Arian slammed the glass on his desk with force.

"That lunatic."

Arian was already standing.

The chair struck the floor behind him.

He didn't care.

And as Caleb's fingers began to pull—

Arian Conti walked out of his study.

Then he walked faster.

By the time he reached the corridor, he was no longer walking at all. He was running.

Not because he was attracted to the Prince.

Not because the sight of Caleb half-undressed had somehow disturbed his self-control.

Absolutely not.

He was running because Caleb Whitmore was, without question, completely insane, and Arian was beginning to understand that if he intended to marry the British Prince, he might have to personally prevent him from doing something ridiculous before the young man managed to get himself into trouble.

So when he burst into the room he was watching through the lens just seconds ago, all he could think of was how to teach that royal troublemaker a lesson he will never forget. 

He was about to scold him, but then the half-naked British Prince opened his smiling mouth, and made Arian lose his mind:

“What? Can’t take what you asked for?” 

And Arian snapped.
