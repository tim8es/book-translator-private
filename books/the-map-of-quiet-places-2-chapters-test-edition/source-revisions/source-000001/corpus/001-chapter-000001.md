# Chapter 1. The Key Beneath the Clock

By the time the evening train reached Northbridge, the rain had turned the station windows into sheets of silver. Mara kept her suitcase close and studied the handwritten address on the back of an old ticket. The ink had faded, but one sentence remained clear: “Come before the clocks stop.”

She had not seen her grandfather for nine years. His letters had always been brief, full of weather reports and strange questions. Did she still draw maps? Did she remember the road to the lighthouse? And, once, in a letter without a return address: What would you save if you could carry only one memory?

Outside the station, every clock showed a different time. The tower above the market said six fifteen. The pharmacy clock said almost eight. A clock in the café window had no hands at all. Mara checked her phone. Its screen read 19:42, then flickered and went dark.

“You’re late,” said a voice behind her. A boy in a yellow raincoat stood beneath the awning. He held an umbrella big enough for two people and a paper bag that smelled of warm bread.

“Do I know you?” Mara asked.

“Not yet. My name is Theo. Your grandfather asked me to meet you. He said you would have a blue suitcase and a difficult expression.” Theo glanced at her luggage. “He was right about one of those.”

They crossed the square without speaking. Rain rattled against the umbrella. The buildings leaned toward the narrow streets as if sharing secrets. At the end of the lane stood a small repair shop, its front window crowded with watches, keys, and brass instruments whose purposes Mara could not guess.

A sign above the door read THE QUIET HOUR. Underneath, in smaller letters, someone had written: Repairs made while you wait. Certain waits may be longer than others.

Theo pushed open the door. A bell rang once, but the sound seemed to continue long after it should have faded. Inside, the shop smelled of cedar wood, machine oil, and orange peel. Hundreds of clocks ticked along the walls, none quite in step with the others.

On the counter lay an envelope addressed to Mara in her grandfather’s uneven handwriting. Beneath the envelope was a small iron key. It was colder than the room, and when she touched it, every clock stopped.

Theo did not look surprised. “That,” he said, “is why we asked you to come.”
