# Chapter 2. A Map That Would Not Stay Still

The silence was so complete that Mara could hear the rain sliding down the outside of the glass. She lifted her hand from the key. Immediately, a clock near the door began to tick. A second clock answered it, and then a third, until the shop filled with the familiar noise of machinery pretending that nothing extraordinary had happened.

“Where is my grandfather?” she asked.

Theo placed the paper bag on the counter. “He went to the archive yesterday. He left instructions. First, you read the letter. Second, you must not open the green cabinet. Third—” He hesitated. “Third, if someone asks you what time it is, you say you don’t know.”

Mara broke the envelope’s seal. Inside was a folded map and a note written on a single sheet of cream-colored paper: Dear Mara, I owe you an explanation, and I have run out of ordinary ways to give one. The city has begun to lose its hours. I believed I could repair them myself. I was wrong. Follow the map, trust what you remember, and do not mistake a familiar voice for a friend.

She unfolded the map across the counter. At first it looked like a conventional plan of Northbridge. Streets were marked in black, the river in pale blue, and the railway line in red. Then the lines began to move. A bridge slid toward the east bank. The market square narrowed. A lane that had not been there a moment before appeared beside the library.

“Paper doesn’t do that,” Mara said.

“Most paper doesn’t,” Theo replied. “That one records the roads people have forgotten. Look carefully. The route changes when you remember something.”

Mara closed her eyes. She pictured the summer when she was ten: bicycles on the promenade, salt in the air, and her grandfather pointing toward a distant lighthouse. He had told her that a good map showed where you could go, but a great one helped you find what you had lost.

When she opened her eyes, the lighthouse appeared at the edge of the paper. A thin white road connected it to a black square labeled ARCHIVE. The road crossed the river at a place where the city had never built a bridge.

Somewhere behind them, a door handle turned. Theo stepped between Mara and the green cabinet. He did not speak, but his face had gone pale.

A polite knock sounded from inside the cabinet: once, twice, three times.

“Excuse me,” said a voice that sounded exactly like her grandfather’s. “Could you tell me the time?”
